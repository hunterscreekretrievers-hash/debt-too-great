# 6. Honolulu

*San Antonio to Honolulu, Territory of Hawaii, June to December 1941*

Katharina sewed forty dollars into the lining of Ruth's coat the night before the train, folded flat in a square of oilcloth, and told her not to take the coat off until she reached Hawaii, though it was the end of June in Texas and a hundred degrees on the depot platform in San Antonio.

"Margaret has lost two," her mother said in German, biting off the thread. "Walter writes that she sits all day in a chair by the window. He is on the ship four days of seven. She needs a woman in the house who knows how to work, and you know how to work."

"Yes, Mama."

"You do not go to dances."

"No, Mama."

"You go to church every Sunday. You do not let a sailor lay a hand on you." Katharina held the coat up to the bulb and looked for gaps in the stitching. "I know what you are, Ruth. You are the one who climbs the pole. You are going to an island full of poles."

Fritz carried her suitcase onto the train and stood on the platform with his hat in his hands while the cars began to roll. He did not wave. Ruth watched him through the window until the platform curved out of sight, a square man in a black hat standing very still, the way he stood at a grave.

Somewhere in that city, on a street of frame houses she had never seen, Sam Mayfield was lying under a Ford with a wrench in his hand, or so she pictured him. Ruth knew the street number by heart from the backs of eleven envelopes. A girl on a train could not find one house in a whole city by looking out a window, and she knew it, and she looked anyway, every block of the way out of town.

---

> *Thursday, July 10, 1941*
>
> *Aboard the S.S. Lurline, fourth day. Sick over the rail three times. The man from Ohio in the next deck chair says sea legs come by Hawaii. Mine have not come yet. The ocean is a color I have no word for in English or German, darker than the Pedernales in a flood and clean as a well.*
>
> *Mama's forty dollars are still in my coat. I am wearing the coat. Everyone stares.*
>
> *Seventeen years old, and never farther than Austin. Tonight the lounge had a dance with an orchestra. I stood outside the door and listened. Listening is not the same as going.*

---

The ship came in to Honolulu on a Saturday morning with the band playing on the pier and brown boys diving off the Aloha Tower wharf for the coins the passengers threw. The air smelled of flowers and bunker oil and the pineapple cannery down the harbor. Green mountains stood behind the town with clouds snagged on their tops like wool on a barbed-wire fence.

Walter met her at the foot of the gangway in his whites. He was thirty-one and had gone heavy in the shoulders, and the sun had burned his face the color of brick. He lifted her off the ground the way he had when she was six.

"Lord, Ruthie. Take off that coat. You'll die."

"Mama said not until Hawaii."

"You're standing in Hawaii." He hung a lei of plumeria around her neck, the flowers cool and wet against her skin. "Margaret wanted to come." He looked down at her, and his brick-red face went soft as a boy's. "She's expecting again, Ruthie. Found out Tuesday. The doctor wants her flat on her back, and for once in her life she's minding a man."

---

The cottage in Kaimuki sat up the slope from Waikiki on a street of small frame houses with tin roofs and hibiscus hedges, and a mango tree dropped fruit on the roof all night with a sound like a man knocking at the door. Margaret Vellmer lay on a daybed on the screened porch with a pillow under her knees. She was a schoolteacher from Brenham, thin and pale, with bitten fingernails, and she cried when Ruth walked in, and then apologized for crying, and then cried again.

"I'm so glad you're here," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm so glad."

"Don't be sorry," Ruth said. "Where's your mop?"

For two weeks Ruth cleaned. She scrubbed that cottage the way her mother had scrubbed the kitchen under the first bulb, on her knees, until her knuckles split. Mrs. Tanaka from two houses down, a small square woman who wore her husband's straw hat in the garden, came over the second morning with a pan of rice and a jar of pickled plums and stayed to show Ruth how to scale a fish without throwing scales on the ceiling. On the nights Walter had duty aboard the *Oklahoma*, Ruth and Margaret sat on the porch in the dark and listened to the mangoes hit the tin.

By the third week the cottage was clean, Margaret was eating, and Ruth had run out of things to scrub.

The *Honolulu Advertiser* ran a notice that the Navy hospital at Pearl Harbor was taking women to help on the wards. **[VERIFY: whether the Naval Hospital Pearl Harbor took civilian women as nurse's aides or ward helpers in 1941, paid or volunteer, and any minimum age]** Ruth read it twice at the kitchen table and folded the paper so Margaret would not see it.

---

The Naval Hospital sat at the mouth of the harbor on a flat point of land, white buildings with screened porches and wide lawns running down toward the channel, so close to the water that the bosun's pipes on the ships going out carried across the grass. **[VERIFY: layout of Naval Hospital Pearl Harbor in 1941, its buildings and lawns, and its position on Hospital Point]**

The woman who interviewed her was a Navy nurse named Miss Draper, fifty years old, with iron-grey hair under a starched cap and the hands of a farm wife. She sat behind a desk with a form and a fountain pen.

"Name."

"Ruth Marie Vellmer."

"Age."

Ruth had decided on the bus out to the hospital. Seventeen was a girl. Eighteen was a woman, and a woman could be asked to hold a basin while a doctor cut into a man.

"Eighteen," she said. "January of 1923."

Miss Draper looked at her over the pen a long time. Ruth looked back with her grey eyes and did not blink, and kept her hands folded in her lap so the nurse could not see them sweat.

"What use are you to me?"

"I can milk, cook for twelve, butcher a hog, set a broken leg on a calf and drive a truck. I can sew. I can read German and English. I've helped my mother lay out the dead."

Miss Draper wrote *1923* on the form. "Your hands, please."

Ruth held out both hands. The nurse turned them over, looked at the calluses across the palms, the short nails, the long fingers, and set them down on the desk like something she had bought at an auction.

"Six-thirty tomorrow. You'll empty bedpans for a month. If you're still here in a month, I'll teach you something useful. If you faint, you're finished."

"I won't faint."

"Everybody says so," Miss Draper said.

---

> *Saturday, August 16, 1941*
>
> *Three weeks on the ward. **[VERIFY: ward designations at Naval Hospital Pearl Harbor, 1941]** More bedpans than there are people in Martinsburg, and no fainting. A boy off a destroyer came in with his hand crushed in a winch, three fingers gone, and Miss Draper had me hold the basin while Dr. Lowell took off what was left. I held the basin. Afterward I was sick behind the laundry, and Miss Draper found me there and said, "Behind the laundry is allowed."*
>
> *The lie comes easier every day. Lord, forgive me. I am not sorry enough to stop.*
>
> *Margaret is three months along and has kept the baby. At night when Walter is home she lets herself hope. Through the wall Walter talks to her belly in German.*

---

Lena's envelopes came to the cottage every two or three weeks, addressed in her round schoolroom hand, and inside each one, folded behind a page of family news, lay a second letter in pencil on tablet paper.

Ruth read the first one sitting on the seawall at the end of the hospital lawn on her dinner break, with her shoes off and the harbor slapping at the stones below her feet. That letter came up out of the trunk tonight in a bundle under a rusted wire, and every word in it is Sam's.

> *San Antonio, Texas*
> *August 2, 1941*
>
> *Dear Ruth,*
>
> *Lena sends word you are in Honolulu. So you got out of the valley first. No surprise. You always did beat me to the top of the windmill.*
>
> *Mr. Booker Odom has me at his garage on East Commerce six days a week for nine dollars. I can pull a Ford engine and set it back in by dinner. Mr. Odom says I have good hands for a boy who talks so much. Two dollars every Saturday goes in a Prince Albert can under the bed for flying lessons. The can holds thirty-one dollars.*
>
> *In June I rode the streetcar out to Stinson Field with five dollars in my shoe and asked a man in a leather jacket the price of an hour in the air. He said for me the hour did not cost anything, because for me there was no hour. He said it nice, Ruth. He said it like a man telling you the price of rain. The field carries the name of two white girls, the Stinson sisters, who were flying airplanes out there before the end of the last war.* **[VERIFY: whether flight schools at Stinson Field, San Antonio, refused Black students in 1941]**
>
> *The Navy man downtown says I can sign tomorrow. The Navy takes colored men for the mess, he says. I asked him who flies the airplanes off the carriers and he laughed. A boy does not come all the way from the Pedernales to pour another man's coffee.*
>
> *The colored paper says the Army opened a flying school for colored men at Tuskegee in Alabama this summer. They want two years of college. I have the eighth grade and Mr. Odom's wrenches. Mama says the Lord makes a way where there is no way. Mama has never stood in an Army office.* **[VERIFY: Tuskegee's first aviation-cadet class, July 1941, and the 1941 two-year college requirement for Army aviation cadets]**
>
> *The Randolph planes go over the East Side every morning at seven, blue and yellow, the same as on the river. Every morning I stand in the yard with my coffee and count them. Isaiah 40:31. Find it in your Bible. You owe me a book anyway.*
>
> *Write me the color of the ocean. Write it so a man can see it.*
>
> *Sam*

She looked up Isaiah 40:31 that night in the King James Bible Katharina had packed in her suitcase between two folded slips. *But they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint.*

Ruth read the verse three times by the lamp. Then she sat down at Margaret's kitchen table and wrote Sam four pages about the ocean: the color of it off the reef at noon, green as a bottle held up to a window; the white line where the swells broke a mile offshore; the flying fish that skipped off the bow of the *Lurline* like flat stones thrown by a boy; the smell of it, salt and fuel oil and flowers all at once. On the last page she wrote that she had not fainted yet, either.

"Who writes you in pencil?" Margaret asked the next morning from the daybed, with her eyes closed and a damp cloth across her forehead.

"A boy from the river."

Margaret smiled without opening her eyes and asked nothing more, and Ruth loved her for it the rest of her life.

---

The dance hall stood near Waikiki, a long open-sided building with a tin roof, a band on a platform and paper lanterns strung from the rafters. Ruth had promised her mother she would not go to dances. In October she went anyway, because Margaret, five months along and bored past bearing, begged her to go and come home and tell her everything.

A velvet rope on two brass posts hung across the main door, with a Hawaiian bouncer the size of a hay wagon standing beside it. White servicemen stepped over the rope or around it. Ruth stood just inside the door with two girls from the ward and saw two colored sailors come up the steps in their whites, saw the bouncer shake his head once, and saw the sailors turn and go back down the steps into the street without a word, as if they had expected nothing else. **[VERIFY: whether Honolulu and Waikiki dance halls turned away Black servicemen in 1941]**

The taller of the two had a long solemn face. For one second, under the lanterns, he was Sam exactly, and Ruth's heart went over like a calf on a rope.

The band struck up a waltz Lena used to play at Luckenbach. A boy from the *Nevada* with a fresh haircut asked Ruth to dance.

"No, thank you," she said. "I came to look."

She stood one song by the rope, watching the dark street where the two sailors had gone. Then she walked out past the bouncer and down the steps and rode the bus home alone to Kaimuki, and told Margaret everything, the lanterns and the band and the boy from the *Nevada*, everything but the rope.

---

> *Saturday, October 18, 1941*
>
> *Margaret wanted every detail, so she got every last one. She laughed until she had to lie flat on the daybed.*
>
> *Nothing to Margaret about the rope. What would a person say about the rope? Papa says Gott sieht alles. If God sees everything, He saw those two boys go down the steps, and He saw the band play on, and He saw me stand there and say nothing.*
>
> *Sam would have been the third one on those steps. If he had signed with the Navy man, he would be here now, pouring coffee on a battleship, and some Saturday night he would walk up those steps in his whites and the bouncer would shake his head.*

---

The next envelope from Lena came in the middle of November. The letter inside began with a line printed in capitals across the top of the page, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly torn the paper.

> *San Antonio, Texas*
> *November 9, 1941*
>
> *RUTH. I HAVE BEEN IN THE AIR.*
>
> *A colored man named Mr. Cornelius Pettis flew a yellow Waco biplane into a hay pasture out past Converse for a church homecoming, a dollar a ride, and I paid him out of the Prince Albert can, and he let me ride in the front seat. He was a mechanic in France in the last war and learned to fly from the Frenchmen, and he has the stub of a cigar he never lights. Mr. Pettis knew a colored man from Georgia over there named Bullard, who flew fighters for the French because his own country would not have him.*
>
> *Ruth, the ground let go. A man has no better words for it. One second the wheels were bumping over the cow patties and the next second the bumping quit, and the whole pasture fell away under us like a quilt pulled off a bed, and there was the creek and the pecans and the church and the tables of fried chicken and all the people looking up with their hands over their eyes. Mama was down there. Eli was down there jumping.*
>
> *Over the creek Mr. Pettis tapped me on the head and pointed at the stick and held up ten fingers. I held the stick for a count of ten. The airplane did what I told it. Ruth, it did what I told it.*
>
> *Nobody can corner you up there. I said so on the rock. I was right.*
>
> *Mr. Pettis says I have the hands. Mama cried. Eli wants a dollar.*
>
> *The can holds fifty-six dollars now. Fifty-seven, before Mr. Pettis.*
>
> *Sam*

Ruth carried that letter in her apron pocket on the ward for three weeks, until the folds wore white and the pencil began to blur along the creases, and on the bad nights, when a sailor off a tin can died at three in the morning of something nobody could fix, she put her hand in the pocket and held the paper in her fist.

---

> *Saturday, December 6, 1941*
>
> *Walter home for supper. Pot roast, the first one made in this house that Margaret said was as good as Mama's, which is a lie, and I love her for it. Walter has the duty and must be aboard tonight. He put his hand on Margaret's belly and the baby kicked him, and he said she will be a Navy girl, and Margaret said a boy, and they argued, and it was the best night ever spent under this roof.*
>
> *He kissed me on the head at the door and said, Gute Nacht, Ruthie, and went down the hill to catch the bus to the landing. Something made me watch him all the way to the corner.*
>
> *Sam's letter is under my pillow.*
>
> *On at seven tomorrow. The clock is set for five.*
