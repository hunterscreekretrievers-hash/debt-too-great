# 7. December 7

*Naval Hospital, Pearl Harbor, Sunday, December 7, 1941*

Ruth was folding sheets on the back porch of the ward when the first plane went over, low enough that the roar came up through the boards under her shoes.

She looked up because everybody looked up at planes. The Army and Navy flew every day of the week, and on Sunday mornings the boys from the carriers sometimes buzzed the harbor for fun. This plane came in over the water from the west with the sun on its wings, dark green on top, pale underneath, a red ball as big as a washtub painted on its side, and it went over the hospital so low that she saw the pilot's head turn in the glass.

Then a sound rolled across the water from Ford Island, a deep thud she felt in her teeth, and a column of black smoke stood up off the island like a tree.

Another thud came, and another. The sheets slid off Ruth's arm onto the porch floor. She ran down the steps and across the lawn toward the water.

---

The lawn ran down to the edge of the channel, and from the seawall Ruth could see across the harbor to the long grey ships moored along Ford Island in pairs, the way Walter had drawn them for her in pencil on the back of a letter. **[VERIFY: sightlines from Hospital Point across to Battleship Row and Ford Island, and the distance]** He had marked his ship with an X. *Outboard,* he had written beside it. *We're always the one on the outside, Ruthie, the one that gets the weather.*

The planes came in two and three at a time, low over the water, so low their propellers threw spray, and each one dropped something long and dark that hit the water and ran toward the ships leaving a white wake. Ruth did not know the word torpedo yet in any way that mattered. She watched the white lines run. She watched them reach the outboard ships, and the water stood up beside the hulls in columns taller than the masts, white and then brown and then black, and fell back into the harbor.

The ship Walter had marked with an X began to lean.

The great ship leaned the way a live oak goes when the axe has bitten through, slowly, with plenty of time to hope it might not fall. Men stood on the deck. Ruth could see them, small and white, running uphill as the deck tipped, sliding, catching at rails and lines. Some went over the high side and down the hull. Some went into the water. The ship rolled farther, and the masts came down toward the harbor, and the men on the hull were running now on the side of the ship, and then on the bottom of it, the red bottom paint coming up out of the green water, until the whole battleship lay over on her back with her screws in the air and men standing on her bottom like boys on a turned-over johnboat in a stock tank.

Someone was screaming beside her. Ruth turned and found her own mouth open.

"Vellmer!"

Miss Draper stood on the porch of the ward with her cap gone and her grey hair down one side. "Vellmer! In here! Now!"

A plane came over the hospital at rooftop height, and Ruth threw herself flat on the grass. Its guns were firing. A row of dirt jumped up across the lawn twenty feet away, one, two, three, four, like a man walking fast in invisible boots, and the plane was gone over the trees. When Ruth stood, her knees were green and her hands were shaking. She ran for the porch.

---

The first of the wounded came in before nine, in trucks, in cars, in the backs of pickups, on doors, on stretchers, on men's backs.

The men came out of the water, and the water was on fire. The battleships were bleeding fuel oil into the harbor, and the oil burned on the surface in sheets, and the men who went over the side had swum up through it or dived under it and come up into it. Those men came in black. Ruth could not tell at first which ones were Negro and which were white, because every one of them was black, coated head to foot in oil like boys dipped in a vat, and under the oil, on the ones who had come up through fire, the skin had cooked.

She would remember the smell before anything else. Fuel oil, and under the fuel oil, burned hair, and under the burned hair, a smell like hog-killing day on the Vellmer place when the scalding barrel ran too hot and the hide came off with the bristles. Her body knew that smell before her mind could name it. Her stomach turned over, and she swallowed the bile back down and kept walking.

The ward filled, then the porch, then the lawn. Men lay on the grass in rows, on blankets and sheets and bare ground. Doctors walked the rows. Miss Draper walked behind the doctors with a box of morphine and a syringe, and when she had given a man his shot she wrote an M on his forehead with her own lipstick, so that nobody would give him another. **[VERIFY: how nurses marked men who had received morphine at Pearl Harbor (lipstick, iodine, grease pencil) and whether morphine was given by syringe or by Syrette in Dec 1941]**

"Vellmer. Take scissors. Cut them out of the clothes. Don't pull anything that sticks. Cut around it."

Ruth took the scissors.

She cut a sailor's dungarees off his legs, and the skin came with the cloth in long grey strips, the way old wallpaper peels off a steamed wall. She stopped. The sailor looked up at her with white eyes in a black face and said, "Go on, ma'am. It don't hurt. It don't hurt at all." The burns that went deepest did not hurt; she learned that before ten o'clock. The nerves were gone. A man who screamed would likely live. A man who lay quiet and polite with his skin hanging off him would be dead by dark.

She cut a boy's shirt off and found his back, from the neck to the belt, the color of a chicken left too long on the fire. She cut a gunner's mate's shoes off, and his toes came with them. The scissors went slick with oil, and she wiped them on her apron, and when the apron was black she took it off, and somebody handed her another.

Doctors painted the burns with tannic acid jelly out of big brown jars, and corpsmen went down the rows spraying the solution out of Flit guns, the same pump sprayers Katharina Vellmer worked against the flies in her kitchen. When the jelly ran out, the corpsmen mixed more from powder in basins, and the burned men lay on the lawn glazed brown and shining, as if somebody had varnished them. **[VERIFY: tannic acid treatment of burns at Pearl Harbor, Dec 7, 1941, and whether sulfa powder was also used]**

Near nine another wave of planes crossed the harbor. Ruth kept her eyes on her scissors. The guns and the bombs and the anti-aircraft fire from the ships still afloat made a sound like the whole sky being torn in half across a man's knee, and then came a shriek and a crash close by, and a wall of heat went across the lawn. A Japanese plane had come down on the hospital grounds. **[VERIFY: a Japanese plane crashed on the Naval Hospital grounds, which building it struck, and when]** Smoke rose over the buildings. Ruth kept cutting.

Through the gap between two buildings she saw a battleship come down the channel with her guns firing, trying for the open sea, so close to the hospital that the concussion knocked the breath out of Ruth's chest. Bombs threw white water up all around the battleship, and the ship turned out of the channel and ran herself aground on the point, just below the hospital, smoking like a brush pile. A corpsman on the seawall shouted the ship's name, *Nevada*, and Ruth thought of the boy with the fresh haircut who had asked her to dance in October. **[VERIFY: USS *Nevada* beached at Hospital Point; position relative to the hospital buildings]**

---

Ruth prayed.

She knelt by every man the doctors had passed over, the ones with no M on their foreheads because there was no point, and she held their hands if they had hands left, and she prayed in German because the German came without her choosing it. *Vater unser, der du bist im Himmel.* She prayed the Lord's Prayer and the Twenty-third Psalm, the words Pastor Thalmann said at St. Paul when he buried the old people, *und ob ich schon wanderte im finstern Tal*, and sometimes the men understood her, and mostly the German meant nothing to them.

A boy from Iowa asked her to tell his mother he was sorry about the car. Ruth told him she would tell her. Then the boy quit looking at Ruth and looked past her shoulder at the curtain over the door, the way a boy looks up when somebody he loves comes into the kitchen. Whatever the boy saw put the pain out of his face. For the space of one breath his cot was lit the way a field is lit at noon, though no lamp hung over that cot. He died with her hand on his chest before she could ask his name.

A fireman from the *Arizona* with no hair and no ears asked her if she was an angel. She told him no, she was from Texas. He laughed, a little wet laugh, and died.

A coxswain off a launch, with both legs crushed below the knee, asked her to pray for him, and she did, out loud, with her eyes shut and her forehead almost on his, and when she opened her eyes he was looking at the sky over her shoulder with nothing in his face at all, and she had not felt him go.

She prayed over thirty men that morning, or forty. She did not count. She asked God for every one of them, plainly, the way her mother had taught her to ask for rain. Every one of them died.

The planes were gone. The sky went blue again, and smoke went up from the harbor in columns, black and straight, and the sun shone on the lawn and on the men lying on it, and God said nothing at all.

---

Around two in the afternoon the trucks brought men from the *West Virginia*.

Ruth knew the name only because a corpsman shouted it. She was washing oil off a sailor's face with a rag and a bucket of water gone grey, and the sailor was a Negro boy in what had been mess whites, with burns across both hands and up both forearms where he had held them in front of his face, and a burn down one side of his neck where the oil had flared. His eyes were swollen shut. She cleaned around them with the corner of the rag.

"You talk like Texas," he said, through lips cracked like a dry tank bottom.

"Pedernales County."

"Marshall. Clarence Banks. Mess attendant, third class." He moved one hand an inch toward her and stopped. "Ma'am, don't write my mama. You hear me? Don't let nobody write my mama till I can write her myself."

"Nobody will write her. Hold still."

She dressed his burns herself. She did not wait for a doctor. She had watched them all morning, and she knew they would come to him last, because his burns would likely heal and there were men on the lawn whose burns would not, and because of the other reason, the one nobody on that lawn needed to say out loud. She cut the sleeves off his jumper with the slick scissors. She washed the oil off his arms with soap until the soap was gone. She painted the burns brown with the jelly from the basin while he gripped the blanket with his good fingers and made no sound. She laid wet gauze over his eyes.

"Can you see anything?"

"Dark."

"The gauze is on them."

"Then it's dark because of the gauze," Clarence Banks said. "Ain't it."

"Yes," Ruth said, and did not know whether it was true.

He lay still a while. Then he started talking, in a voice so low she had to put her ear to his mouth.

"Dorie," he said. "Dorie Miller. Big man. From Waco. Heavyweight champion of the whole ship."

"Hush now."

"No, ma'am. You listen. You write this down someplace." His burned hand found her apron and held tight. "Dorie was collecting laundry when they hit us. Then they hit us again and again, torpedoes, God knows how many, and she laid over to port and everything burned. An officer told Dorie come help carry the captain. Captain had his belly torn open by a piece of something. Dorie carried him to where it was safer, and the captain wouldn't go below, kept giving orders, laying on the deck."

His chest went up and down under the blanket.

"Then Dorie got on a gun. Machine gun, up top. Nobody ever showed him how. Navy don't let us touch the guns, ma'am. We're for the coffee. Dorie got on that gun anyway and fired it at them till it quit. I seen it. I was on the ladder below him, and I seen him firing up at the sky, and I seen them coming in at us low, and then the fire came across the water and I went over the side."

"Is he alive?"

"No telling. Don't know who's alive." The voice cracked down the middle. "Lord God. Don't know who's alive."

**[VERIFY: details of Doris Miller's actions on the *West Virginia*: the laundry, carrying Captain Bennion, the machine-gun position, what a messman on a ladder could plausibly have seen]**

Ruth held his wrist and looked at his hands, burned to the color of a saddle where he had thrown them up in front of his face, and she thought of another pair of hands, long-fingered and black with Ford grease, holding a pencil over a sheet of tablet paper on the East Side of San Antonio. *The Navy takes colored men for the mess.* Sam had said no to the man downtown. If Sam had said yes in August, he would be lying on this lawn now, or in that harbor, and some white girl from somewhere else would be painting his arms brown with a brush.

She finished the dressing. She tucked the blanket under Clarence Banks's chin the way her mother tucked in a sick calf, and went on to the next man in the row.

---

The hospital went under blackout at dark. Men from the Navy Yard hung blankets over the windows, and the corpsmen wrapped their flashlights in blue cellophane, and there were men in every bed and between the beds and along the halls. Ruth had been on her feet sixteen hours. She had not eaten. She had not let herself think of Walter since noon, because when she thought of Walter her hands stopped working, and her hands could not stop working.

At eleven Miss Draper found her on the back porch, sitting on the step where the sheets had fallen that morning, with an empty basin in her lap.

The nurse sat down beside her, knees cracking, and put a tin cup of coffee in Ruth's hands. The coffee was black and lukewarm and tasted of the pot.

"Drink that."

Ruth drank it.

"Your brother's ship," Miss Draper said. "The *Oklahoma*."

"Yes, ma'am."

"There are men alive inside her. The yard men can hear them banging on the hull. They brought cutting torches over from the Navy Yard this afternoon, and they're cutting holes in her bottom by flashlight." Miss Draper looked out at the black harbor, where fires still burned low along Ford Island like the coals of a campfire nobody had banked. "No names. Nobody has a single name tonight. Don't you go down to that landing and ask, because they won't let you through, and you'll only be one more girl crying in the road."

"My sister-in-law is alone in Kaimuki. She's expecting. Nobody can get up the hill in the blackout."

"Then nobody can get up the hill." Miss Draper took the cup back and set it on the step. "Four hours on a cot in the linen room, Vellmer. Then I need you on the ward again. That's an order, and I'm too tired to give it twice."

Miss Draper went back inside the ward. Ruth sat a minute longer on the step. Behind her, through the blankets, a man was calling for his mother in a voice like a child's, and across the water, if she held her breath, she thought she could hear a hammer ringing on steel, far off and faint, the way her father's hammer rang on stone in the quarry on a still morning.
