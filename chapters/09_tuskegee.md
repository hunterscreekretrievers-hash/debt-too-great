# 9. Tuskegee

*San Antonio, Texas, and Tuskegee, Alabama, January 1942 to December 1943*

Sam Mayfield turned eighteen on Sunday, the eleventh of January, 1942, flat on his back under a laundry truck that had to roll by Monday.

He lay on a creeper across the oil-black floor of Booker Odom's garage on East Commerce, over on the East Side of San Antonio, with a drop light hooked to the frame a foot above his face, while the radio on the workbench gave the news out of Bataan. When he rolled out at nine o'clock, his mother stood in the open bay door in her church coat with a cake under a dish towel. She had walked nine blocks in a raw January wind.

"Eighteen," Hattie Mayfield said. "Wash your hands."

They ate the cake standing by the drop light, one slice each. Then he told her.

"The Army's taking colored boys for pilots now. At Tuskegee, in Alabama. The age went down to eighteen this week, and they don't ask for college anymore, Mama. They give a test."

"Can you pass it?"

"I've got the eighth grade."

"I didn't ask about the eighth grade. I asked can you."

For a year and a half he had studied under that drop light after Mr. Odom locked the shop for the night, with an algebra book a teacher at the colored high school lent him on the promise of a free brake job, against the day somebody let him take a test. He learned what a cosine was the way he had learned what a carburetor was, by taking it apart until it quit lying to him.

At seven o'clock the next morning he stood at the recruiting office at Fort Sam Houston, in a room with a flag in the corner and a radiator that knocked like a bill collector. A sergeant at the desk pointed his pencil, without a word, toward a door marked QUARTERMASTER.

"Cadet examination, Sergeant," Sam said.

The sergeant laughed once, through his nose. A lieutenant at the back of the room set down his coffee.

"Let him sit it," the lieutenant said. "Washington says we have no choice."

Sam sat it alone at a card table against the back wall, behind forty white boys at proper desks. He passed. The lieutenant read his score twice and looked at him the way a man looks at a mule that has learned to count, and swore him into the Air Corps Enlisted Reserve to be called to Tuskegee when Tuskegee had a place for him.

Tuskegee had a place for him eleven months later. **[VERIFY: typical wait for qualified Black cadet applicants in 1942, when one training field limited the classes]**

---

Sam spent the eleven months under other people's trucks. At the end of January the *Pittsburgh Courier* printed a letter from a colored man who worked the cafeteria at the Cessna airplane plant in Wichita, the one job Cessna would give him, under the question *Should I Sacrifice to Live 'Half-American'?* In February the *Courier* began printing two big V's on its front page, one for victory abroad and one for victory at home, and Sam cut the Double V out with his pocketknife and tacked it over his workbench beside a P-40 torn from *Life*.

He left on the second day of January, 1943, from the Southern Pacific depot, in his one suit.

Joseph Mayfield shook his son's hand on the platform. Joseph had run another man's herd for twenty-two years and lost the job in two weeks over one neighbor's anger and a boy sitting on a river rock beside a girl, and he had never once said so to Sam, and he did not say so now. He held the hand through two blasts of the engine whistle. Eli, fifteen, punched his brother in the arm and turned his face to the depot wall. Hattie handed Sam a shoe box of fried chicken and biscuits, tied with kitchen string.

"Write every Sunday," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"I mean every one."

The colored coach rode right behind the baggage car, where the coal smoke came through the window cracks and settled on a man's collar like pepper. A wooden partition split the car, colored passengers on one side and mail sacks on the other, with a toilet at the end that had not flushed since Hoover. Through the door at the back Sam could see the white coaches swaying behind, full of soldiers in new wool asleep on each other's shoulders, and past them a dining car with white cloths on the tables, where he could not buy a meal.

At Lake Charles a white conductor read Sam's orders twice, holding them to the light as if the ink might change.

"Aviation cadet," the conductor said. "Huh."

At Mobile a grey-headed Pullman porter came forward with a paper sack of ham sandwiches from the dining car kitchen, set it on the seat beside Sam without a word and went back the way he had come.

**[VERIFY: segregation signs posted at Tuskegee Army Air Field under its first commander, Col. Frederick von Kimble, and his replacement by Col. Noel Parrish in the winter of 1942–43]**

By March the commander, a white Kentuckian named Parrish who had taken command the week after Christmas, had the signs taken down, and for a month Sam walked through the door that had said WHITE as though it might close on his heel.

---

Primary was at Moton Field, the Institute's own grass strip a few miles off, where many of the civilian instructors were Black men, and no one in Sam's life had ever told him a Black man could hold that job.

Sam's instructor was Amos Jessup, a short, thick man from Gary, Indiana, with a matchstick in the corner of his mouth and a voice that came through the speaking tube like gravel down a chute. The first time Sam saw the row of Stearman PT-17 biplanes on the grass, blue in the body and yellow on the wings, he stopped so short that Deacon walked into his back. The same blue and yellow had crawled across the sky over the Harwell pasture when he was eleven. **[VERIFY: blue-fuselage, yellow-wing paint on Army Stearman trainers]**

"You going to fly it or marry it, Mayfield?" Jessup said.

Sam flew it with Jessup in the front seat for eight hours and nine minutes, while Jessup told him through the tube that he flew like a man carrying a pie to a church supper, all elbows. On the twelfth of April, after a landing that kissed the grass and stayed there, Jessup climbed down onto the wing root and stood in the prop wash with his helmet in his hand.

"Take her around the field," Jessup said. "Once. Don't break my airplane."

Sam taxied to the end of the field with the empty front seat bobbing before him. He pushed the throttle forward. The engine came up to a roar that went through his teeth, the tail lifted, the grass streamed under the wheels, faster, and then the grass let go of him.

Nobody had a hand on the stick but Sam Mayfield. The pines dropped away beneath the lower wing, and the red roads, and a mule in a cotton patch turned its long face up to watch him pass, and he was nineteen years old and alone in the air for the first time in his life, and he laughed out loud where only God could hear it.

Up there nobody could catch him. No sign told him which door was his. No conductor held his orders to the light.

He brought the Stearman down as soft as a quilt laid over a sleeping child. Jessup took the matchstick out of his mouth and studied it.

"Well," Jessup said. "You didn't spill the pie."

---

Basic was back at the Army field in the BT-13, a low-wing trainer that shook so hard at speed the cadets called it the Vultee Vibrator, and its instructors were white Army officers.

Sam drew Lieutenant Dale Harmon of Valdosta, Georgia, a lean man with sun cracks at his eyes who called every cadet "mister" and never raised his voice. In the third week of basic Harmon said the only thing he would ever say to Sam about color.

"Mister Mayfield, there's men up the chain who built this field so they could close it," Harmon said, on the wing, with the engine ticking as it cooled. "They're waiting on you people to fail. The War College put it on paper back in '25, that a colored man cannot control himself in the face of danger to the extent the white man can, and some of those men can say it by heart. I'm not one of them. I'm also not going to lie to them for you. Fly it right."

Sam had heard "you people" all his life. He had never heard it said as a fact about the weather, followed by a promise.

"Yes, sir," he said. "That's the plan."

Deacon Hawes washed out of basic on the second of June, on a check ride with a captain nobody knew, for a landing Sam had watched from the flight line and would have called good enough. Deacon packed his footlocker while the rest of the barracks found reasons to be somewhere else, laid his Bible on top of his folded khakis and sat on the lid.

"They say Hondo, Texas, come fall," Deacon said. "Navigator school, maybe. Something with a pencil. Half of us, Sam. Half the boys who come here go home without the wings."

"You'll fly yet."

"Don't say it." Deacon picked up the footlocker by its rope handles. "Get the wings. Get them for me and for every boy they put on the train home, and don't you dare go humble on me when they pin them on you."

---

In July, on a Saturday pass, Sam and a cadet from Chicago named Lucius Garrow walked into the town of Tuskegee in pressed khakis.

A white policeman stood at the corner by the drugstore with his thumbs hooked in a gun belt and a face as red as the clay, and he watched them come for half a block. When they were ten feet off, he stepped into the middle of the walk and planted his feet.

"Y'all boys lost?"

"No, sir," Sam said.

"Then get off my sidewalk."

Lucius did not move. Lucius had grown up on the South Side of Chicago, where a man sat anywhere he liked on the streetcar, and he had a mouth to match. Sam saw his jaw set, and saw the policeman's hand slide off the belt toward the holster, slow, the way a man reaches for a favorite tool.

Sam took Lucius by the elbow and stepped them both off the curb into the red dirt of the street.

They walked the length of the block in the gutter while the policeman watched, and two white women came out of the drugstore and stepped around the place where the cadets had stood as though something had spilled there. Sam's face did not move. Behind his eyes he was thirteen again with his back to the sheet iron, holding still the way his daddy had taught him to hold still for men like this one.

"You let him," Lucius said at the end of the block.

"I let him. You want wings, or you want to be right on a sidewalk in Macon County?"

Lucius did not speak to him the rest of the afternoon. On the bus back to the base, though, he said, "Both. I want both," and Sam said, "Me too," and the friendship that began on that bus was the kind a man carries into a war.

---

Advanced was the AT-6 Texan, with a gun that fired real bullets at a cloth sleeve towed behind another airplane, every cadet's rounds dipped in his own color of paint so the instructors could tell one man's holes from the next man's. Sam put sixty-one green holes in the sleeve on the eighth of September, and Lieutenant Harmon looked at it laid out on the grass and said, "Huh," exactly the way the conductor at Lake Charles had said it, and Sam took both as compliments.

Class 43-I-SE got its wings on the first of October, 1943.

Hattie did not come. The fare from San Antonio was more than three weeks of other people's washing. Lucius Garrow's mother came down from Chicago in a hat with a feather in it, and when she had pinned the silver wings on her own son's chest she turned and looked at the tall boy from Texas standing alone in the ranks with his wings in the palm of his hand.

"Give them here, baby," Mrs. Garrow said.

She pinned them on Sam's left breast above the pocket and pressed them flat with her thumb, and stepped back and looked at him the way a woman looks at a cake that came out right.

"Your mama would want somebody to do it."

That afternoon they made him a second lieutenant and handed him new identification tags, two on a beaded chain, with a serial number that began with the letter O. **[VERIFY: new tags and an O-series serial issued on commissioning, 1943]** He read them that night under the barracks bulb.

Mama, I am not angry, and I want you to know it. I am something that has no name yet. When I find the name I will write it to you.*
>

>
> *Your son,*
> *Sam*

---

The P-40 Warhawk had no second seat, so a man learned it from the book and then took it up alone. Once, in November, at twelve thousand feet over the red country, with the engine pulling him into the blue like a hand under his chin, Sam caught himself singing "Ein feste Burg" in the German Ruth had taught him on the river, and laughed, and could not have told anybody why.

His orders came the week before Christmas: Selfridge Field, Michigan, to a new squadron that trained replacement pilots for the 332nd Fighter Group, which was packing for the war without him. **[VERIFY: 553rd Fighter Squadron, activated at Selfridge 1 November 1943, training replacements for the 332nd; the group's departure from Selfridge 22 December 1943]**

The transportation sergeant asked which way he wanted to go.

"Through Washington," Sam said.
