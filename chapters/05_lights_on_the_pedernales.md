# 5. Lights on the Pedernales

*Pedernales County, Texas, 1938 to 1940*

The courthouse square in Johnson City smelled of mule sweat and hot tar the summer morning in 1938 when Henry Vellmer signed his name for the light.

Ruth rode the twenty-odd miles in the bed of the Ford with her braid whipping her face, because her father had said she could come if she kept her mouth shut, and she had agreed to the terms before he finished stating them. Her nose had healed with a bump in the bridge that her mother still touched with a thumb some mornings, the way a woman checks a stove she suspects is hot.

Farmers packed the square in overalls washed thin as cheesecloth. The co-op had been chartered in Austin in May, and now it was signing members out of Johnson City. A hand-lettered sign nailed to a post read PEDERNALES ELECTRIC COOPERATIVE. **[VERIFY: where PEC signed members and hired linemen in Johnson City, summer 1938]**

Henry was twenty-six, and he wrote his name in the ledger in the hand Katharina had drilled into him, every loop closed.

A tall man came through the crowd behind him. He stood a head above the farmers and moved through them the way a cutting horse moves through cattle, touching a shoulder here, bending to a woman there. His ears stuck out like jug handles, and each hand was the size of a dinner plate.

"That's the congressman," somebody said. "That's Lyndon Johnson."

Johnson looked at the ledger, then at Henry, and put out his hand. Ruth watched her brother's hand disappear inside it.

"You a lineman, son?"

"I've climbed my daddy's barn in a norther, sir."

Johnson laughed, slapped Henry's arm and looked past him to Fritz, who stood with his hat in both hands. "Your daddy?"

"Friedrich Vellmer," Fritz said, in English, carefully. He spoke English the way a man walks on ice.

"Well, Mr. Vellmer, your boy's going to bring the light to your house." Another hand reached for Johnson's, and he was gone into the crowd, bending, pulling, laughing.

On the drive home Fritz said one thing, in German, over the engine: "That man wants something."

By the fire Marcus had the phone up again, typing a name with both thumbs, J-O-H-N-S-O-N, while Ben slept against Lily's knee. The old man in the chair let the boy hunt. The next spring belonged to the axe crews, and one of the axes was in a Mayfield's hands.

---

The poles came up the valley in the spring of 1939, creosoted pine forty feet long, dragged by mules where the trucks could not go. Two feet below the grass lay limestone, and the crews broke it with long iron bars, and every stroke rang up the valley like a farrier's hammer.

Clearing the right-of-way was work for men with axes, and some of those men were colored. Ruth carried Henry his dinner in a flour sack one noon in April and saw Sam Mayfield on the cedar crew, fifteen years old, stripped to the waist, swinging an axe at a trunk as thick as his leg, the sweat running down his back in the cedar dust. The white crew ate under one live oak and the colored crew under another, forty yards apart, and the forty yards stood as plain as a fence.

Ruth crossed the forty yards with half of Henry's cornbread.

Sam took the cornbread without a glance toward the white crew. "Your daddy know you're over here?"

"My daddy's at the house."

"Somebody'll tell him."

"Then he'll know where to find me." She sat on a cedar stump. "Is your house getting the light?"

Sam chewed and looked down the valley, where the new poles marched in a line toward the Harwell place, the ranch whose cattle his family had worked for two generations. The line ran straight past the Mayfield cabin, close enough to hit with a thrown rock, and never dropped a wire to it.

"Mr. Harwell says the cabin don't need wiring," Sam said. "Says we been fine with kerosene this long."

Ruth had nothing to say worth his time, so she said nothing, and he finished the cornbread, and the foreman's whistle blew.

At the end of that week Ruth talked Henry into strapping his climbing hooks to her boots, and from the crossarm forty feet up she saw the whole valley at once: the Vellmer house small and white, the thin smoke from the Mayfield chimney, the bell tower of St. Paul square against the sky. Fritz stood at the foot of the pole with his arms crossed until she came down, and he unbuckled the belt from her himself and said nothing at all, which was worse than any whipping.

---

The line fence two miles up the river had a coffee can wired to the corner post, a Folgers can with a lid, and the can was the post office of the Pedernales.

Ruth put Walter's old copy of Lindbergh's *We* in it that May, and the book came back with a balsa propeller laid across the cover and every page about the airplane's engine marked in pencil, small careful ticks in the margin like a man counting fence posts. She sent him *Wind, Sand and Stars* the next summer, and he kept that one six weeks and sent it back with a single line printed inside the cover: *This man flies at night by the stars. I could do that.* Sam sent her Paul Laurence Dunbar, a poetry book swollen from damp with *Hattie Mayfield* written on the flyleaf, and Ruth read "Sympathy" sitting in the crotch of a pecan tree, read its last line twice, *I know why the caged bird sings*, and did not know for a long time why the poem made her angry.

Ruth and Sam met at the fence a Saturday a month, when the cattle needed checking on both sides of it. Sam brought airplanes. By sixteen he was building them with wingspans as long as his arm, tissue skins doped tight as a drumhead, and he launched them off the bluff over the river while Ruth stood in the Vellmer pasture to mark where each one landed in the grass. The river kept the secret, and the cedar kept it, and the cattle.

---

Anna married Clint Mabry in November 1939, a month after the co-op threw the switch on its first lines. The wedding was a rodeo dance on the Sabinal River below Utopia, under live oaks so old their lower limbs rested on the ground like elbows on a table.

Clint rode saddle broncs at the county fairs and was neither German nor Lutheran. Fritz had said so once, at supper, and Katharina had said, "Fritz," and the subject had been nailed shut.

Lanterns hung in the oaks over a floor of planks laid on sawhorses. Lena played fiddle with the band, a Mexican guitar player and an old man on an accordion, and when she drew the bow for the wedding waltz the whole crowd went quiet.

Late in the evening Anna found Ruth on the arena fence and climbed up beside her in her wedding dress.

"Papa told me I could come home," Anna said, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "If Clint ever raises a hand to me, Papa will come for him with a hammer." She took Ruth's hand. "He sits on everything, Ruthie. He sat on the war. He sat on what those four men did to him on our porch in 1918. Don't you sit on anything. If you ever want out of that valley, you go."

Then Clint took her back to the floor, and Ruth watched her sister turn under the lanterns, the white dress throwing the light, while up the river, in canyons nobody could see in the dark, the maples Clint had promised Anna for a wedding present were going red. Almost nowhere else in Texas grew a maple.

---

The line reached the Vellmer place in March of 1940, and Henry wired the house himself.

He ran the cloth-covered wire along the beams on white porcelain knobs and drilled through the limestone with a star drill, and Fritz stood in every room watching the steel go into his walls the way another man would watch a surgeon cut into his wife. One bulb went in the kitchen, on a cord with a pull string. At sundown Henry walked in with his hands black and said, "Mama. Go ahead and pull it."

Katharina wiped her hands on her apron, reached up and pulled the string.

The light came on white and hard and showed everything.

Katharina had lived in that kitchen thirty-two years by coal-oil lamps, the yellow light that leaves the corners soft. Under the bulb she looked at the corners: the soot over the stove, the spider webs along the beams, the dirt ground into the floorboards by six children and a husband who came in from the pens. She took the scrub bucket from under the sink.

At midnight she was still on her knees, and Ruth was scrubbing beside her, and Fritz sat at the table under the bulb running his thumb over the stone of the windowsill, where his own chisel marks from 1908 stood out sharp as the day he cut them.

From the kitchen window Ruth could see the Mayfield cabin two miles up the river, one yellow point, a kerosene lamp turned low behind the glass, beside a wire that ran past it toward somebody else's house.

---

In July of 1940 the river dropped to green pools between shelves of rock, and the sun went down every evening red through the dust of other men's hay fields.

On a Thursday at dusk the two sixteen-year-olds sat on a limestone ledge above the pool below the line fence, a foot of warm rock between them, with her bare feet in the water and his boots set side by side on the ledge as neat as a deacon's Sunday shoes under a pew. A balsa airplane with yellow wings lay across his knees. Cicadas sawed in the pecans. A heron stood in the shallows on one leg.

"Colored paper says they're teaching colored boys to fly at a college in Alabama," Sam said. "Tuskegee. Government program." **[VERIFY: Civilian Pilot Training Program at Tuskegee Institute, 1939–40]**

"Then go to Alabama."

"It's for college men, Ruth. I've got the eighth grade and a pocketknife." He turned the airplane over and sighted down the wing for warp. "There's a war coming, though. Everybody says so. A war uses up pilots the way a norther uses up firewood. Someday the Army's going to run short of white boys who can fly, and on that day a Mayfield's going to be standing at the gate."

"They'll still care about the color of your skin."

"They'll care," Sam said. "They'll care right up until the day they need me."

She put her hand flat on the warm rock between them. He looked at the hand a long time, then laid his own hand on the rock beside hers, an inch away, not touching, the way a man sets a coffee cup down near a sleeping baby, and the two hands stayed there on the limestone while the light went orange on the water and the heron lifted off and flapped away down the river.

Neither of them heard the boots on the river path.

Fritz Vellmer stood at the top of the ledge with a hay hook in his hand, out looking for a cow that had gone through the water gap. He looked at the two hands on the rock, and then he looked at Sam, and his face did not change at all, the way the face of a stone does not change.

Sam got to his feet. He picked up his boots, and he took off his hat, and he held the yellow airplane against his chest like a boy holding a hymnal.

"Mr. Vellmer," he said.

"Go home," Fritz said, in English. Then, in German, to his daughter, without looking at her: "Get your shoes."

Sam went up the river path in his bare feet with his hat back on his head, his boots in one hand and the yellow airplane in the other, and he did not run, and he did not turn his head. Ruth put on her shoes. Her father walked the two miles home a step behind her and never spoke one word, and the hay hook swung at his side the whole way, catching the last of the light.

He did not whip her. He did not speak of it at supper. That night, through the stone wall between the kitchen and her bed, Ruth heard her mother's voice, low and hard, in German.

"Fritz. *Was hast du vor?*" What is your plan?

Whatever her father answered, the stone wall kept it.

On Saturday morning Ruth saw the Ford parked at the Harwell gate, and her father standing in Mr. Harwell's yard with his hat on, talking, and Mr. Harwell nodding with his hands in his back pockets. On the following Friday, Hattie Mayfield's washing stopped coming to the Lindauer back door. Joseph Mayfield had run the Harwell herd twenty-two years, and his father before him, and Mr. Harwell let him go with two weeks' wages.

Neither man ever spoke of the Mayfields again, not at the feed store and not on the church steps.

---

Lena played the Luckenbach dance hall every other Saturday that summer, and on the second Saturday in August Ruth rode with her.

Lena parked the truck under the hackberries with the keys in the ignition, the way everybody in Pedernales County left a truck at a dance. The hall stood at the crossroads with its wooden floor worn into shallow troughs by fifty years of boots, and June bugs banged against the screens. Near the door that night stood a stranger with a Waco accent and a pint in his coat, and when Lena started a German waltz he threw his arm out stiff and shouted "Sieg heil."

The hall went dead quiet. A white-haired farmer by the door set down his beer, pushed the screen open with one hand and held it there without a word. Lena did not stop. She played the waltz through to the last note, slower and sweeter than she had ever played it, and when the dancers clapped she bowed to them and then bowed, separately, to the stranger, who went out through the held door before the next song.

"Why didn't you stop?" Ruth asked her on the drive home.

"Because stopping would have handed him the night," Lena said. "Remember that."

A long mile went by in the dark. Then Ruth told her sister about the ledge, and the hands, and the Ford at the Harwell gate, all of it, in a voice that did not shake until the very end.

Lena pulled the truck onto the shoulder and turned off the engine.

"Mail comes to the Sunday house on Tuesdays," Lena said. "Papa never in his life opened a letter with my name on the envelope. Tell the boy to write *Miss Lena Vellmer* on the outside and nothing else." She started the engine again. "Ruthie, if Papa finds out, I never heard of any of this."

"Not one word of it," Ruth said, and Lena reached across the seat and squeezed her knee once, hard, the way she pressed a string to the neck of the fiddle.

On Monday Ruth rode the mule to the line fence and put a folded paper in the Folgers can with Lena's name and the street of the Sunday house printed on it in block letters. The Sunday house was one room in town with an outside stair to a sleeping loft, where the Vellmers spent Saturday nights so they could make church in the morning.

The Mayfields left on the Thursday after, in a borrowed truck with the stake sides up and everything they owned roped into the bed: the iron bedstead, Hattie's washpots, a cookstove, two chairs, a crate of chickens. Ruth watched from the top of the windmill, where she had first laid eyes on Sam Mayfield. Joseph drove. Hattie sat beside him in her church hat. In the back, on the mattress, sat Eli, thirteen, with his arms around his knees, and beside him sat Sam with his face lifted to the sky, though no airplane crossed it that morning. The dust of the truck hung over the river road a long time after the sound was gone.

That evening the paper was gone from the can. In its place lay the balsa airplane with the yellow wings, the rubber band wound tight, ready to fly.

---

Walter's letters came from Pearl Harbor all that fall, in pencil, in short lines, about green water and a ring of mountains and the battleships moored in pairs along Ford Island. Margaret had sailed out in September to a rented cottage in Kaimuki. In November a baby came early and did not live, the second in two years, and Walter wrote that fact in one line and then wrote three lines about the weather.

At the bottom of the page he had added, in a different pencil, as though he had come back to it later:

*Ruthie, you would like it here. The whole world comes through this harbor.*

The first envelope addressed to *Miss Lena Vellmer* came to the Sunday house the week before Thanksgiving, and Lena brought it out to the homeplace buried in a sack of flour. Inside lay two pages in pencil, and the return address on the back named a frame house on the East Side of San Antonio.

Ruth read both letters on the roof of the barn that night by the spill of the kitchen window, Walter's and Sam's, with her coat pulled around her knees. The lights of the valley stood out across the dark, white and hard: the Vellmer kitchen, the Harwell house, the square in Martinsburg glowing faintly over the trees.

Up the river, where the yellow lamp had burned in the Mayfield window every night of her life, there was nothing now but dark and the long black line of the wire running past it.

Below her, Katharina pulled the string in the kitchen, and the window went black. Ruth sat on the cold tin with two letters inside her coat and watched the lights of the Pedernales go out, one by one, until the valley was as dark as it had been before anybody brought the light.
