# 38. The Coal Goes Grey

*Christmas morning, 2026, before first light. The cabin on the Vellmer homeplace.*

"Hand me her Bible, son."

Marcus finds it on the quilt, cracked at the spine and soft as a glove from her hands, and passes it to the old man. At the back, where the maps were before she wore them away, lies a clipping from a church magazine, folded once, the newsprint gone the color of a pecan shell. The crease has nearly cut it in half, and somebody has laid a strip of yellowed tape down the back to keep it in one piece.

The underlining is blue ink. So is the word in the margin, in small square capitals, pressed so hard the nib dented the back of the sheet.

RAVENSBRÜCK.

The fire is down to that one coal. The clipping goes close to the lamp.

The words belong to A. W. Tozer, the Chicago preacher. He wrote that God is not nearly so gentle as the popular teachers paint Him. He wrote that the tools a sculptor takes to a block of marble are cruel ones, the saw and the hammer and the chisel, and that without them the stone stays a rough and shapeless lump forever.

She underlined one short line twice in blue, and then, sometime later, traced it over a third time in pencil, as if she had come back to it on a hard night.

*A sculptor does not use a manicure set.*

The old man reads it out loud. Lily asks for it again, and he reads it again.

In December of 1944 Miss Ruth stood at a washroom window and saw what the chisel had finished: Betsie ten Boom on the concrete with the lines gone out of her face, like a woman of thirty asleep after a day in her garden. Seventeen years later Ruth wrote the name of the quarry in the margin.

Behind the clipping Lily's finger finds a card, the drugstore kind, gone soft at the corners. Folded with the card is a newspaper page with seven young women's faces across the top.

Lily reads the card out loud, slowly, the way she reads in church.

*The thief cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy. John 10:10. Men ought always to pray, and not to faint. Luke 18:1. I pray for you every day, Mama Ruth. Every day. K.*

"That's Kate Hartmann's hand," the old man says.

Marcus already has the phone in his hand. "Big Thompson flood. July thirty-first, 1976. A hundred and forty-four people dead." He scrolls with his thumb. "Seven of them were women from Campus Crusade for Christ, at a retreat on the river. One of the women who lived, Ney Bailey, wrote a book about that night and called it *Faith Is Not a Feeling*. She said she had to choose to give thanks with her will, not her feelings."

"That's Betsie's verse," Lily says. "The fleas."

Marcus keeps scrolling. "There's a video. The coroner who got the bodies ready in Loveland told them he could tell how the Crusade women died just by looking at them. He said a lot of people in that flood died with horror on their faces, and these women had none. He said he thought the Lord gave them that peace before the water took them." Marcus swallows. "One of the seven, a girl from the Philippines, had Isaiah 43 underlined in her Bible."

"*When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee,*" the old man says, "*and through the rivers, they shall not overflow thee: when thou walkest through the fire, thou shalt not be burned; neither shall the flame kindle upon thee.*"

The old man lets the verse sit a while before he goes on.

"Every one of us who comes out of a fire asks the same thing, children. *How could God let that happen to me?* Miss Ruth asked it for thirty-three years. The question sounds like grief, and some of it is, but under the grief sits an idea nobody ever said to us out loud, that we were owed an easy life, a life of comfort and happiness, and that God broke His end of the bargain the day the fire came. God never made that bargain. A child born into a Christian house is born into a war, the way Sam was born into one and Miss Ruth was born into one, and the Book says so plain: *For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places.* An old preacher told me once that this world is the closest a Christian will ever come to hell, and the closest an unbeliever will ever come to heaven. Miss Ruth stood closer to that first half than any woman I ever knew. That is where Tozer's chisel cuts. Folks read *a sculptor does not use a manicure set* in a warm church and nod, and not one of them imagines how a hammer and chisel feel to the stone. Evil men can do the most evil things a man can think of, and they do them, because the thief has one agenda, and Kate copied it out on that card: to steal, and to kill, and to destroy. The thief swings at the stone to break it. The Sculptor takes the same blows and cuts a woman out of them.

"Nobody wins that war alone. Moses could hold his arms up over the battle only as long as Aaron and Hur held them up for him, and when his arms came down, Amalek prevailed. That is why a family matters, and why a child out of a broken house starts the fight a step behind, because the hardest thing in this world is knowing whom to trust. Trust the wrong man and he pours you lemonade while the Milice wait in his back room. Trust nobody, the way Miss Ruth tried to for thirty years, and you fight every night by yourself in the dark. Kate held up her arms." He lifts the drugstore card. "That card is Aaron and Hur."

"Most days, though, we are not Aaron and Hur. Read a few verses back in that same chapter and you will find the same people picking up rocks. *They be almost ready to stone me,* Moses cried. The Book gives us our orders plain: *Bear ye one another's burdens,* and *pray one for another.* We would rather throw the stone at the man whose arms are shaking. When we do, evil walks in through the gap, into a life first, then a family, then a town, then a whole country, and behind evil come the real enemies, the ones who hate the God of the Bible and have sworn to kill every infidel who will not bow, whether the god they bow to is called Baal or Allah.

"So the health of a land does not lie with the lost, children, or with the prisoners, or with the men who are slaves to evil. The health of a land lies on the people who know the truth. *If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land.* The Lord did not lay that on the lost. He laid it on His own. Only the truth can turn a key like that. *And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.*"

Nobody in the cabin says a word. Marcus lays his phone face down on the quilt, and for once that night the boy does not reach for it.

Ben, who everybody thought was asleep, lifts his head off Lily's knee. "Grandpa. What about the bad ones? The man with the lemonade. Can he still go to heaven?"

"Every one of them could have gone either way, son," the old man says. "Peter swore three times he never knew the Lord, and Judas sold Him for thirty pieces of silver, and on the same night both men knew what they had done. *And Peter went out, and wept bitterly.* Judas *went and hanged himself.* Peter came back, and the Lord built His church on him. Judas could have come back, too. The door stood open to the man who poured the lemonade, and to the man who swung the hammer, and to the woman who threw the stone, and the name of that door is repentance."

The old man holds the card up to the lamp a long time.

"Kate never knew one thing about the war," he says. "Not the camp, not the fleas, not Betsie. She prayed for a wound she couldn't see, every day from the year she turned thirty until the Christmas Miss Ruth died, forty-one years, and she never once quit. Some battles run long. Noah hammered on that ark through the hundred and twenty years God gave that generation. Moses walked forty years in the desert. Kate prayed forty-one." He lays the card on the quilt. "Exodus seventeen. Israel is fighting Amalek down in the valley, and Moses stands on the hill with the rod of God in his hand. As long as his hands stay up, Israel wins. When his hands drop, Amalek wins. *But Moses' hands were heavy; and they took a stone, and put it under him, and he sat thereon; and Aaron and Hur stayed up his hands, the one on the one side, and the other on the other side; and his hands were steady until the going down of the sun.*"

He looks over at Ruthie.

Ruthie's face wears the look it wore at the start of the night, when she sat on the arm of the sofa with her coffee, the look that had crossed it only twice before Christmas Eve: the night her mother told her that Grandpa Eli had died, and the July night in 2020 when her daddy drove home alone from the parking garage at the Baptist hospital.

"My mama kept the prayer list for the missionary society at Mount Zion for better than forty years. When we buried her, your mama and I found the list in her Bible, and in her hand, from the week Grandma Hattie died: *Miss Ruth, the lady who was good to Mother Hattie.* I never knew why Mama kept that name there. I know tonight. Kate held up one arm and Mama held up the other, in the same city, and neither woman knew the other one was praying. Kate died in 2019 without hearing that the wound had a name, and the man who could have told her kept the answer buttoned under a cardigan on the other side of the same town.

"Church folks will tell a tired soldier to pray more and read his Bible more. Miss Ruth's own brother stood up in a church hall and threw Scripture at her like rocks. Moses didn't need a sermon on that hill. Moses needed two people to hold up his arms. Josh McDowell, the Campus Crusade evangelist, said it so plain that preachers wrote him nasty letters over it: when it comes to his salvation, all he needs is Jesus, but almost everything after his salvation, he needs more than Jesus. He needs the rest of us."

The old man points the poker at the grate, where the last red coal sits alone in the ash.

"At supper that coal lay in a bed of forty others. Pull one ember out of the fire, and it goes out by itself, every time. The devil knows it. The thief never has to put out the whole fire. He only has to get one of you off alone.

"If one of you ever lands in a desert for half your life, don't let anybody tell you God walked off and left you. Look for the wound, and look at who should have been standing on that hill beside you and walked off instead. Then find your Aaron and your Hur, and be one for somebody else."

Daniel stops snoring. The coal goes grey, the room goes blue, and the window over the pecan bottom turns from black to the grey of a mourning dove.

Across the frozen pasture the barn roof comes up out of the dark, then the fence lines, then the cedar breaks. The ice on every limb of every live oak catches the first light and holds it. A rooster on the far side of the river crows the day awake.

Ben wakes on the quilt and blinks at the window.

"Did Christmas come?"

"Christmas came, son."
