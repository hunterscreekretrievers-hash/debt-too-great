# 26. The Hearing

*Pedernales County Courthouse, Martinsburg, Texas, Tuesday, August 20, 2013*

The air conditioner in the second-floor courtroom quit at nine, and by ten the tall windows stood open on the square to the heat and the grackles. Ceiling fans turned overhead, slow as a windmill in a dying wind.

Miss Ruth sat at the counsel table on the left in her navy dress with the white collar, the one she wore to funerals, with her pocketbook on her knees and her long hands folded on top of it. Honey lay under the table with her chin on Miss Ruth's shoe.

The clerk's file on the bench held Eric's typed answer and, behind it, one page Miss Ruth had written at her kitchen table the week she climbed into the hayloft, in blue ink and her tall slanted hand, and made Eric file it: *I have read every page of this application. Ms. Strand never met me before she signed hers. I am eighty-nine years old, which is a different thing from incapacitated. I do not need a guardian. I need to be left alone on my father's land. Ruth M. Vellmer.*

Across the aisle Clayton Rusk had two tables pushed together, an associate with a laptop and a rolling cart of white binders tabbed in five colors. Margo Strand sat beside him in linen the color of oatmeal, with reading glasses on a beaded chain. She looked at Miss Ruth once, the way a woman checks the price tag on a lamp.

Kate sat in the first row behind her mother in her church clothes, and beside Kate sat a big Black man in his one good suit who had driven up from San Antonio at five that morning. Nobody in that room but Kate and her mother knew my name. Three rows behind us, by himself on the aisle, sat a small old man in a black suit too big in the shoulders, holding a straw hat on his knees.

The rest of the gallery was Martinsburg: Bonnie Gold, with a CLOSED TILL NOON sign on the Mustang Grill, Inge Lochte and Doris Ahrens near the back, and Dale Kunkel at the end of a row, turning his hat in his hands.

At five past ten Eric leaned down beside her and spoke under the noise of the fans. "Judge Wehmeyer is a Kerr County judge, sitting here by assignment, and I duck hunt with him," he said. "He's a fair judge, Miss Vellmer."

Miss Ruth kept her eyes on the empty bench. "Does he hit anything?"

"Every duck I miss."

The Honorable Leon Wehmeyer came in at ten past ten, a heavy, slow man with a white mustache, half-glasses and a voice like gravel shaken in a bucket. He looked at Rusk's binders, then under Miss Ruth's table at the dog.

"Mr. Rogers," he said. "Is that animal going to give me any trouble?"

"No, Your Honor. She's better behaved than her lawyer."

"That's a low bar, counselor." The judge opened the file. "Mr. Rusk. Your application. Proceed."

---

Rusk put on his case the way Miss Ruth had expected, and he put it on well.

He put Margo Strand on first, and she spoke for twenty minutes in a warm voice about vulnerable elders.

Eric stood for his cross-examination with nothing in his hands.

"Ms. Strand, how many times have you met Miss Vellmer?"

"I've reviewed the file extensively."

"How many times have you met her?"

Ms. Strand touched her glasses. "Today is the first occasion."

"Who first brought you Miss Vellmer's name?"

"Mr. Rusk's office contacted me."

"Mr. Rusk, who has offered on two occasions to buy Miss Vellmer's land."

"I'm not aware of the details of Mr. Rusk's business."

"If you were appointed guardian of her estate, could you petition this court to sell that land?"

"If it were in the ward's best interest."

"Whose interest, ma'am?"

Rusk rose. "Objection. Calls for speculation."

"Sustained," the judge said.

Eric picked up a single sheet from his table. "Ms. Strand, you are presently guardian of forty-one people in four counties. Is that right?"

"Approximately."

"How many of those forty-one have had a guardian's sale of real property in the last three years?"

The courtroom went quiet except for the fans. Ms. Strand looked at Rusk, and Rusk looked at his binder.

"I would have to check my records."

"I checked them for you," Eric said. "From the county dockets. Nineteen." He laid the sheet on the clerk's desk. "Pass the witness."

---

Dale Kunkel told the court about the Dollar General parking lot without once looking at Miss Ruth. Eric asked him two questions: who had typed the statement, and whether he had told that person the dog found the car. A young woman from Mr. Rusk's office, Kunkel said, and yes, he had told her the dog found the car. His neck went red as a turkey's wattle.

Harlan Moellering told the court about the eleven o'clock telephone call, the old woman asking whether he would hide a downed flier in his barn before the soldiers came. Eric handed him the option he had signed with Clayton Rusk in January and had him read aloud the paragraph that raised his price if an event venue went up next door. Harlan's voice slowed like a loaded truck on a long hill.

"Mr. Moellering," Eric said, "when Miss Vellmer called you that night, what did you tell her?"

Moellering looked at the page in his hands.

"I told her nobody'd come down in my pasture since a crop duster in 1962, and she said, oh. She said she was sorry, she'd been dreaming. Then she hung up the phone." He swallowed. "None of that went in my statement."

"Why not?"

"Nobody asked me. The young man from Austin typed it up, and I signed where he showed me."

---

Dr. Neil Prescott testified by telephone from San Antonio, his voice coming out of a black speakerphone on the rail of the witness box as if he were calling from inside a coffee can.

Rusk walked him through the certificate. Clock drawing, abnormal. Word recall, one of three. Dementia, probable Alzheimer's type. Totally without capacity.

Eric leaned toward the speakerphone. "Dr. Prescott, how long did you spend with Miss Vellmer on April twenty-third?"

"I'd have to look at my notes."

"Your clinic's billing record says eleven minutes. Does that sound right?"

"The nursing staff performs the screening. My portion of the visit would be shorter than the total."

"When Miss Vellmer came to your office, did you tell her you were examining her for a court proceeding?"

"She came for an annual wellness visit."

"Ms. Strand's office requested your certificate two weeks later."

"Yes."

"Doctor, on page three of your certificate you list eleven medications. Let me read you three of them." Eric took a folded square of butcher paper from his file, Kenji's calendar in carpenter's pencil, and laid it on the table without opening it. He read from a typed list. "Oxybutynin, for the bladder. Meclizine, for dizziness. Zolpidem, for sleep. Are you familiar with the American Geriatrics Society's Beers list, Doctor?"

A pause. "Of course."

"Tell the judge what the Beers list covers."

"A list, published by the Society last year, of medications that are potentially inappropriate in older adults."

"Why inappropriate?"

"For a number of reasons. Some have anticholinergic effects."

"Which cause what, in an eighty-nine-year-old woman?"

The speakerphone was quiet.

"Doctor?"

"Confusion. Memory impairment. Dizziness. In some patients."

"The same things you checked on your certificate."

"Those symptoms can have multiple causes."

"Did you rule out the medications as a cause before you signed?"

"I documented the medications."

"Doctor, did you rule out the medications? Yes or no."

"No," Dr. Prescott said.

"How many of those eleven did you prescribe yourself?"

"I'd have to check the chart."

"Five," Eric said. "Pass the witness."

---

After lunch Eric called Dr. Miriam Castellanos, a geriatric psychiatrist from the medical school in San Antonio. In the first days of August she had run a blood test on Miss Vellmer.

"Doctor, what did you find in her blood?" Eric said.

"Strongyloides," Dr. Castellanos said. "A threadworm, picked up barefoot in bad ground, the kind of infection that can live in a body for decades. I started treatment the day the result reached my desk."

Two cortisone shots for the hip that spring had put the body's guard to sleep and let the worm multiply, and the worm on top of eleven prescriptions made the fog Dr. Prescott wrote down as dementia. On the ninth of August, with the pills coming off, Miss Vellmer scored twenty-nine of thirty on the Mini-Mental State Examination.

"The affidavits in this file describe a woman who could not find her car," Dr. Castellanos said. "The woman I examined is clearer than those affidavits. She does not have dementia."

"What did she miss?" the judge asked from the bench. "The one point."

Dr. Castellanos smiled. "She couldn't tell me the floor number, Your Honor. She said she rode up in the elevator, and she has never in her life trusted a machine to count for her."

The gallery laughed, and the judge let the laugh run its course.

Whitney Hale, the attorney ad litem, told the court she had visited Miss Vellmer again on the fifteenth of August, found her putting up forty pints of peach preserves and been handed a wooden spoon and told to stir the pot. She now recommended that the application be denied.

Rusk looked at her the way a man looks at a tool that has broken in his hand.

---

At half past two Eric Rogers called Ruth Vellmer.

She rose, and Honey rose with her. Miss Ruth said, "Stay," and the dog lay back down with the one ear standing. Miss Ruth walked to the witness box without touching the rail, swore, and took the chair.

Eric asked her the date, and she told him. He asked her the name of the President, and she told him, and added that she had not voted for him. He asked her how many acres were in the homeplace, and she said two hundred and forty, and how many head of cattle her lessee ran on it, and she said sixty-one cows and two bulls, and that one of the bulls was sorry and should be sold before winter. He handed her a yellow legal pad and a pencil and asked her to draw a clock with the hands at ten minutes past eleven.

She drew a round clock. She put the twelve, the three, the six and the nine first, the way her father had laid out the corners of a building before he set the rest of the stone, and then she filled in the numbers between them, evenly spaced as fence posts. She drew the hands to the eleven and the two and handed the pad to Eric, who carried it to the bench.

The judge held the pad at arm's length and studied the clock.

"Miss Vellmer," Eric said, "what happened to you in the Dollar General parking lot on May sixteenth?"

"I couldn't find my car," she said. "I'd taken a pill for dizziness that made me dizzy and a pill for my bladder that made me stupid. My dog found the car."

"What do you want to happen to the Vellmer homeplace?"

"I want it left alone," she said. "My father built that house in 1908. I want to live in it till I die and then leave it to whomever I please."

"Pass the witness."

---

Clayton Rusk rose, buttoned his jacket and smiled at her the way he had smiled on her porch swing in October.

"Miss Vellmer, you've had a remarkable recovery."

"Yes."

"According to Dr. Castellanos, you stopped several medications in June and July. Who advised you to stop them?"

"A man named Kenji Morita."

"Mr. Morita isn't a physician, is he?"

"No."

"Mr. Morita, in fact, holds no license in any profession in the State of Texas. He's an herbalist. He lives on a creek outside Comfort with no telephone. He looks in people's eyes with a flashlight and tells them they have worms."

"He told me I had worms."

"You believed him."

"I believed him. Dr. Castellanos's blood test says he was right." She folded her long hands in her lap. "I saw them, Mr. Rusk. You may ask me how, if you care to hear the answer."

The judge put his hand over his mustache. Rusk did not ask her how.

"Miss Vellmer, Dr. Prescott holds a license to practice medicine in this state."

"A license is a piece of paper, Mr. Rusk. A long time ago I saw girls limp on legs that licensed doctors had cut open to test a sulfa powder. The surgeon who ran it held every license in the Reich. The Americans hanged him in 1948. The woman doctor who worked under him got twenty years, was out in five and kept a family practice in Germany until 1958."

"Miss Vellmer, a man you met a few weeks ago took you off medications prescribed by your doctor and gave you some sort of homemade preparation in a Mason jar. Now you want this court to find you capable of making sound decisions."

"Mr. Rusk," she said, "Mr. Morita sat in a chair with his knees almost touching mine and looked at me for an hour. Dr. Prescott looked at a computer for eleven minutes, and then he signed a paper for you. One of them made me sick. One of them made me well." She looked at the speakerphone. "I'd call that a sound decision."

Rusk smiled again. The smile stayed only on his mouth.

"Miss Vellmer, Ms. Strand is a certified professional guardian. Her only purpose is your protection."

"I know what she's paid for it," Miss Ruth said. "Five percent of everything that comes into my estate and five percent of everything that goes out, paid from my own money. A long time ago I watched a polite professional at a typewriter decide who lived and who went up the chimney, Mr. Rusk. He was certified too."

Rusk turned to the bench. "Your Honor, I'd ask that the answer be struck as nonresponsive."

"You asked her, counselor," Judge Wehmeyer said. "She answered you."

"Miss Vellmer, in March you telephoned your neighbor at eleven o'clock at night and asked him to hide a man in his barn. From soldiers."

"I made that call."

"What soldiers, Miss Vellmer? What man?"

She did not answer at once. The fans turned. Out on the square a cattle truck downshifted for the light.

"I was dreaming about the war, Mr. Rusk," she said. "Old women who were girls in 1944 dream about the war. Some of us dream about it every night under God's heaven. I was dreaming on a pill that makes old women walk in their sleep, and I woke up with the telephone in my hand. Harlan told me nobody had come down in his pasture, and I told him I was sorry, and I hung up the phone and sat on the floor of my kitchen until the windows went grey." She did not look at Harlan Moellering. "I have carried that war by myself for sixty-eight years. I don't need a pill to bring it back, and I don't need you."

Nobody in the courtroom moved. Kate's hand found my wrist and held it like a tourniquet. Under the counsel table Honey stood up, as if somebody had called her name.

"No further questions," Rusk said.

---

Judge Wehmeyer did not take the matter under advisement. He took off his half-glasses and folded them.

"Twenty-two years on a bench," he said. "I've signed off on a lot of guardianships, and most of them were needed, and I'd sign them again." He looked at the speakerphone, and the bailiff, without being asked, reached over and silenced it. "I don't believe I've ever had a proposed ward draw me a better clock than I could draw myself."

He looked at Rusk.

"The application is denied. The applicant has failed to prove incapacity by clear and convincing evidence. Costs of this proceeding, including the fees of the attorney ad litem, are assessed against the applicant. I'll want briefing from Mr. Rogers in two weeks on whether this application was brought in good faith." He picked up his pen. "Miss Vellmer, you're free to go home. I'd sell that bull."

---

In the hallway Kate, crying, had her arms around her mother before Miss Ruth was through the door, and my one hand went to Kate's back and the other to Miss Ruth's shoulder. Inge Lochte and Doris Ahrens stopped at the head of the back stairs. Doris half lifted a hand toward Miss Ruth, let it fall, and followed Inge down with her eyes on the stairs. Kenji Morita stood by the water fountain with his straw hat in his hands. When Miss Ruth looked at him, he bowed his head to her, short and formal, and she bowed hers in return.

"I told you he was a fair judge," Eric said at her elbow.

"You told me." Miss Ruth looked back at the courtroom door. "In February of 1943 the president of the People's Court in Berlin, Roland Freisler, screamed from the bench at a college girl of twenty-one named Sophie Scholl, and she went under the guillotine the same day. Every judge on that court had a law in his hand, Eric. A courtroom is only as honest as the people in it. This courtroom happened to be honest."

Clayton Rusk came out last, with his associate pushing the cart of binders behind him.

He stopped in front of her. He did not offer his hand. He looked past her shoulder through the tall window toward the river, the way a fence cutter looks at another man's wire.

Honey stood up from Miss Ruth's feet. She looked at Rusk, head to shoes. Then she turned her back on him, walked to Miss Ruth's other side, and lay down again.

For one second Rusk's jaw worked, and then his face shut like a bank at five o'clock. He walked down the stairs into the white sun on the square, and the cart of binders bumped down every step behind him.

"Well," Kate said, wiping her face. "What was that?"

"A grade," Miss Ruth said.
