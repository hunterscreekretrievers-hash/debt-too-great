# 36. The Four Drawers

*Christmas morning, 2026, still dark. The cabin on the Vellmer homeplace.*

"How do you know all of it?" Lily asks her grandfather.

The old man waits long enough for the last coal to tick twice, then lays two fingers on the inside of his own wrist, the way he laid them on Ben's at the start of the night.

"Because I was in the room, sugar. Some of those rooms. In the summer of 1969 a captain from the division's intelligence detachment came for me at the aid station outside Cu Chi. The detachment had a Viet Cong prisoner in a sandbagged hut. Prisoners had died on other teams in the middle of the questions, and the captain wanted no more of that paperwork. My job was to make sure this man did not die on them. First they gave him the water: a towel over his face and a five-gallon jerry can tipped slow, until he fought it like a roped calf. Then came the field telephone, two wires off the hand crank to his skin and a sergeant turning the handle. Last came a syringe of sodium amytal, the stuff the newspapers called truth serum, to loosen whatever the water and the wires had left. For three hours I kept two fingers on that man's wrist and one eye on his chest. When the pulse ran wild or the breathing went shallow, I told the captain, and the captain waited. When the pulse settled, the captain started again. The prisoner gave up a tunnel hospital and the names of the men who ran it, and the names went to the Phoenix teams. What that man said under the needle was used later, and so was I."

"Grandpa," Lily says. "Did you ever tell them to stop?"

The old man lifts the two fingers off his own wrist and looks at them a long time. Lily gets no answer.

Marcus has stopped scrolling.

"After that the Army had a use for a medic who could read a sick man's chart and see the secret in it. The Army kept me twenty years carrying a government briefcase between San Antonio and places I still will not name: military intelligence first, then medical intelligence, reading the hospital files of other governments and the books of the drug houses that supplied them. In 1977 a Senate committee read the CIA's own MKUltra files into the record, drugs tried on men who never knew they had taken them, and I was not one bit surprised. I had held the wrist. I finished where I started, in medicine, sixteen years on the city ambulance."

Ruthie pulls the afghan tighter around her shoulders. "Mama used to say you could talk the paint off a barn when something was on your heart."

"Your mama was right about most things. I learned to talk in the back of an ambulance. Sixteen years I rode on the bench beside people who were scared or dying, a girl cut out of a car on Loop 410, an old man with his heart misfiring at three in the morning, and a man in that seat learns to keep talking. You talk about the Spurs, the weather, their grandbabies, anything to take their mind off the blood and the siren. As long as you talk, they listen, and as long as they listen, they stay with you. Miss Ruth was made the other way, and the war required it of her. A courier who talks is a courier who dies, and so do the people who trusted her with their names. She learned to hold her tongue in a cell on Avenue Foch with a man's cigarette an inch from her arm, and she held it the rest of her life, every card face down on the table. Between the two of us you children have heard both halves tonight, what she would never say and what I cannot stop saying. Tonight something is on my heart. Twelve years I have carried it, and you children are the first ones with ears to hear the whole of it." He settles back in the cowhide chair the way a man settles in for a long drive.

"I started reading two years before Cu Chi, on a street corner. In the spring of 1967 I was twenty-one, in my third year at St. Mary's and pumping gas on Commerce Street, and every Saturday a man in a bow tie and a pressed white shirt sold *Muhammad Speaks* off a card table across from Mount Zion, a quarter a copy. He told every young Black man who walked past his table that Christianity was the slave master's religion, that the white man's Jesus came over on the slave ships, and that the Crusades proved it, Christians riding into the Holy Land to butcher brown people. On the twenty-eighth of April, 1967, Cassius Clay, who called himself Muhammad Ali by then, stood in the Army's induction station on San Jacinto Street in Houston and refused to step forward when the officer called his name. By the next Saturday the man in the bow tie had the Ali story on his card table, and half the boys on the East Side wanted to be Ali.

"I wanted to answer the man. Grandma Hattie had taught me to read on the King James, so I took my day off to the main library downtown and asked the librarian for the history. I read six Saturdays running. Here is what the man left off his card table. Christ was preached in Africa before there ever was a Muhammad. Augustine was a bishop in North Africa, at Hippo, in the year 400, and the king of Ethiopia was baptized two hundred years before Muhammad was born. The armies of Islam took Jerusalem in 638, took Christian Egypt three years later, swept the whole north coast of Africa, crossed into Spain in 711 and reached the middle of France by 732. In 1071 the Turks broke the Christian army at Manzikert and took most of Asia Minor, and the emperor in Constantinople begged the West for help. In 1095 the Pope answered him and called the First Crusade. The seventh Saturday I laid my library notes on his card table, 638 to 1095, and the man in the bow tie read every page without a word. The Crusades did not start that fight, children. The Crusades answered four hundred years of it. The man sold me his paper anyway. A quarter is a quarter.

"Here is one more thing the man never sold for a quarter. The Arab slave trade carried Black Africans north across the Sahara and east across the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean for eight hundred years before the first Portuguese ship loaded a slave on the African coast, and it kept running long after the last American slave ship sailed. The scholars count the people carried in the millions. Saudi Arabia did not abolish slavery until 1962. Mauritania waited until 1981. Two years after Ali refused induction, I went, with a rifle and a medic's bag, and the first man I carried out of a paddy under fire was a white boy from Ohio. That library gave me the one habit that kept me alive in the Army: read the history yourself, and never buy a man's summary of it. Every drawer in my garage started on that corner."

Marcus types *Mauritania 1981* and turns the screen to Lily.

"Twenty years inside taught me one thing about governments, ours included. The paper always tells on them, if a man lives long enough to read it.

"The infiltration started the summer the war ended. Under a program called Operation Paperclip our government brought more than sixteen hundred German scientists, engineers and technicians to this country after 1945 and scrubbed the Nazi parts out of their files before they ever landed. Wernher von Braun had worn the uniform of an SS officer, and he built the rocket that carried our men to the moon. Arthur Rudolph ran production at the Mittelwerk tunnels, where slave laborers from the Dora camp died by the thousands building those rockets, and he managed the Saturn V program for NASA. In 1984, with the Justice Department's Nazi hunters at his door, Rudolph gave up his citizenship and flew to Germany rather than face them. Hubertus Strughold ran aviation medicine for the Luftwaffe. In October of 1942 he sat in a conference in Nuremberg where the Dachau doctors laid out their freezing experiments on prisoners, men held in tubs of ice water until they died. Five years later our Army Air Forces brought Strughold to Randolph Field in San Antonio and called him the father of space medicine. My medical intelligence desk sat a few miles from Brooks Air Force Base, and for years I walked past his portrait in a hallway of the Strughold Aeromedical Library there. The Air Force took his name off that library in 1995. The Polish girls Miss Ruth met in the camp hospital at Ravensbrück, the ones the camp called the Rabbits, had their legs cut open by the same school of doctors."

Lily's hand goes to her own shin under the quilt and stays there.

"One more folder in THE MEN drawer came out of the CIA's own vault in 2017: a dispatch from October of 1955 about a German who swore he had met Adolf Hitler alive in Tunja, Colombia, in 1954, and carried a photograph to prove it. The station chief sent it up the chain. The paper sat in a CIA drawer sixty-two years."

Marcus finds the photograph that came with that dispatch and turns the phone to Lily. "He looks like somebody's grandpa," Lily says. Her hand has not left her shin.

"Strughold looked like somebody's grandpa on the library wall at Brooks, sugar.

"On the fourth of November, 1979, a mob in Tehran climbed the wall of our embassy, took sixty-six Americans and held fifty-two of them to the end, and for the next four hundred and forty-four days I read the cables on that crisis in a room at Fort Sam Houston with no windows. In April of 1980 the rescue went in at a patch of desert the planners called Desert One, and a helicopter cut into a refueling plane in the dark, and eight of our men burned to death in the sand. The ayatollah's people showed the bodies on television. On the twenty-third of October, 1983, a truck bomb from Iran's own Hezbollah flattened the Marine barracks at the Beirut airport and killed two hundred and forty-one of our servicemen in their bunks. One of them was the grandson of a deacon at Mount Zion. I stood beside that deacon at the grave. Remember those three, children: the embassy, the desert and the barracks. You will need them in a minute.

"The morning of the eleventh of September, 2001, I was on the ambulance, and our crew sat in the station on Commerce Street with the television on and our boots on, waiting for a call that never came, because every call in the country that morning was in New York. We watched the towers fall. Two airplanes, two towers, the whole world saw that much. Then, at twenty minutes past four our time, a third building came down, forty-seven stories called Building Seven. No airplane ever touched it. The building went straight down into its own footprint, and for better than two seconds it fell at the speed of a dropped stone, and the government's own engineers at the National Institute of Standards and Technology finally admitted that free fall in their report of 2008, after a high school physics teacher made them."

Marcus finds the footage and turns the phone to the lamp. Building Seven drops straight down out of the frame in about the time Ben takes to draw two breaths. Lily asks to see the fall again, and Marcus plays the clip a second time without a word.

"Twenty minutes before that building fell, a reporter for the BBC stood on camera in New York and told the world Building Seven had already collapsed, with Building Seven standing in the window over her left shoulder. The government's engineers said office fires brought the building down, the first steel high-rise in the history of the world to fall from fire alone. In March of 2020 a team of engineers at the University of Alaska Fairbanks spent four years on the same building and reported that fire did not cause the collapse of Building Seven. I worked sixteen years of fires on that ambulance, children. I have stood in the street and watched old buildings burn all night. Fire does not do what I watched Building Seven do on that television.

"In the spring of 2002 we picked an old man up off a porch on the South Side with his left arm gone dead and his words coming thick. He had spent forty years taking down grain elevators, smokestacks and hospital wings for a wrecking outfit out of Houston. On the stretcher he could not find his wife's name, but he could still find his trade. The television in the emergency room hall was running the towers again while we waited on a bed, and he lifted his good hand and stopped me with it. 'That one,' he said, slow, and pointed at the third building as it folded into its own dust. 'That's a pull. Charges on the columns. Forty years I set them.' He held my wrist until the nurse took him from us. A man who cannot remember his wife's name does not make up a lie on a stretcher."

Marcus plays the clip a third time and watches the bottom floors instead of the top.

"Ten years later the soldiers came home from Iraq, at the end of 2011, and every man who ever read an intelligence summary knew what would fill the empty ground behind them. The Defense Intelligence Agency wrote it down in August of 2012. The report said there was 'the possibility of establishing a declared or undeclared Salafist principality in eastern Syria,' and then it said, 'this is exactly what the supporting powers to the opposition want.' I wrote memos like that for twenty years. A memo like that goes up the chain for one reason: so that nobody at the top can ever plead ignorance. That September armed men burned our consulate at Benghazi, and Miss Ruth wrote the paper about it two days later.

"In January of 2014 President Obama told a magazine writer the Islamic State was a JV team. In June of 2014 the JV team took Mosul. The general who ran the Defense Intelligence Agency when that report was written, Michael Flynn, went on Al Jazeera in 2015 and said the rise of the Islamic State came from 'a willful decision' in Washington. In December of 2017 a weapons-tracing outfit in London called Conflict Armament Research published what it found in the Islamic State's armories: rifles and rockets the United States and Saudi Arabia had bought for the rebels in Syria, now in the hands of the men painting that letter on Christian doors.

"Now remember the embassy, the desert and the barracks. On the seventeenth of January, 2016, Iran let four Americans fly home. That same day an unmarked cargo plane landed in Tehran with four hundred million dollars on board, euros and Swiss francs and other money stacked on wooden pallets, cash that left no trail. Two more planeloads followed within weeks, a billion three hundred million more. That August the State Department admitted it had held the first cash back as 'leverage' until the Americans were out of the country. I spent four hundred and forty-four days of my life reading what that government did to the Americans in its hands. Thirty-six years later my own government paid the ransom on pallets and called it something else."

Ruthie's hand closes on the afghan.

"The infiltration never stopped, children. Only the flag on the men changed. In my day a man who wrote Communist on his polygraph would not have been cleared to empty the trash at Fort Sam. I sat through eleven of those polygraphs myself, with the cuff on my arm and a stranger asking me whether I had ever belonged to any organization that sought to overthrow the government of the United States. One folder in my garage has a single name on the tab. John Brennan was our CIA station chief in Riyadh in the 1990s. In 2016 Brennan told a room full of people that in 1976 he had voted for Gus Hall, the Communist Party's candidate for president, and that he had admitted it on his CIA polygraph. In February of 2010, at New York University, Brennan called Jerusalem by its Arabic name, Al-Quds. That May he told a Washington audience that we should not call our enemies jihadists, 'because jihad is a holy struggle, a legitimate tenet of Islam.'"

Marcus lifts his head. "The CIA let a Gus Hall voter in the front door?"

"The Senate handed the man the keys to the building, son.

"In February of 2013, the month the Senate took up his nomination to run the CIA, a former FBI counterterrorism agent named John Guandolo said publicly that Brennan had converted to Islam while he served in Saudi Arabia. The Senate confirmed him that March, and he ran the CIA four years. In October of 2020 John Brennan put his name on a letter about Hunter Biden's laptop, one of fifty-one signers. A Nazi doctor in San Antonio, a Communist voter in the director's chair, and the Brotherhood's own words in the director's mouth. The Communists wrote down where they meant to go, and so did the Brotherhood: into the government, the schools and the churches."

The fire pops once, and a spark lands on the hearthstone and goes dark by Ruthie's foot. Nobody moves to brush the spark off the stone.

"The third drawer says THE MEDICINE, and it is the fullest, because I started it in a sandbagged hut in 1969. The chemists and the pill makers never broke their habits, and I can show you the scars on the men who paid for those habits. In 1969 the helicopters laid Agent Orange, Dow's and Monsanto's best, along the riverbanks around Cu Chi, and the 25th Infantry breathed it in the dust and drank it in the paddy water. Our platoon went one by one, lymphoma and prostate and heart, while for years the VA paid on one disease alone, a skin rash called chloracne, until the Agent Orange Act of 1991 wrote lymphoma into the law. The government took twenty-two years and an act of Congress to own what the Army sprayed on the 25th Infantry, and by then the cancers had put names on half of us. I carried two of those men to the hospital myself in the back of a city ambulance, thirty years after the jungle, and one of them called me Doc on the way. From 1996 on, a good share of what the city ambulance carried off the East Side came out of an OxyContin bottle.

"In the summer of 2001 we worked a man in a pressed shirt and good shoes on the asphalt beside his company car in a parking lot off Loop 410, his heart quitting on him at forty-four. He sold pills for a living. On the ride in he told me about the bonus money, more than his salary that year, and about the map in his trunk with every high-writing doctor in Bexar County circled in red. The company had trained him to tell every doctor that fewer than one patient in a hundred ever became an addict. 'They gave us that number on a card,' he said. 'I said it to every doctor on the map.' The number came from a letter five sentences long, printed in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1980, about patients in hospital beds, and the card never named its source. That year Purdue paid its sales force forty million dollars in bonuses. The man lived, and he quit, and he sent me a Christmas card for nine years.

"Purdue pleaded guilty in May of 2007, and by its own second guilty plea, in 2020, its next fraud began that same month. In 2018 Bayer, a founding piece of I.G. Farben, bought Monsanto and retired the name. The same companies, the same habits.

"For twenty years the Army paid me to read who owned what, because the man who owns the factory owns the decision. Look up who owns Pfizer, son."

Marcus types the company's name and reads the top of the shareholder list off the glass. "Vanguard. BlackRock. State Street."

"Merck has the same three at the top. Johnson & Johnson has the same three. The same three funds sit near the top of the list for the companies that own the networks selling those drugs on the evening news. In 2024, by the OpenSecrets count, the drug and health-products industry spent more on lobbying than any other industry in America, about two hundred and ninety-four million dollars. Since 1992 the drug companies have paid user fees to the Food and Drug Administration, and by 2022 those fees covered two of every three dollars the agency spent on its human-drug program. The referee draws his pay from the team.

"One night in the nineties a mother rode the ambulance bench beside me with her baby boy in her lap, two days past his shots, and asked me the name of the company she could take to court. Since 1986 a child hurt by a childhood vaccine has had to take the claim to a special federal court before any jury hears the case, and in 2011 the Supreme Court held in *Bruesewitz v. Wyeth* that the maker cannot be sued over the vaccine's design. On the second of September, 2009, Pfizer agreed to pay two billion three hundred million dollars, the largest health care fraud settlement in Department of Justice history, and a Pfizer company pleaded guilty over the painkiller Bextra.

"In May of 2016 two doctors at Johns Hopkins, Martin Makary and Michael Daniel, wrote in the *British Medical Journal* that medical error killed about two hundred and fifty thousand Americans a year. That number would make medical error the third leading cause of death in this country, behind heart disease and cancer. After sixteen years on the ambulance, I believe every digit of it."

"Eleven bottles," Lily says. "Miss Ruth had eleven bottles on her windowsill. The Japanese man read every label through his bifocals."

"Eleven bottles and an eleven-minute doctor, sugar. Miss Ruth was one woman inside that number, and a Japanese man with a penlight pulled her out of the count."

The tag slides around on its chain while the old man talks, and a thumb sets it back over the breastbone before the next drawer.

Daniel snores in the recliner, a long ragged pull and a whistle as the breath leaves him. The old man tips his head toward him.

"Your daddy goes duck hunting every January at Port Lavaca and comes home with a sunburn and two teal. The second drawer says THE MONEY, and the first folder in it is another duck hunt. In November of 1910 six men went duck hunting and never fired a shot. A United States senator, Nelson Aldrich, had his private railroad car pulled into a siding in New Jersey after dark, with the shades drawn on every window. Into it climbed a Morgan partner, the president of the National City Bank, Paul Warburg of Kuhn, Loeb, an assistant secretary of the Treasury and the senator's own secretary, every one of them carrying a shotgun case that stayed shut the whole trip. The car rolled south to Jekyll Island, off the coast of Georgia. At the club there the six men called one another by first names only, Frank and Harry and Paul, so the colored waiters pouring their coffee would never learn who sat at the table. Grandma Hattie would have known every one of their names by the second pot of coffee. A woman pouring coffee hears everything said at a table, and the men at the table never once look at the woman. For nine days they wrote the plan that became the Federal Reserve, and Paul Warburg wrote the heart of it. Paul Warburg sat on the first Federal Reserve Board while his brother Max ran the family bank in Hamburg and advised the Kaiser's government through the first war. Frank Vanderlip of the National City Bank owned up to the trip in 1935: 'We had disappeared from the world onto a deserted island.' On the twenty-second of December, 1913, the House passed the Federal Reserve Act, 298 to 60. The next day the Senate passed it, and most of the senators ran from the vote straight to the trains at Union Station. The President signed the act that evening, two days before Christmas. The men who own the money have kept a duck-hunting story handy from that day to this one.

"The second folder in that drawer is a tower. In 1901 J. P. Morgan put up the money for Nikola Tesla to build a tower at Wardenclyffe on Long Island. Tesla meant to send power through the air to anybody with a receiver, and a man cannot hang a meter on the air. Morgan cut off the money. In 1917 the tower was dynamited for scrap. Tesla died alone in a room at the Hotel New Yorker in January of 1943, an American citizen for more than fifty years, and Miss Ruth told Dixie Beck the rest of that story herself, over a co-op light bill on her porch swing in 2013: the dead man's trunks carted off by the government's Office of Alien Property Custodian, and the MIT engineer the government sent to go through them, John G. Trump, uncle of the man who became President. Dixie told me so at the funeral supper, with a dish towel over her shoulder. 'I told her she sounded like my Jody after two beers, baby,' Dixie said, 'and she told me my Jody was right more often than I ever told him.'

"The third folder is a mechanic from Grove City, Ohio, named Stanley Meyer, who built a dune buggy to run on water split by a fuel cell of his own design. In March of 1998 he stood up from a restaurant table, grabbed his throat, and told his brother they had poisoned him. The coroner wrote down an aneurysm."

"It runs on water?" Lily says.

"The man built it to run on water, sugar, and a man cannot hang a meter on a creek any more than on the air.

"The last folder in THE MONEY is the courtroom. Look at the flag behind any federal judge: gold fringe on three sides. That fringe marks a court of admiralty, the law of the sea and of commerce, and not the common law our fathers carried over from England and wrote into the Seventh Amendment. Every judge who has heard that argument has ruled against it. The men who hold the gavel decide what gets heard, children. That has never once decided what is true.

"The last drawer says THE PLAN, and for twelve years that drawer had a hole in the front of it. I could never find where the plan was first read out loud in this country."

Lily has found another of Miss Ruth's pages. Across the top runs a date, *March 1963*, and under it five lines typed in red ribbon, the rest of the page blank. Beside four of the five sit pencil notes, each in a different hand of the same woman, steadier on the early ones and shaky on the last.

"Number seventeen. 'Get control of the schools.'" Lily looks at the pencil and does not read it aloud. Then she says it anyway. "She wrote, *Ask any child.*"
