# 18. Kate

*San Antonio and the Vellmer homeplace, August 1950 to February 1956*

Margaret Vellmer died in an iron lung in the county hospital in San Antonio on a Tuesday in August, in the polio summer of 1950, with the machine still breathing for her after her heart quit.

Ruth got there an hour too late. A nurse walked her down a green hall that smelled of Lysol and hot rubber, past a ward where the big tan cylinders sat end to end like boilers in a ship's engine room. A head stuck out through the rubber collar of each one, with a tilted mirror above it. The bellows under the tanks pumped all together, a long wheeze and a thump, over and over, so the whole room seemed to breathe with one set of lungs.

The bellows under Margaret's tank had already gone quiet. Somebody had pulled a sheet over the head end.

"The girl's in the waiting room," the nurse said. "She's been there since yesterday. She won't let anybody take the suitcase."

Kate Vellmer was eight years old and sat on a wooden bench with her feet not touching the floor. She wore a brown plaid dress with the hem let down twice and saddle shoes somebody had polished that morning. A cardboard suitcase stood on the bench beside her, tied shut with a man's necktie. On her lap she held a Mason jar full of buttons.

She had Walter's cowlick. Ruth saw it from the doorway, the same stubborn whorl at the crown that no water had ever flattened on her brother, and for a second Ruth could not take another step.

"I know your face," Kate said. "You're Aunt Ruth. Mama said you'd come."

"I came."

"You're late."

"Yes."

Kate studied her. Her eyes were Margaret's, brown and wide-set, and dry.

"Is she dead?"

Ruth had told men on the wards in Honolulu that they would lose a leg. She sat down on the far side of the suitcase, so as not to crowd the child, and gave Kate the answer straight.

"Yes. She died this morning."

Kate nodded. She unscrewed the lid of the Mason jar and screwed it shut. "These are her buttons. She cut them off every dress that wore thin. She said you never throw away a good button. I'm not going to an orphan home."

"No," Ruth said. "You're coming with me."

"Where do you live?"

"On the river. The place your daddy was raised."

Kate considered this. "Mama said he could climb anything."

"He could, and so could I."

---

They buried Margaret beside her own mother in Brenham. Kate rode the whole way to the Pedernales with the jar of buttons between her knees and her face turned to the window. She did not cry once.

The homeplace had gone as quiet as a church on a Monday that year. Fritz's heart had turned on him in the winter, and the doctor in town had told him plainly that he could not lift another stone. He and Katharina had moved into Lena's Sunday house in town. Ruth kept the big limestone house going alone.

The first night on the river, Kate would not sleep in the big house.

"It's too big," she said, standing in the hall in her nightgown with the suitcase in one hand and the button jar in the other. "Mama and me had one room."

Ruth took the lamp and walked her across the yard in the dark, past the windmill and the stock tank, to the old cedar-and-limestone cabin Fritz had put up for the hired men before the first war. Inside, the moonlight came through the one window onto a stone fireplace and an iron bedstead with a bare ticking mattress.

"The hired hands slept here," Ruth said. "Your Opa built that fireplace. Every rock."

Kate walked to the fireplace and put her palm flat against the stones as if they might still be warm.

"I want this one," she said.

They slept there that night, the two of them in the iron bed under three quilts, with a fire in the grate though the August night did not need one. Kate slept with her back pressed against Ruth's ribs. Sometime after midnight Ruth woke and felt the child shaking. Kate made no sound. Ruth lay still and let her shake, and in the morning neither of them said a word about it.

By September the cabin had curtains made from flour sacks, a braided rug, and the Mason jar of buttons in the window where the morning sun lit them red and blue and mother-of-pearl. Kate called it her house. By Christmas the name she had for Ruth was "Mama Ruth," though nobody had ever suggested it.

Every night Kate knelt by the iron bed in the cabin and said the German prayer her Oma Katharina had taught her, the same prayer Katharina had taught all six Vellmer children. *Müde bin ich, geh zur Ruh, schließe beide Äuglein zu.* Ruth stood in the doorway with her arms folded and her lips shut. She had said the same prayer every night of her own childhood, and she had not said one word to the Lord of her own will since a sacristy in the Drôme. *Vater, laß die Augen dein über meinem Bette sein.* Father, let Your eyes be over my bed. Ruth turned out the lamp and walked back across the yard to the big house, and she lay awake a long time knowing exactly whose eyes those were, and that they were over her bed too, and still she would not speak to Him.

---

People in Martinsburg knew some things about Miss Ruth Vellmer. They knew her as the mail lady, the tall woman in the Willys who came down every county road six days a week. They knew she had gone overseas in the war with the Red Cross and come home thin as a buggy whip, that she had never married, and that she did not come to church. They knew she ran cattle on the river place by herself and could back a stock trailer down a fence line straighter than her brothers.

Every rancher on the route learned inside a week to pinch out his cigarette before he took his mail from her hand, and not one of them learned why. One morning in 1951 an old German shepherd charged the fence at the Moellering place as the Jeep came up the lane, and Ruth stopped dead in the road with both hands on the wheel and sat there until the shaking passed. The next week Mr. Moellering chained the dog behind the barn, and he never asked her a thing about it.

In 1952 Ruth sent off to a bookshop in San Antonio for a London book called *The White Rabbit* and read it in bed after Kate was asleep in the cabin, until the window went grey. Sixteen of the men chained in that train corridor had been hanged in a cellar at Buchenwald in September of 1944. The wing commander had lived only by taking the name of a Frenchman dead of typhus and hiding in the camp's own typhus block, and nobody in the book had the face of the man who had whispered God bless you.

Other things she kept in the dark, the way a woman banks a fire for the night.

Behind the winter quilts on her closet shelf, the shoebox held the photograph face down, the two clippings from 1945, and a column from January 1950: Alger Hiss, the State Department man who had sat at Roosevelt's elbow at Yalta, stood convicted of perjury for swearing he had never handed government papers to a Soviet courier named Whittaker Chambers, who had hidden the microfilm overnight in a hollowed-out pumpkin on his Maryland farm. Ruth had been a courier herself, and she knew what a courier carried, and for whom.

Hattie Mayfield's letters came in care of Lena at the Sunday house, so that no envelope from the East Side of San Antonio ever passed through the pigeonholes Ruth cased at six-thirty every morning, and Lena kept each one in her fiddle case under the cake of rosin. The fall Kate came, the first envelope was waiting the Saturday Ruth drove in with the cattle tally for her father, and Ruth read it standing at Lena's drainboard, the paper smelling of rosin, with Fritz asleep in his chair by the radio. France had taught her to keep a secret off the roads her friends traveled every day.

Nobody in town knew who paid off the account at Weber's Grocery for Mrs. Grieshaber after her husband rolled the tractor into the creek in 1951, or who left a side of beef wrapped in butcher paper on the back porch of the Ybarra family the winter Tomás Ybarra lost his job at the gin.

Kate found out one secret at ten, when she saw Ruth slip an envelope under the door of Reverend Charles Hartley's little white church on the east side of Martinsburg, where the town's handful of colored families worshiped, the week a May hailstorm stove in the church roof.

"Is it money?" Kate asked.

"Get in the truck, Katie."

"Why don't you just give it to him?"

Ruth started the engine. She drove halfway home before she answered. "Because then it's about me."

Kate found the second secret at eleven, hunting a quilt in the back of Ruth's closet: a canvas bag, packed and buckled, with a change of clothes, a flashlight and a grey wool coat folded on top. Two hundred dollars lay sewn flat inside the coat lining, the way Katharina had sewn forty dollars into Ruth's coat the night before the train to Hawaii. Kate put everything back exactly as she found it and never asked, and the bag stayed packed in that closet the rest of Ruth's life.

The biggest thing nobody knew went out of the Kerrville post office every December in a Christmas card signed with two initials. Twenty dollars went in 1947, forty in 1950, and sixty in 1952, the winter Hattie Mayfield wrote that the roof of the frame house on the East Side had let the rain into the back bedroom for the third year running.

*Dear Miss Ruth,* Hattie wrote in January 1953. *Your Christmas put a new roof on this house. The man came on a Tuesday and was done by Friday and it has rained twice since and not one drop. Eli's boy Isaiah is six now and reads everything with print on it, the funny papers and the Bible, and he keeps his Uncle Sam's picture by his bed, the one with the airplane. He says he is going to fly too. I tell him Sam would like that. I do not tell him the rest. God bless you for what you do, and for what you keep to yourself.*

Ruth read Hattie's letter through again in the truck outside Lena's Sunday house. In 1945 she had come home calling herself Mara, Naomi's bitter name, and here was Sam's mother thanking God for a roof that did not leak. Ruth saw then that she had been reading the wrong woman in that book. Ruth was not Naomi. Ruth was the daughter-in-law who stayed, *better to thee than seven sons*, and the Lord had kept her in the story the whole time she was refusing to speak to Him. Then she drove home and put the letter in the shoebox with the others, under the photograph, and went out to feed the cattle.

---

Men came courting. Ruth was twenty-six when Kate came to her, still a young woman, with the grey eyes and the long Vellmer bones and a mouth that did not smile easily. Most of them she sent home before the coffee was cold. Dr. Paul Ahrens was different. He had spent the war in the Army Veterinary Corps doctoring mules in Burma, and now he ran a practice out of a converted filling station on the Kerrville highway. He came out to the Vellmer place in March of 1953 at two in the morning because Ruth's best Hereford heifer was trying to calve a breech.

Ruth already had her own arm in to the shoulder when he came through the barn door. Kate, eleven, sat on a bucket holding the flashlight.

Ahrens stripped off his coat. "What's the trouble?"

"Tail first. Both hind legs tucked back under her. I can get a hand on one hock, but I can't raise it."

"Let me in there."

She drew her arm out, wet to the shoulder, and he took her place. He worked for twenty minutes with his cheek pressed against the heifer's flank and his eyes shut, cursing under his breath like a mule skinner. At last one small hoof came, then the other, and Ahrens looped the calving chains above the fetlocks and said, "Now. Pull with her, down and out toward her hocks, when she pushes."

Ruth took one chain and he took the other. The heifer bawled and strained. They pulled. The calf came all at once in a hot rush of fluid and lay in the straw, slick and red, its white face smeared yellow, not moving.

Ahrens shoved a straw up the calf's nostril. The calf did not move. He swung it upside down by the hind legs to drain its lungs and rubbed its ribs hard with a feed sack. The calf still did not move.

Ruth knelt, cleared the mouth with two fingers, put both hands flat on the ribcage, and pushed, once, twice. She knew where the heart was by the feel of the bones. Kate watched her do it.

On the fourth push the calf sneezed, a wet, outraged sound, and shook its head so hard its ears slapped.

Ahrens sat back on his heels in the straw and looked at her.

"Where'd you learn that?"

"Hospital," Ruth said.

"Which one?"

She wiped her arms on the feed sack and did not answer, and Ahrens, who had come back from Burma with things he did not discuss either, did not ask again.

He came out often after that, and not always for the cattle. In June he brought Kate a black Labrador pup from a litter out of a duck dog in Comfort, and Kate named her Schatzi and let her sleep in the iron bed in the cabin against all instructions.

In September, on the porch, with the pup asleep across his boots, Ahrens asked Ruth to marry him.

He laid out the practice, the house in town, the room for Kate. He said every girl who went overseas came home with somebody she never named, and he didn't expect to take that somebody's place. He said he could wait.

Ruth looked at her hands in her lap. Her right thumb went to the bare third finger of her left hand and rubbed it, back and forth, the way she always did when she was thinking.

"You never wear a ring," Ahrens said. "Whoever he was, I figured that meant he never got around to asking."

"He asked me," Ruth said.

He looked at her hand.

"A woman took it off me," she said. "Over there, in a place I won't name. That ring never left me by my choice, Paul. Nobody asked my leave." Her thumb kept moving over the knuckle. "I'm still wearing it. You just can't see it."

He sat there a long time. Then he scratched Schatzi behind the ears, stood and put on his hat.

"I'll still come out for the cattle," he said.

"The cattle will be glad of you."

He came out for the cattle for twenty years. When he married a schoolteacher from Harper in 1957, Ruth and Kate sat in the third row.

---

In January 1956 Ruth carried home in her own mail sack the issue of *Life* dated the thirtieth, along with the Hereford journal and the light bill. She sat down at the kitchen table to read it with her coffee, and she did not get up for an hour.

Five American missionaries had been killed on a sandbar on the Curaray River in the jungle of Ecuador, speared by the Indians they had gone there to reach, the people the magazine called the Aucas. The photographs showed the young men clean-cut and smiling, college boys, Jim Elliot a Wheaton College man from the class of 1949. The little yellow airplane sat on the sandbar with its fabric torn off the frame. The wives waited at the mission station beside a radio that did not call.

Jim Elliot. Nate Saint. Ed McCully. Pete Fleming. Roger Youderian. Ruth went down the names a second time, the way she had once gone down casualty lists in Honolulu. Nate Saint's wristwatch had stopped at twelve minutes past three.

She looked a good while at a picture of one of the widows, Elisabeth Elliot, a tall young woman with a plain, strong face, holding a baby girl. The women at church would have called that face calm. Ruth had seen calm. Calm was the face a woman wore so the guard with the dog would not pick her. In the young widow's face Ruth saw a woman who had been told God would protect them and had found out what that meant, and who had not walked away from Him.

Out in the yard Kate, nearly fourteen and all legs, was teaching Schatzi to fetch a feed sack stuffed with rags, throwing it over the stock tank and laughing when the dog went straight through the water.

Ruth had walked away, out of a requisitioned French church with a swastika flag behind the altar, in the summer of 1944. In Ravensbrück she had watched Betsie ten Boom thank Him for fleas and die, and He had not explained Himself then either. Ruth had decided she owed Him nothing more, since He had told her nothing.

Elisabeth Elliot had not been told anything either. Ruth could see that in the picture.

The next Sunday, the fifth of February, Kate came out of the cabin in her church dress to wait for Aunt Lena to drive her to St. Paul. Ruth came out of the big house after her in a navy-blue dress, a hat and gloves, and got behind the wheel.

Kate stopped with her hand on the door. "Where are you going?"

"Get in the truck, Katie."

"You're going to *church*?"

"Get in the truck."

St. Paul Lutheran was nearly full when they came through the doors. Heads turned. Ruth knew most of the faces: the Lindauers in the fifth pew; the Grieshabers in the sixth, Mrs. Grieshaber among them, who had never found out who paid her grocery account; old Mr. Weber, who had carried her father's grocery bill through the Depression. Here and there a face broke into a smile.

Ruth did not walk up the aisle to the Vellmer pew, the fourth row on the left, where Fritz and Katharina sat with Lena. She turned into the very last pew, on the right, by the door, and sat on the end. Kate stood in the aisle staring at her.

"Go sit with Opa," Ruth said.

"No, ma'am." Kate slid into the pew beside her.

When the congregation stood for the first hymn, Ruth stood with them. She held the hymnal open in her gloved hands. She did not sing. She did not go forward for communion when the rows emptied one by one up the aisle ahead of her, and she did not bow her head during the prayers. She sat in her corner of the last pew with her back straight and her grey eyes on the cross over the altar, the plain brass cross with no banner behind it, and she looked at it the whole hour like a woman across a table from a man who owed her money.

At the door afterward, the pastor shook her hand and said he was glad to see her.

"I didn't come for you," Ruth said, and went down the steps.

She came back the next Sunday, and the one after that, and by spring to the Wednesday supper too. She always took the same seat.

That May Ruth took Kate to the picture show in town to see *The Man Who Never Was*, about the dead man the British floated ashore in Spain in 1943 with forged papers to fool Hitler about Sicily. When the officer on the screen filled the corpse's pockets with love letters and theater stubs, Ruth's hand went to her own pocket, and she saw a Prague tailor in London again, handing her a used French comb and a pencil stub.

In the summer of 1958 Paul Ahrens drove out on a Tuesday with no cattle to see. A black Labrador sat up in the cab of his truck beside him, grey coming in around her muzzle.

"Gus Lindig died," Ahrens said on the porch. "You knew him."

Ruth knew him. Gus ran the one-bay garage at the river crossing and had kept the Willys, and then the pickups, running for eleven years. Gus was a bachelor, and in 1944 he had turned wrenches on the American bombers at Blida, the airfield in the orange groves below Algiers, where a squadron flew agents and guns into southern France by night. The first time Ruth drove in with a bad fuel pump, Gus wiped his hands on a red rag, looked at her a long moment and said one word, "Blida." Ruth said nothing at all. Gus never said the word again, to her or to anybody, and he never once charged her for a part.

"His heart," Ahrens said. "Nobody found him for four days, and she wouldn't let the deputy through the screen door. The deputy had his hand on his holster when I got there." Ahrens looked at the dog in the truck. "Gus had no wife, no children, nobody but a brother in California, and the brother wired the funeral home money and never asked about the dog. Gus told me once, if anything ever happened to him, the mail lady would know what to do with her."

Ruth went down the steps and opened the truck door. The dog got down, smelled her shoes and leaned her whole weight against Ruth's shins.

"Her name is Liesl," Ahrens said.

After the truck was gone, Ruth sat on the porch step with her hand on the black head and thought of every bachelor and widow on her route, of the dogs asleep on their porches, and of the fact that nobody in Pedernales County had ever written down what became of a lone man's dog when the man went first. Liesl rode the route with Ruth for seven years, and she slept across the foot of the iron bed every night Ruth woke in the dark with her heart going like a flushed quail.

In October of 1958 the San Antonio paper reported that Elisabeth Elliot had gone with her little girl and Rachel Saint, the sister of the pilot, to live among the same people who had speared their husband and brother. Ruth cut the article out and put it in the back of her King James.

She kept her seat in the last pew. She did not sing.
