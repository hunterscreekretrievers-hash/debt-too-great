# 3. The Sixth Vellmer

*The Vellmer homeplace below Martinsburg, Pedernales County, Texas. 1924 to 1936.*

Ruth Marie Vellmer came into the world on the ninth of January, 1924, in the north bedroom of the stone house, on a night when a blue norther had the windmill screaming on its tower and the water in the stock tank froze an inch thick.

Katharina Vellmer had been through five births and knew the business better than any doctor in the county. By the time Fritz got back from Martinsburg with Dr. Hahn asleep in the passenger seat of the Ford, the baby was out and yelling. The doctor pronounced her healthy, loud and a girl, and went home.

Five children stood in the bedroom doorway in their nightshirts: Walter, thirteen and nearly as tall as his father; Henry, eleven; Anna, nine; Ernst, seven, whom everybody called Ernie; and Lena, who had turned five that same week. Lena looked a long while at the red, furious creature in her mother's arms.

"Is that one staying?" Lena asked.

"Ja," said Katharina. "That one is staying."

Lena went back to bed and cried until morning.

---

The Vellmer place lay along the Pedernales in a long bend below Martinsburg, two hundred and forty acres of limestone hill, cedar, live oak and bottomland. Fritz Vellmer had bought it in 1906 with stonemason's money and built the house himself two years later, out of rock he cut from the hill behind it. The walls were two feet thick. The porch ran the whole front of the house under a tin roof that rang like a church bell when it hailed.

Every rock in that house had passed through Fritz's hands, and those hands were broad as feed scoops, scarred white across the knuckles. When he held a child's face between them to look at a scrape or a splinter, the child's whole head disappeared.

In the summer of 1925 a traveling photographer came through the county taking pictures of families on their porches for fifty cents a print. Katharina scrubbed all six children and lined them up on the porch in order of height, with Walter at the end holding Ruth on his hip, because Ruth would not stand still for anyone else. In the instant before the shutter clicked, Ruth gave the camera a look of such deep disapproval that the photographer laughed out loud. Katharina mounted the print on grey cardboard and stood it on the parlor mantel, where it stayed for fifty years.

---

On Sundays the Vellmers drove to St. Paul Lutheran in Martinsburg and sat in the fourth pew on the left, the Vellmer pew since Fritz's father's day. The church was built of the same pale limestone as the Vellmer house. Fritz had laid the top two courses of its bell tower himself in 1911, swinging stones up on a gin pole, and he could not walk under it on a Sunday without lifting his eyes.

In the 1920s the hymns at St. Paul were still sung in German, out of black hymnals with the gold rubbed off the spines. Ruth learned "Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott" before she could read a word of it, the way she learned the shape of her mother's hands, by living close to both. She learned that the Lindauers sat in the fifth pew, directly behind the Vellmers, and the Grieshabers in the sixth, and that Mrs. Lindauer sang a quarter tone sharp on every high note and that nobody would ever tell her. She learned that God lived in that building and was strict about shoes.

Her faith came to her the way the stone house came to her. Somebody else had built it. She lived in it.

She was a child who climbed. By five she had gone so high in the live oak by the cattle guard that Katharina, hanging wash, saw her through the leaves and dropped a clean sheet in the dirt. By seven she had been on the barn roof and the windmill platform, thirty feet in the air, where she lay on her stomach on the boards with the wheel clanking over her head and looked east for the ocean, because Walter had told her it was out there.

She could not see the ocean. She could see a hawk turning below her and her mother's white face in the yard with both hands over her mouth.

"*Ruth Marie! Setz dich!* Sit down this instant and don't move until your father comes."

Every grown person in Ruth's childhood said some version of the same thing. Sit still. Mind your sister. Wait your turn. By the time Ruth came along five years behind Lena, everybody in the house had a job and an opinion, and the baby was expected to have neither. She learned that nobody would ever take her seriously, which meant nobody would ever watch her very closely, and she put that away the way a squirrel buries a pecan.

Fritz came in from the field and stood at the foot of the windmill with his hat pushed back off his forehead. He almost never shouted.

"Can you see the ocean?" he called to the platform.

"No, sir."

"Then come back to the ground."

She climbed back to the ground. He caught her off the last rung and held her face between those enormous hands and looked at her with eyes the same grey as her own.

"You will break your neck," he said in German.

"I didn't break it."

"Not today." He let go of her face. "When you go up a thing, you go up it right. Three holds all the time. Two feet and a hand, or two hands and a foot. Never less."

Katharina, behind him, made a noise in her throat like a kettle coming to the boil.

"She will climb either way," Fritz told his wife, in the tone of a man stating the price of feed. "I would rather she did it right."

---

Easter fell early in April in 1931, and on the Saturday night before it Fritz drove the family twenty miles up the river road to Fredericksburg to see the fires.

Every Easter Saturday, people built bonfires on the hills around Fredericksburg, and you could stand in the road and count them burning on the dark ridges like a crown laid on the town. Lena, who was twelve and knew everything, explained the fires to Ruth in the back of the truck.

The town tells the story this way. In 1847, when Fredericksburg was new and its people still lived half in tents, a man named Meusebach rode north to make peace with the Comanche. While he was gone, the Comanche lit signal fires on the hills around the settlement. One mother, to quiet her frightened children, told them the fires belonged to the Easter Rabbit, who was boiling eggs in great kettles on the hilltops and coloring them with wildflowers for the morning. The children slept. In May the treaty was signed, a treaty never once broken, and the fires have burned every Easter since that spring.

"Is it true?" Ruth asked.

"Of course it's true," said Lena.

"Which part?"

Lena opened her mouth and shut it again.

Ruth looked at the orange fires on the hills and thought about a mother in a tent with Comanche fires on every ridge, making up a rabbit so her children would sleep. She thought that mother must have been more frightened than anybody, and that the lie was the bravest thing in the story.

---

Fritz Vellmer did not speak German in town.

Ruth noticed this the way a child notices that a dog favors one leg, long before she knew the reason. At home Fritz spoke German to Katharina. On the square in Martinsburg he spoke only English, slowly, every word set in place like a stone, and if another man spoke to him in German, Fritz answered in English.

When Ruth was nine she asked Walter about it. Walter was home on leave, lying on the cool boards of the porch with his hat over his face, and Ruth sat on his stomach until he answered.

"The war," he said.

"Which war?"

"The first one. The one with the Kaiser. When America went in against Germany, the State Council of Defense in Austin got funny about anybody named Vellmer or Lindauer or Grieshaber. The Council's men went farm to farm through the German counties, seeing who bought Liberty bonds and who did not. Seeing who talked German."

"Did Papa buy bonds?"

"Papa bought bonds. Papa bought more bonds than the banker." Walter was quiet a while. "They came out here anyway. Four of them in a car from Austin, a Council man named Purcell and three of his, not one from this county. I was eight years old. They had a flag with them, a little one on a stick, and they stood Papa on his own porch, right there by that post, and told him to get down on his knees and kiss it to prove he was a loyal American."

Ruth looked at the post, the second one from the steps.

"Did he?"

"He got down on his knees."

"Why?"

"Because Mama was standing in the door with Lena in her belly and four of us behind her skirts, and those four men had a shotgun in the car. That same year a mob in Illinois wrapped a German named Prager in a flag, made him kiss it, and hanged him." Walter put his hat back over his face. "He kissed the flag. They shook his hand and drove back to Austin. He went in the barn and stayed there until dark. That's why Papa doesn't talk German on the square, and if you ever ask him about it I'll throw you in the stock tank."

Ruth never asked her father about it. She understood a thing about him then that she had no words for until much later: that he would carry a two-hundred-pound stone up a ladder without a word, and that the thing he could not carry was the memory of his own knees on those porch boards.

---

Walter was the hero of her childhood, and the Navy was his church.

He had gone into the service in 1928 at eighteen, because of a Fredericksburg boy, a man named Chester Nimitz. Nimitz's grandfather had kept the Steamboat Hotel on Main Street, and Chester had gone off to the Naval Academy at Annapolis and become a Navy officer. To a boy in a cedar-post barn on the Pedernales, Annapolis lay as far off as the ocean, and he had never seen either one.

"A Fredericksburg boy," Walter would say, as if that settled the matter. "Out of a town with no water deep enough to float a canoe, and he's going to be an admiral someday. You watch."

On every leave he told her about the ocean, which was bigger than Texas and the color of a blue jay's back, and about ships that were whole towns made of steel.

"When I'm a chief," he told her once, "I'll get a house in Honolulu with a lemon tree in the yard, and you can come and stay with me and see the ocean."

"Promise?"

"Cross my heart."

She was eight, and she took it as a binding contract. Walter forgot most of what he said on that porch by the time he reached the cattle guard. Ruth forgot none of it.

By the fire Lily held the photograph up to the light and found the tall boy with the baby on his hip. "Walter," she said, to nobody in particular, and on the hearth the post oak broke and settled while the story went two miles up the river.

---

Two miles up the river, past the Vellmer line fence, lay the Harwell place, and on the Harwell place lived the Mayfields, a Black family that had worked Hill Country cattle since freedom. Joseph Mayfield ran the Harwell herd. Hattie Mayfield took in white folks' washing. Their two boys were Sam, born the same winter as Ruth, and Eli, three years younger, who followed his brother everywhere like a heeler pup.

Ruth first saw Sam Mayfield in the summer of 1935, from the top of the Vellmer windmill. A boy her age stood alone on a limestone ledge in the Harwell pasture with his head thrown back and one hand shading his eyes, watching the sky. Over the hills to the southeast came a droning, and then an airplane, a biplane with a blue body and wings yellow as a canary, crawling north through the heat shimmer at a height that made it small as a dragonfly. Walter had told her about those airplanes. The biplanes came up from the flying fields around San Antonio, where the Army taught young men to fly. The boy on the ledge turned with that airplane the whole way across the sky, the way a sunflower turns, until it was gone over the ridge toward Martinsburg.

The next Saturday she rode the mule up the river to the line fence and found him there, sitting on a cedar post with a knife and a block of balsa wood no bigger than a cigar box, carving a wing. Six finished airplanes lay in the grass at the foot of the post, covered in tissue paper and glue, with propellers whittled from tomato-crate slats and wound on rubber bands.

"You built those?" Ruth said.

The boy looked at her, at the mule, at the Vellmer fence between them. "Yes, ma'am."

"Do they fly?"

He wound the rubber band on one, forty turns, and set it loose off the top of the post. The little airplane climbed, faltered, caught the wind off the river and went sailing out over the Vellmer pasture a hundred feet and more, and came down in a patch of firewheels. Ruth slid off the mule and ran to get it. When she came back with the airplane cupped in both hands, he took it through the fence wire without touching her fingers.

"Sam," he said. "Sam Mayfield."

"Ruth Vellmer."

"I know who you are, Miss Ruth. You're the girl on the windmill."

---

By twelve, Ruth had climbed everything on the Vellmer place worth climbing. On a Saturday in October of 1936, while her father and Henry were reroofing the parsonage at St. Paul and nobody was minding the church, she went up the bell tower.

She took the inside ladder to the bell deck, climbed out through the louvers onto the stone ledge forty feet over the churchyard, and from the ledge started up the stonework, finding the joints between the blocks with her fingers and the toes of her shoes, three holds on the wall all the time, exactly as she had been taught.

The stones under her hands were the stones her father had swung up on a gin pole in 1911. She could feel the tooth of his chisel in the face of every one.

She got her elbows over the parapet, pulled herself onto the roof of the tower and stood. The whole town lay below her. She could see the courthouse square, the limestone storefronts along Main with their tin awnings, and away to the south the green line of pecans that meant the Pedernales and home. She could see a man on the parsonage roof straighten up, put his hammer down and turn his head toward the tower, and she knew from the shape of him that he was her father.

The wind pulled at her dress. For one long minute she stood on top of the tallest thing in Martinsburg and was not small.

Then Pastor Thalmann came out of the parsonage below her, and Mrs. Lindauer out of the church hall behind him, both of them shouting at once.

"*Ruth Vellmer! Get down from there!*"

"*Sit down, child! Sit down before you fall!*"

She did not sit. She looked at her father on the parsonage roof, eighty yards away, and her father looked back at her. He did not shout. He lifted one hand with three fingers held up and pumped it three times, the way a man signals a mule driver across a quarry: three holds, three holds, three holds.

She climbed down that tower the way she had climbed it, three holds all the way, while the pastor prayed out loud on the church steps and Mrs. Lindauer prayed louder. When her feet touched the grass, Fritz was there, breathing hard from his run across the churchyard. He took her face in his hands, and his thumbs were shaking. She had never seen his hands shake in her life.

"Three holds," she said into his palms. "The whole way."

"I saw them."

"Are you going to whip me?"

"Your mother is going to whip you." Fritz let go of her face and wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist. Then he turned and looked a long time at the top two courses of the tower, which he had laid twenty-five years earlier.

"Was it good work?" he said in German, very quietly, so that the pastor would not hear him. "Up top. The joints."

"Ja, Papa," said Ruth. "Good work. Every one."

Katharina whipped her that night with a peach switch, six licks across the backs of the legs. Ruth did not cry. At supper she sat on a pillow, and her father, without looking up from his own plate, put the biggest piece of chicken in front of her twice.

By Sunday morning the whole congregation of St. Paul Lutheran knew about Ruth Vellmer and the bell tower. The Grieshaber boys, Arno and Willi, grinned at her from the sixth pew. Otto Lindauer, who was fourteen and big for it and sat right behind her in the fifth, leaned forward during the second hymn and whispered in her ear that she was going to end up a cripple and nobody would ever marry her.

Ruth kept her eyes on the hymnal. She sang every verse of "Ein feste Burg" in her loudest voice, in German, a quarter tone sharp, exactly like Mrs. Lindauer.
