# 4. The Schoolyard

*Martinsburg and Enchanted Rock, Pedernales County, Texas. April and May 1937.*

On Friday afternoons in the spring of 1937, a Black boy named Samuel Mayfield walked the river road from the Harwell place into Martinsburg with a bundle of white laundry on his head.

He carried the bundle on a folded towel, one hand up to steady it. Inside, wrapped in a bedsheet and tied at the corners, were Mrs. Lindauer's table linens, starched stiff as shingles, and six of Mr. Lindauer's white Sunday shirts folded around squares of cardboard. His mother, Hattie, took in washing for four white families in Martinsburg, and the Lindauer money kept the Mayfields in flour and coal oil in the lean months when cattle prices fell and ranch wages fell with them.

Sam was thirteen, all elbows and shins, with a long solemn face that made strangers think he was sullen, and in the bib pocket of his overalls he carried a balsa wing he was sanding, so that his hands always had something to do on the road. He had walked that road every Friday since he was nine. He knew to walk the far shoulder and step into the grass when a car came. He knew to keep his eyes on the ground on the square, to go to the Lindauers' back door and never the front, and to wait on the back step, the bundle at his feet, until the screen door swung open on its spring.

The road came into town past the schoolhouse, where the schoolyard was bare packed dirt, and behind the yard stood the old cotton gin, sheet iron gone brown with rust, where a boy could stand and not be seen from the school windows.

Sam always timed his walk to pass after the white children had scattered home. On the sixteenth of April a calf got through the fence on the Harwell place, and he chased it half an hour in the creek bottom. He came past the schoolhouse gate just as the bell rang.

---

Ruth Vellmer was the last one out the door, because Miss Hartung had kept her to wash the blackboards, her punishment for the bell tower since October. She came down the schoolhouse steps with chalk dust to her elbows and her books on a strap, and she heard them before she saw them: boys' voices behind the gin, high and quick, the sound a pack of dogs makes when it has something cornered under a porch.

She knew the voices. She had heard them every Sunday of her life from the pews behind her.

Otto Lindauer was fourteen and the biggest boy in school. For as long as Ruth could remember, Otto had kicked the back of the Vellmer pew during the sermon, gently, steadily, the way a bored horse knocks a hoof against its stall. Arno and Willi Grieshaber sat in the sixth pew beside their mother, who wore a hat with a bird on it. Otto and Arno had both recited Luther's Small Catechism at the altar rail the spring before, in white shirts, every word, while their mothers wept.

Ruth went around the side of the gin.

The three boys had Sam against the sheet iron wall. Otto stood square in front of him, and the Grieshaber boys had the sides. The first thing Ruth saw was Sam's hand, still raised to hold the bundle on his head, as if the laundry were what mattered.

Otto said the word.

Ruth had heard Otto say that word once before, at the feed store, about a man standing right there with a sack of oats on his shoulder, and the man behind the counter had run Otto out of the store for it. Now she watched the word go into Sam Mayfield's face like a nail into soft wood. His face did not move. Only his eyes changed. Something behind them stepped back and shut, the way a door shuts in a house where somebody is hiding.

Otto said it again, louder, so the Grieshabers would laugh. The Grieshaber boys laughed.

"Put that bundle in the dirt," Otto said.

"That's your mama's tablecloths," Sam said. His voice was quiet and very even.

"I know they're my mama's. Put them in the dirt."

"No, sir."

"He called you sir, Otto," said Arno, and laughed again.

Otto reached up and knocked the bundle off Sam's head.

The bundle burst at one corner when it hit, and Mrs. Lindauer's linens and Mr. Lindauer's white shirts slid out across the dirt. Willi Grieshaber ground his heel into a shirt. Sam stood with his back to the sheet iron and his hands open at his sides, and he did not make a fist.

Otto shoved him. Sam's head hit the iron with a sound like a dropped bucket. Otto shoved him again.

"Hit me," Otto said. "Hit me, why don't you."

Sam's hands stayed at his sides.

Ruth Vellmer weighed eighty-four pounds. She dropped her books where she stood, walked across that schoolyard and put herself between Otto Lindauer and Sam Mayfield, her back to Sam and her face about level with the second button of Otto's shirt.

"Leave him alone," she said.

Otto was too surprised to be angry yet. "Get out of the way, Ruth."

"No."

"This ain't any of your business."

"You're standing on his laundry. Pick those shirts off the ground, you big dumb ox."

Willi Grieshaber laughed. Otto could stand a good deal, but he could not stand being laughed at by a Grieshaber. His face went red from the collar to the hairline. He put a hand flat on Ruth's chest and pushed, and she did not move, because her father had built her feet the way he built a wall, low and wide. So Otto hit her.

He hit her with his right fist in the middle of her face. She heard the bone in her nose go, a small wet crack inside her own head like a green stick breaking under a boot. White light went off behind her eyes. She took one step backward into Sam, and Sam's hands came up for the first time and caught her by both shoulders and held her.

The blood came all at once, out of both nostrils, over her mouth, off her chin and down the front of her blue gingham dress, and it dripped onto Mrs. Lindauer's white tablecloth lying open on the ground at her feet. The drops hit the starched linen and spread, round and red, like coins.

Ruth wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist. Her eyes were watering so badly that Otto was only a big blur against the sky.

"Pick up the shirts, Otto," she said. Her voice came out thick and strange.

Otto looked at the blood on his mother's tablecloth. Then he wheeled and walked off fast across the schoolyard, and the Grieshaber boys went after him, and none of the three turned his head.

---

Sam knelt in the dirt behind the gin and gathered every tablecloth and every shirt, one at a time, shaking each one and folding it on his knee. Ruth stood with her head tipped back against the sheet iron. The blood ran down the back of her throat and tasted of rust, like the first water out of the stock-tank pipe.

"Don't tip your head back that way," Sam said, without raising his eyes. "Lean forward. Pinch it. My mama says so."

She leaned forward and pinched, and the pain went up into her eyes.

Sam picked up the bloodied tablecloth last and looked at the red coins on the white linen the way a man looks at a bill he cannot pay. Then he folded the stain inside and tied the bundle shut.

"Otto will say I did it," he said.

"Did what?"

"Bloodied you." He stood with the bundle under his arm, still not looking at her face. "Otto will tell it that a colored boy hit a white girl behind the gin. You know what happens then?"

Ruth had no idea. Years later she learned the answer. On that April afternoon she said the only thing that came to her.

"I'll tell them Otto did it."

Sam raised his eyes to her then and looked at her the way a grown man looks at a child who has said something about money or God.

"Miss Ruth," he said, "who do you think they're going to believe, Otto Lindauer or a colored boy?"

He walked her as far as the corner of the schoolyard fence, where the road turned toward the square, and there he stopped. A Black boy walking a white girl with a bloody face down any Main Street in Texas in 1937 was walking toward a rope, and both of them knew it. He went on toward the Lindauers' back door with the bundle on his head. Ruth walked the four miles home alone, the front of her dress gone stiff and brown.

---

That night Fritz set the nose at the kitchen table.

Katharina held the lamp. Lena held Ruth's hands. Fritz washed with lye soap at the basin and stood over his daughter looking at her face the way he looked at a stone before he struck it. Then he put a thumb on either side of the bridge of her nose and told her in German to breathe out, and when she breathed out he pushed.

She screamed. The scream hit the stone walls of that kitchen and came straight back at her. Lena started crying. Fritz did not stop until the bone moved under his thumbs with a small grating click and sat where he wanted it.

"There," he said. "Straight."

He taped a splint of cedar lath across the bridge with adhesive plaster from the barn. Then he sat down across the table from her and asked who had done it.

She told him all of it, the gin, the word, the shirts in the dirt, the fist.

Fritz listened without a word. When she finished he looked at the lamp a long time. Ruth waited for him to stand and reach for his hat. She waited for him to say he would drive to the Lindauer place in the morning, or do anything at all with those broad scarred hands.

"Go to bed," he said.

---

On Sunday the Vellmers sat in the fourth pew, the Lindauers in the fifth, the Grieshabers in the sixth.

Ruth sat with a cedar splint taped across her face and both eyes gone black as a raccoon's. When the congregation rose for the first hymn, Otto stood directly behind her, close enough that she could smell the bay rum in his hair.

He did not kick her pew that morning. Otto Lindauer never offered her any more than that.

During the announcements Mrs. Lindauer leaned forward and touched Ruth's shoulder with one gloved hand. "Poor lamb," she said. "Otto says you took a fall on the schoolhouse steps."

Ruth turned in the pew and looked at her. She looked at Mr. Lindauer in one of the white shirts Hattie Mayfield had ironed and Sam had carried on his head. Then she looked to the end of her own pew, where her father sat with his hands folded on his knees and his eyes on the altar.

Fritz never turned his head. He never said one word to Mr. Lindauer about it, that day or any day in Ruth's hearing. After the service she watched the two men shake hands on the steps, the way they had shaken hands every Sunday for twenty years.

Pastor Thalmann preached that morning on the Good Samaritan. He was a kind man who had been told Ruth Vellmer had fallen on the schoolhouse steps, and he believed it, the way he believed every child in his congregation.

When they stood for "Ein feste Burg," Ruth held the hymnal open and kept her mouth shut through all four verses. Nobody noticed, because the organ was loud and Mrs. Lindauer was louder. God lived in that building and was strict about shoes, and He had nothing to say about a girl with a broken nose. Ruth heard His silence. She kept it a long time.

---

Three weeks later Fritz took a stone job north of Martinsburg, building a chimney for a rancher in the granite country below Enchanted Rock. He needed a hand to haul rock. He drove up the river to the Mayfield house on the Harwell place on a Tuesday evening, stood in the yard with his hat in his hand and asked Joseph Mayfield whether Mr. Harwell could spare him four days for wages, and whether his boy would like to come along and carry water.

Ruth never learned what the two men said in that yard. She knew only that her father came home and told her to pack a bedroll. She was old enough by then to understand that this was the only apology Fritz Vellmer knew how to make: with his hands, out under the open sky, where nobody had to say a word.

On the second night they camped in a cedar flat at the foot of the rock. Enchanted Rock rose out of the hills like the back of some great beast asleep under the ground, a bald dome of pink granite more than four hundred feet high. At sundown the whole dome turned the color of the inside of a peach.

In the night the rock began to talk.

Ruth woke to a long low groan off the dome above them, then a crack like a rifle shot far off, then a slow, deep creaking, like the timbers of a ship. Across the dead fire Sam was sitting straight up in his blanket with his eyes wide and white.

"Papa," Ruth whispered.

"I hear it." Fritz lay on his back with his hat over his face.

"The Indians said spirits lived on top," said Joseph Mayfield from the far side of the fire, low and amused. "Ghost fires up there at night."

"That groaning is the stone," said Fritz, from under his hat. "The sun heats it all day. At night it cools, and the skin of it shrinks faster than the heart, and it moves. Every stone talks when it moves. Go to sleep. It will talk until morning."

The rock talked until morning. Ruth lay awake listening, and she thought it the loneliest sound she had ever heard, like something enormous trying to get comfortable in a bed too small for it.

---

She and Sam climbed it at first light, before the men woke.

The granite was cold and gritty as sandpaper, and in the hollows lay round pools of rainwater with fairy shrimp darting in the green. Ruth went on all fours with her splinted nose a foot from the stone. Three holds, three holds. Sam went beside her in long easy strides, his bare feet slapping the rock. At the lip of one pool Sam squatted and watched the shrimp a long minute. "How does a shrimp get to the top of a granite mountain?" he asked. Ruth had no answer.

On top the wind blew steady out of the south. The hills rolled off in every direction in the grey light, live oak and cedar and pink granite, and the sun came out of the east red and lit the rock under their feet until it glowed.

The two of them sat on the crown of the dome with their knees drawn to their chins.

"Why didn't you hit him?" Ruth said at last. "You're as big as Otto."

Sam watched the sunrise. "You know about Mr. Lacy's boy? Down in the Brazos bottoms, where my mama's people live?"

"No."

"He hit a white boy. Over a ball game. Fourteen years old." Sam picked up a chip of granite and turned it in his long fingers. "Men came out to his daddy's place that night. Six of them. They took his daddy out to the barn." He set the chip on the rock very carefully, the way his mother set a hot iron on its stand. "They didn't take the boy. They took his daddy. You understand me? You hit a white boy, they don't come for you. They come for your daddy."

Ruth said nothing for a while. Far below, the breakfast smoke rose thin and blue out of the cedar flat, and beside it stood the small figure of her father and the taller figure of Joseph Mayfield.

"So I stood there," Sam said. "Stood there and let him."

"I'm sorry."

"You got your nose broke." He looked at her for the first time that morning, at the splint and the black eyes, and one corner of his long mouth lifted, barely. "You look terrible, Miss Ruth."

"Don't call me Miss Ruth. I'm thirteen."

"Can't call you anything else. Not in town."

"We're not in town."

He thought about that. "Ruth," he said, trying the name.

"Sam."

Far off to the south, small as a gnat against the red, an airplane was creeping north out of the San Antonio country, and Sam watched it the way he always watched them, his whole face lifted.

"Up there," he said, "nobody can catch you. Nobody can corner you behind a cotton gin. Nobody can come for your daddy." He did not take his eyes off the airplane. "One day I'm going to fly one of those, Ruth. Army or no Army. You watch."

"I'll watch," she said, and she meant it as a promise, and she kept it for the next seventy-six years.

---

The fire had gone to coals. Another split of oak went across them and hissed, and for a minute the old man in the cowhide chair did not trust his voice.

Lily sat very still on the quilt with the photograph of the six Vellmer children in her lap. Ben was half asleep with his head on her knee. Marcus's phone lay face down on the hearthstone, and he had not touched it since the cotton gin. Lily opened her mouth to ask the question, and her grandfather spoke first.

Two fingers went to the top button of the grey cardigan. The button was already fastened.

"That boy was my uncle."
