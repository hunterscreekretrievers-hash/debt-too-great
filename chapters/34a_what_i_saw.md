# 35. What I Saw

*Christmas morning, 2026, the hour before light. The cabin on the Vellmer homeplace.*

The old man picks up the boy's phone and turns the screen to the lamp. The one bar has brought the news. Marcus leans over his grandfather's arm and reads the headline the way a boy reads a box score.

"Soldiers with rifles at the Christmas markets in France."

"Every December since 2015," the old man says. "Lily, hand me those pages, sugar."

Under the page Miss Ruth headed FOR WHOEVER PAID lies another, typed on the same Royal and titled in the same capitals, THEY TOLD US. Lily holds it to the phone's light and reads it aloud.

> *In Mein Kampf Hitler wrote that twelve or fifteen thousand "Hebrew corrupters of the nation" ought to have been held under poison gas in the first war. In January of 1942 Hitler promised a crowd at the Sportpalast "the complete annihilation of the Jews." The world chose not to believe him until the GIs walked into Dachau and counted the dead in the boxcars.*
>
> *The men of the new war have been as plain. The crowds in Tehran chanted "Death to America" in 1979, and they chant it yet. Article Seven of the Hamas charter of 1988 has the stones and the trees cry out, "there is a Jew behind me, come and kill him." Bin Laden's fatwa of February 1998 made killing Americans and their allies, "civilians and military," what he called "an individual duty for every Muslim."*
>
> *I heard the same promise in Arabic from the back of a German truck in 1944.*

Lily lowers the page. "She stopped in 2013."

"The promises did not stop, sugar, and neither did I."

The old man sets the page on the quilt and squares its edges with his fingertips.

"On Christmas Eve, on her own front porch, a man from a tire shop shot the woman who paid for my schooling, and a deputy had that man's name in his hand two weeks before and never ran it. A medic who did twenty years of intelligence work does not grieve the way other men grieve. The week after the funeral I cleared the junk off the workbench in my garage on Hackberry Street and bought a four-drawer steel file cabinet at the Army surplus on Frio City Road. I lettered the drawers in block capitals the way the Army taught me: THE MEN. THE MONEY. THE MEDICINE. THE PLAN. Your grandmother thought I was out there doing the taxes. For twelve years I read every night the way I once read the hospital files of other governments, with a pencil in my hand, hunting the secret in the chart. I went through the trial exhibits out of Dallas, the Congressional Record, the court rulings, and the books that never got a newspaper review. Most nights I read until two in the morning with Uncle Sam's tag under my shirt. Some men take up golf."

Ruthie has her hand over her mouth. "That's what you were doing in the garage."

"That is what I was doing in the garage. The first drawer is THE MEN, and they kept writing their promises after Miss Ruth quit typing. In July of 2014 the Islamic State painted a letter on the Christian houses in Mosul. *Nun*, for *Nasrani*. Christian. The families got a deadline: convert, pay the tax or face the sword. That September their spokesman told people, 'If you can kill a disbelieving American or European,' then 'kill him in any manner or way however it may be.' Within weeks of October 7, 2023, a Hamas official went on Lebanese television and said the massacre was 'just the first time, and there will be a second, a third, a fourth.' Those men have kept every promise within reach. Now type Rotherham, son."

Marcus types the name, and the boy's voice gets smaller as he reads. In Rotherham, England, gangs of men, most of Pakistani heritage, raped and trafficked at least fourteen hundred children from 1997 to 2013. Professor Alexis Jay's inquiry found the staff afraid to name the men's origins "for fear of being thought racist."

"The word they pick for you kept the police and the council quiet for sixteen years," the old man says. "Read the rest of the list, son. Read it the way the paper printed it, one day at a time."

So Marcus reads the list. Charlie Hebdo, January of 2015, twelve dead over a cartoon. That May two men drove from Phoenix to Garland, Texas, to shoot up a Muhammad cartoon contest, and Garland police killed them both in the parking lot, and the Islamic State hailed them as "soldiers of the Caliphate."

"That one was four hours up the road from this fire," Ruthie says from the hearth.

The Bataclan that November, ninety dead in one concert hall and a hundred and thirty across Paris. New Year's Eve in Cologne, where mobs of men, most of them from North Africa, swarmed the women beneath the cathedral, and the police called the night "mostly peaceful" with more than five hundred sexual-offense complaints still to come. Pulse, in Orlando, June of 2016: forty-nine dead, and the gunman swore himself to the Islamic State on a 911 call in the middle of the killing. Nice on Bastille Day of 2016, a truck driven through families walking home from the fireworks, eighty-six dead, fourteen of them children.

Ruthie reaches off the hearth and closes her hand around Ben's bare foot where it pokes out from under the quilt.

Marcus keeps reading down the list. Five dead at a Christmas market in Strasbourg in 2018. Marcus scrolls back up to the headline about the soldiers at the Christmas markets and does not read it a second time. Samuel Paty, a history teacher, beheaded outside Paris in October of 2020 for showing his class a cartoon. On October 7, 2023, Hamas butchered about twelve hundred people in Israel and dragged 251 hostages into Gaza, and since that day synagogues have burned on five continents. On Yom Kippur of 2025 two worshipers died when a man attacked a Manchester synagogue and rang 999 in the middle of the killing to swear himself to the Islamic State.

Marcus stops. He reads the last one to himself first, the way a boy checks a thing before he says it in front of his mother. "On the first night of Hanukkah last December, a father and son killed fifteen people on Bondi Beach."

Lily pulls the quilt up over Ben's shoulder without looking down, and her eyes do not leave her grandfather's face.

"Daddy," Ruthie says, "you sound like the lady across from Miss Ruth's house."

"I ought to sound like her. That woman has filled half a drawer of mine, and she is the reason I could stand to read the rest of it."

---

"The lady bought Emil Klier's house across Elm Street in 2019, after Emil passed. The first week she lived there she walked down to the square and sat on the bus bench beside the bronze of Miss Ruth, the way a woman sits waiting on a bus, and I came up the walk with a coffee can of zinnias on my way to the grave and found her there. She will tell you her name herself at church this morning, if she wants you to have it. She lets me tell her stories.

"She was born in a Christian village in the south of Lebanon, a few miles from the Israeli fence. In 1976, when she was ten, the Muslim militias pushed south, and a shell came through the roof of her house and buried her in the stone of her own bedroom. Her family lived seven years in a bomb shelter dug under their garden, with no running water and no window, and when the bread ran out her mother boiled dandelions and grass from the yard. The men with the guns on the hill were the men who had eaten at her mother's table. When her mother was hit, Israeli soldiers carried her across the border to a field hospital, and the girl sat by the cot and watched the same Jewish doctors work on a wounded militiaman in the next bed, the same way, with the same hands. She told me that was the night she learned which side of that war loved life.

"She came to Houston at sixteen with one suitcase and learned her English off the evening news. Then she went to work in the evening news. She carried a microphone into Afghanistan with the Special Forces the winter after the towers fell, and into Baghdad, and back into Kabul again and again, past keeping count. In Cairo, the night the president fell in 2011, a crowd of men in the square tore her away from her crew, and what they did to her for the next half hour she has told me in one sentence. I will not tell you children in any sentence at all. She went back to work three weeks later. Some years after that she said out loud, on a stage, that the networks had all gone one way, and the work dried up the way water leaves a stock tank in August.

"She keeps a folder the way Miss Ruth kept hers. The first time she opened it for me on that bench, she said, 'Your Miss Ruth heard them in Arabic from a German truck. I heard them in my own village. Nobody who grew up in that shelter needs a declaration from Cairo to spell out the law for her.' Then she showed me the declaration from Cairo anyway."

Ruthie sets her cup down on the hearth.

"Islam is not a religion the way St. Paul on the square is a religion, children. Islam is an ideology, a law and a government and an army bound into one book, and the book means to conquer its enemies, not to win them. Ask the women who live under it. The lady across Elm Street watched the girls in the villages around hers put on the veil one year and lose their names the next.

"In September of 2022 the morality police in Tehran arrested a girl of twenty-two named Mahsa Amini for the way her headscarf sat on her hair, and three days later she died in their custody. The lady across Elm Street keeps that girl's photograph at the front of her folder, and she turned it to me on the bench without a word. That same year the Taliban shut every girls' school in Afghanistan past the sixth grade, and by Christmas the universities too."

Lily counts the grades on her fingers, and her lips stop at six.

"The ideology wears the word religion like a press badge, because in this country a press badge gets a man past every guard at the gate. The First Amendment protects a church, so the ideology calls itself a church and goes through the gate smiling, and the lawyers bill by the hour to keep the gate open. Then the brothers go to work on the institutions one office at a time, the way the memorandum laid out the job.

"I know the look of a forward post. Around Cu Chi in 1969 we built firebases across that flat country, a few acres of sandbags and wire, each one in range of the next, until the whole province was covered by our guns. A mosque is a firebase. The man who runs Turkey today said so himself. In December of 1997 Recep Tayyip Erdoğan stood up in Siirt and recited the lines, 'The mosques are our barracks, the domes our helmets, the minarets our bayonets and the faithful our soldiers,' and a Turkish court sent him to prison for it and banned him from politics for life. In 2003 Erdoğan was prime minister.

"The mosque survey counted about twelve hundred mosques in this country in 2000. By 2010 the count stood at two thousand one hundred and six, and by 2020 at two thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine. That number grew thirty-one percent in ten years. At Cu Chi a count like that meant a whole province under the guns. In February of 2024 the East Plano Islamic Center announced a city of its own on four hundred and two acres of Collin and Hunt counties, a thousand homes around a mosque, a school and a college, and buyers reserved more than five hundred lots within days of the video. In February of 2025 the Governor of Texas wrote, 'Sharia law is not allowed in Texas,' and in March he set the state's investigators on the project. The developers changed the name on the sign this January and kept the plans on the table.

"The same ideology takes ground at the grocery store too. Halal meat is butchered with a prayer to Allah said over every animal, *Bismillah, Allahu akbar*, as the knife goes in, and the packages sit in the cooler at the H-E-B with a little seal in the corner, unread. Your grandmother read every one of those seals. I buy my beef, my lamb and my goat from Kurt Hoerster's daddy, who runs cattle on the river road below this cabin, and I watch him pack the ice chest myself. No meat goes on my table with a blessing to Allah on the butcher's knife.

"The declaration from Cairo is the paper the lady across Elm Street showed me on the bench. In August of 1990 the Organisation of the Islamic Conference adopted the Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam and made every right in it 'subject to the Islamic Shari'ah.' She had the declaration on a page in her folder with her own pencil line under those five words, and she held her finger on the five words until I read them twice. In 2003, ruling against Turkey's Refah Party, the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights held that 'sharia is incompatible with the fundamental principles of democracy.'"

"Incompatible," Lily says.

"Incompatible, sugar. Islam is not compatible with Western values in any shape or form, and the Muslim nations put that in writing themselves. Leave Islam in Iran, Saudi Arabia, Afghanistan, Qatar or Yemen, and the law can put you to death. In 2010 a Pakistani court sentenced Asia Bibi, a Christian farmhand, to death for blasphemy after a quarrel with Muslim women over a cup of water in a field. When Salmaan Taseer, the governor of Punjab, spoke up for her, his own bodyguard shot him twenty-eight times, and more than three hundred lawyers offered to defend the killer for free."

"Three hundred lawyers," Lily says.

"Three hundred of them, working for free. Ruth laid out the record as far as it ran in 1979, at the Wednesday supper, and her own brother stood up and talked her down with Scripture. Seven weeks later she laid it out again in the Martinsburg paper as Lucie, and she kept at it for thirty-four years. Allen West tells the history plain. His books sit on the shelf over my workbench, under the folded flag the Army handed me the day I retired. West wore the uniform twenty-two years, a Black officer out of Atlanta who left the Army a lieutenant colonel, then served Florida in Congress and chaired the Republican Party of Texas, and the papers called him every name they later called Miss Ruth. On the sixth of March, 2020, the anniversary of the day the Alamo fell, West spoke at the Cenotaph on Alamo Plaza against the plan to move it, and I stood at the back of the crowd in my VFW cap. When he finished I shook his hand and told him a white widow in the Hill Country had been writing the same warning in her county paper since 1980. He held my hand a while and listened, his eyes never leaving mine. The jihadists have told us what they intend, out loud, for forty-seven years. Believe them, the way Ruth learned to believe *Mein Kampf*.

"On Tuesday mornings that lady and I take the front booth at the Mustang Grill with a third old fool, a man who grew up in a refugee camp outside Bethlehem. He threw his first rock at an Israeli jeep when he was nine and loved the sound of the glass. In his school the arithmetic problems counted dead Jews. He came to Texas to study engineering, married a Baptist girl from Bandera, and opened her Bible one night to prove her wrong, and Jesus met him on the page before he could close it. When Miss Ruth's line about the stones and the trees came up at our table, he recited the words in Arabic, every one. He learned that hadith in the third grade, he said, before he could spell his own father's name."

Marcus has kept the phone. He has typed something and holds the screen to his grandfather without a word.

"Read it, son."

"The companies," Marcus says. "In 2020 an Australian institute traced eighty thousand Uyghurs shipped out of Xinjiang into factories supplying eighty-three brands. A United Nations mission called Facebook 'a useful instrument for those seeking to spread hate' in Myanmar while the army drove out the Rohingya. In 2018 Google was building China a search engine that buried human rights, and the code name was Dragonfly."

The little blue Facebook *f* glows in the corner of his screen. Marcus turns his own phone over and reads the maker's name on the back.

Lily lifts the page headed FOR WHOEVER PAID off the quilt and holds it beside the phone. Her grandfather nods at Ruth's typing.

"Miss Ruth always held that they would do it again for whoever paid."
