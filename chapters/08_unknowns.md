# 8. Unknowns

*Honolulu and Pearl Harbor, December 1941 to June 1942*

Ruth went up the hill to Kaimuki on Monday in the back of a bread truck, because the buses were not running. The driver told her the governor had handed the islands to the Army on Sunday afternoon, courts and all, and the word for it was martial law.

She had not slept. The oil was still in the creases of her knuckles and under her nails, and her hair smelled of smoke. Soldiers stood at the street corners in steel helmets. A car went by with its headlights painted blue. The truck climbed through McCully, where a whole block of stores and frame houses had burned to the slabs, and the driver said the Jap bombs did it.

The cottage door hung open. Margaret sat on the porch daybed in the same housedress she had worn Saturday night, with her hands on her belly and her hair uncombed. Mrs. Tanaka sat beside her holding a cup of tea Margaret had not touched.

Margaret looked at Ruth's face and read everything on it before Ruth reached the steps.

"Say it," Margaret said.

"The *Oklahoma* rolled. I saw it from the hospital. They're cutting men out of the bottom. Nobody knows any names yet."

"Say it."

"I don't know anything, Margaret. That's the truth. Nobody knows a single name."

Margaret nodded as if Ruth had read her the weather off the radio. She stood up off the daybed, walked into the kitchen, and began to scrub the sink, which was already clean. Mrs. Tanaka set the cup on the floor and folded her hands in her lap.

"My husband says they will put us in camps," Mrs. Tanaka said quietly, in careful English. "My son is American. He is in the Army, at Schofield." She lifted her eyes to Ruth. "Will they come for us?"

"I can't tell you that," Ruth said. She thought of her father on the porch in 1918, and four men in the yard asking him to prove something no man can prove, and she sat down beside Mrs. Tanaka, and the two of them listened to the brush in the kitchen until it stopped.

---

The telegram came to the cottage before Christmas, and a second one came to the Vellmer place on the Pedernales, carried out from Martinsburg by the depot agent in his own car. Both said the same thing: *Missing following action in the performance of his duty and in the service of his country.*

Missing, Ruth learned, was a word with walls around it and no roof. Missing meant Walter's name stood on the list of men who belonged to the *Oklahoma* and was absent from the list of men who had answered muster since the seventh.

She went to the casualty office at the Navy Yard three times in December and stood in line with wives and mothers. A yeoman with grey half-moons under his eyes told her the same thing three times.

"The ship's on her back in forty feet of water, miss. Nobody goes inside her until she's raised. Could be a year. Could be more." **[VERIFY: depth of water where the Oklahoma lay and when salvage and righting took place (1943)]** He looked at her card. "I'm sorry, miss. I mean that. We've got four hundred men out of that ship and no trace of them."

Katharina's letter came in January, in German, on the onionskin paper she saved for Christmas. Ruth read it on the porch of the barracks where the aides now slept, four girls to a room.

*Your father went out to the barn when the man from the depot left,* her mother wrote. *He did not come in for supper. When I went out with the lantern he was setting stone on the wellhouse in the dark, by feel. He has not spoken Walter's name since that day. He goes to church. He sits. He does not sing. You know your father has always sung.*

*Ruth, you are to come home. Bring Margaret and the baby when it can travel. Your father says so too, though he will not write it.*

Ruth folded the letter into the pocket of her apron and went back on the ward. She did not answer for a month.

---

On the last day of 1941 a new admiral took command of the Pacific Fleet.

He came in by flying boat on Christmas Day, the corpsmen said, and on the thirty-first he took command standing on the deck of a submarine, because the battleships he would have stood on were lying in the mud. **[VERIFY: change-of-command ceremony aboard the submarine USS *Grayling*, December 31, 1941]**

Ruth heard the name from a corpsman while changing the dressing on a burned sailor. She stopped with the gauze in her hand.

"Nimitz?"

"Chester Nimitz. Out of Washington." The corpsman shrugged. "You know him?"

"My brother knows him," Ruth said, and then tried to correct the tense, and could not get the correction out of her mouth, and went on with the gauze.

Inside his locker Walter had taped a newspaper picture of the admiral, the grandson of the man who built the Steamboat Hotel on Main Street in Fredericksburg, the hotel with its front like the bow of a ship. The locker lay upside down at the bottom of the harbor now, with the picture inside it, and Walter somewhere near it in the dark.

That night Ruth knelt beside her bunk in the barracks out of seventeen years of habit and found no words in her at all. She stayed on the board floor until her knees ached, her mind empty and cold. Then she got into bed.

She did not pray again for a long time. She never chose the silence. She had no words left to send, and nothing came back across the dark.

> *Wednesday, December 31, 1941*
>
> *Admiral Nimitz has the fleet. Walter, you would have stood on your head.*
>
> *The chaplain came through the ward and asked would I like to pray with him. I said I had prayed enough for one month. He said that was not possible. I said, Then you come down to the lawn and count them, Chaplain. He went on to the next bed. Not sorry.*

---

Clarence Banks's eyes came back in the third week of December. The gauze came off, and he saw light and then shapes and then Ruth's face over him, and the first words he said with his eyes open were, "You look younger than your voice, ma'am." His hands healed tight, the skin on the backs grown in shiny and pink-brown, and Ruth worked them every day, one joint at a time, while he swore under his breath and apologized for swearing.

On the January morning he left the ward for a new ship, he sat on the edge of his cot in a fresh set of whites while she checked his fingers one last time.

"Dorie's alive," he said. "Boy off the *West Virginia* came by the ward and told me. Says an officer wrote Dorie up for something."

"I hope so."

"They won't give him a thing," Clarence said. "You watch the papers."

---

Margaret's labor began in the blackout on the night of February 10, three weeks ahead of the doctor's date, and nobody could get her to a hospital.

The cottage had no telephone. When the first pain doubled Margaret over the kitchen table, Ruth sent Mrs. Tanaka's youngest boy running down the hill to the Army post on Waialae for a doctor, and lit one kerosene lamp in the back bedroom with a blanket over the window.

The boy came back three hours later. The doctor never came.

Mrs. Tanaka came. She came up the dark street in her house slippers with clean rags and a pair of scissors she had boiled on her own stove. She had borne four children. Ruth had pulled calves.

"You hold her hand," Mrs. Tanaka said. "I will do the rest. When I tell you, you push on her here."

Ruth held Margaret's hand for six hours. Margaret broke two of the small bones in Ruth's right hand before morning, though neither of them knew it until later; the knuckles swelled up blue, and Ruth carried them crooked the rest of her life, the same way she carried the bump in her nose. Margaret did not scream. She bit a rolled washcloth and said Walter's name into it, *Walter, Walter, Walter*, as if she were calling him in from the yard for supper.

The baby came at a quarter to five in the morning, blue and slick and silent, the cord around her neck. Mrs. Tanaka slipped the cord off with two fingers, turned the baby on her forearm and slapped her back twice, hard. The baby coughed, and then she screamed, and kept screaming, red-faced, furious, alive.

"Girl," Mrs. Tanaka said. "Strong girl."

Margaret held out her arms. Ruth laid the baby in them. The blanket over the window had gone grey at the edges with first light, and Margaret looked down at her daughter, and her face did something Ruth had not seen it do since the sixth of December.

"Katharina," Margaret said. "For your mother. Walter wanted it. We'll call her Kate."

> *Wednesday, February 11, 1942*
>
> *Katharina Margaret Vellmer. Born 4:45 in the morning in the back bedroom, by a coal-oil lamp, in a blackout, on an island at war. Six pounds on the kitchen scale in a dish towel. She has Walter's chin.*
>
> *Mrs. Tanaka delivered her. A Japanese woman delivered Walter's daughter while the radio said Japanese spies in Hawaii guided the planes. That fact goes in this book where nobody can tear it loose.*
>
> *My hand is swollen up like a toad. Miss Draper says two bones. The left hand still carries a bedpan.*
>
> *Held Kate for an hour while Margaret slept. Did not pray over her. Too afraid. Everyone I prayed over in December died. The Iowa boy looked at the door like somebody had come for him.*

Ten days after Kate was born, a court in Honolulu sentenced the Pearl Harbor spy to be shot, and the spy was a German named Otto Kuehn. Years later Ruth read the signals the German had worked out for the Japanese: a light in a window of his beach house at Lanikai, a star on the sail of a small boat, a want ad for a Chinese rug. The Japanese consul cabled those signals to Tokyo on the third of December, and the Navy translated the cable on the eleventh, four days after the *Oklahoma* rolled over with Walter inside her.

---

Lena's envelope came the first week of April, fat and soft from the long trip and slit along one end by a censor and taped shut again. Behind Lena's page of news about the price of feed and Anna's new baby lay two sheets of tablet paper in a tall square pencil hand.

Ruth read them on the seawall at the edge of the hospital lawn. Across the harbor the *Oklahoma* lay belly-up in the mud like a dead whale, with gulls walking her hull.

Ruth folded the letter along its creases and held it against her chest with both hands. The broken knuckles ached in the damp. Over in the Navy Yard a welder's torch flared blue and went out and flared again, and she watched it a long time, and for the first time since December she said one word toward the sky, under her breath, and the word was *please*.

---

On the twenty-seventh of May, on the flight deck of the *Enterprise* in the harbor and in front of her crew, Admiral Nimitz pinned the Navy Cross on Doris Miller, the first Negro sailor ever to receive it. The *Advertiser* ran the picture. Two days later a note came to the ward for Miss Vellmer, three words in pencil on the back of a mess-hall menu, signed *C. Banks*: *I was wrong.* **[VERIFY: Navy Cross ceremony for Doris Miller, May 27, 1942, aboard USS *Enterprise* at Pearl Harbor]**

---

In the second week of June, the wounded came in from Midway.

Men came off the ships in boatloads, men from the carrier *Yorktown* and the ships that had gone with her, burned and broken and shot full of holes. **[VERIFY: arrival of Midway wounded at Naval Hospital Pearl Harbor, June 1942]** This time Ruth's hands did not shake. This time she knew which men would scream and live and which would lie still and polite and die, and she worked down the rows with her scissors and her basin and did not pray over any of them.

A seaman off one of the cruisers lay in the third bed on her ward with a shell fragment in his thigh and a fever of a hundred and four, talking in his sleep in a harder German than the Vellmers spoke, with the r's growled deep in the throat. He had been born at Koblenz, he told her when he woke, and raised in Milwaukee. In the fever he had gone home to the Rhine. He thought Ruth was his sister.

She sat with him and answered him in German, because the German calmed him and the English did not. She told him the vineyards on the hills were green and their mother was well, none of which she knew, and he quieted.

She did not see the men come down the ward behind her until they had stopped at the foot of the bed.

Four men stood there, three officers in khaki and a doctor, and the man in front was older than the others, with white hair and a face burned brown by years of sun on water, and very pale blue eyes. Every corpsman on the ward had gone stiff as a fence post.

"That's not Milwaukee German," he said. "Where's home, young lady?"

"Pedernales County, sir. Below Martinsburg, on the river."

He looked at her a moment longer. Then, in a slow and plain German, he said, "Und welche Familie? Which family?"

"Vellmer, sir. Fritz Vellmer. The stonemason."

"Vellmer." His eyes went down the long ward as if the river ran at the far end of it. "Vellmers farmed the Pedernales when I was a boy." He glanced at the boy in the bed, then back at her. "Is there a Vellmer in the fleet?"

"My brother Walter, sir. Boatswain's mate, *Oklahoma*."

The admiral's face did not change. The change was in his eyes. The Rhineland boy muttered in his sleep about the river.

"I am sorry," Admiral Nimitz said. "Your brother was a long way from the Pedernales."

"He joined because of you, sir," Ruth said. "He used to stand on Main Street and look up at your grandfather's hotel."

The admiral nodded slowly. Ruth could not read what moved behind his face. He looked at her hand on the boy's forehead, at the crooked knuckles.

"How old are you, Miss Vellmer?"

"Nineteen, sir."

The pale eyes rested on her a second longer than was comfortable for a girl telling a lie.

"Keep up your German," he said, in English, and moved on down the ward with the officers behind him, and every corpsman on that ward let out his breath.
