# 10. The Boy Who Lied

*Honolulu, Territory of Hawaii, June to September 1943*

The laundry truck pulled up to the hospital loading dock every morning at a quarter past seven, a Navy deuce-and-a-half with canvas over the bed and a dent in the left fender. The driver backed it in too fast and stopped it six inches short of the concrete with a squeal of brakes that brought the orderlies out to watch.

Ruth noticed the driver because of the brakes, and kept noticing him because of his face.

He was a Marine, broad through the chest, with arms that filled his rolled sleeves, and he smoked on the running board while the orderlies unloaded the clean sheets. From across the dock he looked like any Marine on the island. Up close, when he climbed down to sign the manifest on her clipboard, his cheeks had no more beard on them than a peach, a red pimple sat at the corner of his mouth, and his voice cracked on the word *ma'am.*

He saw her see it. His eyes went flat and hard. He signed the manifest *J. H. L.* in a big, round, schoolboy hand and handed back the pencil without a word.

The next morning he stopped the truck a foot short of the dock instead of six inches. The morning after, he backed it in slow.

---

His name was Jack. He came from Plymouth, North Carolina, on the Roanoke River, and he told her that much on the fourth morning, with his cigarette cupped in his hand the way he had seen older men cup theirs.

"Where's home for you, ma'am?"

"Texas. The Hill Country."

"I figured Texas. You talk slow like a Texas girl."

He grinned. The grin made him look twelve, and he knew it, and wiped it off with the back of his hand.

On the sixth morning the sheets came back damp, and the chief petty officer in charge of linens stood at the tailgate and cussed the laundry, the truck, the driver and the Navy in that order. Jack sat on the running board and took the cussing. Ruth brought him a cup of coffee from the ward pantry, the only kindness anybody on that dock showed him all morning.

"How old are you, Jack?"

"Nineteen," he said.

"I'm twenty-two," said Ruth.

He looked at her over the rim of the cup. She was nineteen in fact and twenty on the Navy's paper. At seventeen, in the summer of 1941, she had walked into the Naval Hospital and told Miss Draper, a Navy nurse with a fountain pen, that she was born in January of 1923, and the round chin, the Hill Country freckles and the bump in the bridge of her nose had all testified against her. Jack studied the freckles. Ruth studied the pimple.

He laughed first. He laughed so hard he spilled the coffee on his dungarees, and the chief petty officer turned and glared at them both.

"Lady," Jack said, when he could talk, "you ain't twenty-two."

"Well, you ain't nineteen."

"No, ma'am."

"What are you?"

He glanced at the chief petty officer, who had gone back to cussing the sheets, and lowered his voice.

"Fifteen."

Ruth had held the hand of a boy of nineteen while a surgeon took off his arm at the shoulder, and she had thought nineteen was young. She looked at the boy in front of her, at the ropes of muscle in his forearms and the down on his lip, and saw his age in his eyes for the first time, the way a pup's age shows in its eyes no matter how big its paws grow.

"How old when you joined?"

"Fourteen." He said it with pride, and then he heard the pride and was ashamed of it, and then he was proud again. "I signed up last August. Told them I was seventeen and signed my mama's name on the paper. They never looked twice. I went through boot camp like everybody else, and they shipped me here to go fight the Japs." He flicked his cigarette off the edge of the dock. "Then somebody found the truth."

"How?"

"Don't matter how. They found it, and they didn't send me home, and they didn't send me west. They gave me this."

He kicked the front tire of the truck, hard, the way a boy kicks a dog that has bitten him.

"I haul sheets and I haul spuds and I haul toilet paper. Boys I went through boot camp with are in the Solomons. I'm hauling toilet paper."

"Worse things ride in trucks on this island, Jack."

"I know it, ma'am. I seen the hospital trucks."

He said it flat. Every morning for months he had watched the grey Navy ambulances come up the hill from the harbor and the stretchers come out of them, and the shapes under the sheets on some of those stretchers, and he wanted to go anyway.

---

Ruth told no one.

She lay awake one night under the mosquito netting in the aides' quarters and turned the question over the way her father turned a stone in his hands before he set it. The Corps knew the boy's age and had put him in a truck to keep him alive. Ruth had seen the Solomons written in the bodies off the hospital ships, the jungle rot and the malaria shakes and the boys who screamed at the ceiling fans. If she told, they might put him on a ship for home after all, back to Plymouth and his mama and the tenth grade.

Jack's hating her would be a small thing. The large thing was that he would find another way, and she knew it as surely as she knew the Pedernales ran east. She was the sixth Vellmer, the baby, five years behind the next one, and she had watched Walter and Henry and Ernie walk out the front door of the limestone house to do men's work in the wide world. She had lied to the United States Navy to keep from being left on the porch. A boy like Jack, told to sit down, would climb out a window.

She kept his secret, and in July she learned the rest of it.

"Ships leave this harbor every day," Jack said. He sat on the running board with his elbows on his knees. "Transports full of Marines going west. Nobody counts heads on a transport. A man with a sea bag and a rifle walks up a gangway in a crowd. Who's going to stop him?"

"The Marine Corps will stop him. They'll court-martial him."

"For what? Going to the war?" He looked up at her. "They can't court-martial a fella for fighting. They'd look like fools."

"They'll put you in the brig."

"Then I'll do my time in the brig and go anyway." He said it the way another boy would say he was going to the county fair. "They can't do nothing to me worse than this truck."

"Jack," she said. "Where's your daddy?"

He looked across the hospital lawn toward the harbor, where oil from the *Arizona* still came up in a rainbow on the water.

"Gone," he said.

He said nothing else, and Ruth asked nothing more.

She went into the ward and washed her hands and came back to the dock. Jack had his cap on and his hand on the truck door. He eyed her sideways, wary, the way he had looked at her the first morning, as if he were waiting for the sentence every grown woman in his life had handed him sooner or later.

"I won't tell anybody," Ruth said.

His face did not change. His hand stayed on the door handle.

"You're going to do it anyway, no matter what I tell you. So hear this. Don't run off half-cocked on the first boat with a gangway. Wait for the right one. Get to the fight, Jack. Then get home."

He took his hand off the door.

"I know a boy who waited eleven months for the Army to let him fly," she said. "He's in Alabama now, flying. Waiting didn't kill him."

"Your fella?"

Ruth looked at the dented fender a long moment.

"A friend from home."

"Uh-huh," said Jack, fifteen years old and nobody's fool, and climbed into the cab and drove the laundry truck down the hill toward the harbor without grinding a single gear.

---

In June the Navy had set the *Oklahoma* upright again.

For eighteen months she had lain across the harbor on her back like a drowned steer, and through the spring of 1943 the salvage crews ran steel cables from her hull to a row of twenty-one electric winches on Ford Island, and a few degrees at a time the winches rolled her up out of the mud. **[VERIFY: righting of the USS *Oklahoma*, March–June 1943, by cables and winches on Ford Island]** On Sunday evenings Margaret rode the bus out to the heights above Aiea and watched the *Oklahoma* rise, with Kate on her hip. The ship came up streaked with mud and weed, her guns gone, her side stove in where the torpedoes had opened it. In the weeks after, the yard men took the dead out of her by ones and twos, and the Navy buried them in the cemetery at Halawa and the one in Nu'uanu Valley under stones that said Unknown. **[VERIFY: *Oklahoma* remains recovered in 1943 and buried as unknowns at Halawa and Nu'uanu cemeteries]**

Margaret went to every burial whose date she could learn. She stood at the back with the baby and a sun hat and watched the boxes go down, and she never asked which one, because nobody could have told her.

---

The man from Washington came on a Tuesday in August.

He sat in the hospital commander's office in a tan civilian suit, a small, neat, balding man with rimless glasses and a sunburn on top of his head, and when Ruth came in from the ward with her apron still on, he stood and shook her hand like a banker. The commander shut the door behind her and left them alone, a thing Ruth had not seen him do in two years.

"Miss Vellmer," the man said. "My name is Arthur Leland. I'm with the Office of Strategic Services."

The name meant nothing to her. He saw that and seemed pleased by it.

"Your name came to Washington from this island," he said. "Admiral Nimitz stopped at a bed on your ward last summer and heard you speaking German to a wounded sailor from the Rhineland. Somebody on his staff wrote it down, and somebody in Washington read it. I've come a long way to hear it for myself."

"I learned it in church," Ruth said. "At home."

"Martinsburg, Texas."

"Below Martinsburg. On the Pedernales."

He switched to German without a pause, a clipped, educated German with a Berlin edge on it, the German of the newsreels. He asked about her father's work. She said in German that he cut limestone and built houses on the Pedernales and sang bass in the choir at St. Paul, and that the best stone in Pedernales County came off a hill her father could see from his porch. He asked what she would say to a policeman on a train who asked to see her papers. She told him she would say *Bitte schön* and hand them over and ask the policeman whether his mother had found any coffee this month, because nobody in Germany had found coffee in a long time. He laughed once, surprised.

"Your accent is a hundred years out of date," he said in English. "You sound like somebody's great-grandmother from a village in Hesse."

"So my mother tells me."

"Where you'd go, that could be useful." He sat down at last across the desk from her and folded his hands. "I'll be plain. I can't tell you the nature of the work. I can tell you it's in Europe, it's dangerous, and it's voluntary, and if you say no, nobody will think less of you, and I'll fly out tomorrow. You'd train in the States first. Months of it."

"Why me?"

"Your German. Your nerve." He took off his glasses and polished them on his tie. "One more thing, since you ask. Your personnel jacket at this hospital says you were born in January 1923. The Pedernales County birth register says January 1924. You walked in here at seventeen, looked a Navy nurse in the eye and watched her write your lie on the form in ink, and nobody caught you in two years."

Ruth felt the heat climb her face. She did not look away from him.

"Are you going to report me?"

"Report you? Miss Vellmer, in my line of work that's called a qualification."

---

Ruth told Margaret that night on the porch of the cottage in Kaimuki, with the blackout curtain drawn behind them and Kate asleep in the front room in a crate lined with a folded Navy blanket. She gave her the part she could tell: Washington, war work, Europe, and nothing more.

Margaret listened with her hands around a cup of tea gone cold. She was thinner than she had been in December of 1941, and her schoolteacher's hair was pinned up the way she pinned it for a classroom, though she had not stood in front of one since Brenham.

"You already said yes," Margaret said.

"I said I'd think about it."

"Ruth. I've known you since you were twelve and danced on Walter's feet at my wedding. You said yes in the room with the man." She set the cup on the rail. "Walter would have had a fit. Then he'd have bragged on you to every boatswain's mate in the fleet."

"Will you go home now? To Texas?"

Margaret looked out across the blackout toward the harbor, where the *Oklahoma* sat upright and unlit on the mud off Ford Island.

"They're taking the last ones out of her," Margaret said. "The harbor has nothing left to give me. I'll take Kate home to Brenham in the spring, when a woman with a baby can get a berth." She was quiet a while. "She'll grow up never knowing which stone is her daddy's. I've made my peace with that, most days."

Ruth reached over and took her sister-in-law's hand, and Margaret let her hold it, and the two of them sat on the porch in the dark and listened to the mangoes hit the tin, the way they had on the nights Walter had duty.

---

A letter from Alabama came the week before she sailed, in the envelope Lena always used, forwarded from the Sunday house in Martinsburg. Sam had written it in pencil on the back of a training form, and the censor had passed it whole.

*The boy who tows our gunnery sleeve prays harder than any deacon. Wings the first of October if I don't fly into a pine tree before then. I'd ask you to come and pin them, but you're four thousand miles off and white, and Alabama would have opinions about both. Pin them on in your head, Ruth. I'll feel it.*

She read it four times sitting on her bunk and put it in the bottom of her sea bag with the rest of them, every letter he had sent since the fall of 1940, tied in a bundle with a length of fiddle string Lena had mailed her for the purpose.

---

Ruth sailed on the last day of September, on a Matson liner painted battleship grey.

The pier below the Aloha Tower was crowded with wounded men on crutches, Hawaiian women selling leis, and sailors' girls crying into handkerchiefs. Margaret came through the crowd in the sun hat with Kate on her hip, nineteen months old and solid as a sack of feed, in a yellow dress Mrs. Tanaka had sewn from a printed flour bag.

Kate had Walter's chin. She had been told nothing, because a baby cannot be told anything, and she knew anyway. When Ruth kissed her, Kate grabbed two fists of her hair and would not let go. When Margaret pried the fingers loose one at a time, Kate threw her head back and screamed, red-faced and furious, the same scream she had given the world at a quarter to five in the morning in the back bedroom with the blanket over the window.

"She's a Vellmer," Margaret said, over the noise. "She doesn't like to be left."

"Neither did I, at her age."

"I know it. Go on, before I start too."

The ship's whistle blew, one long blast that shook the pier boards under their feet. Ruth climbed the gangway with her sea bag on her shoulder and Sam's letters at the bottom of it. At the rail she found Margaret's sun hat in the crowd, and the yellow dress, and the small red face still screaming. The tugs leaned into the hull, a strip of green water opened between the ship and the pier, and the sound of that baby carried across the widening gap long after Ruth could no longer make out the dress.

Tomorrow, in Alabama, they would pin wings on Sam Mayfield. Ruth stood at the rail with her hands on the warm iron and pinned them on in her head, the way he had asked, until Diamond Head went blue and small behind the ship and the whole island sank into the sea.
