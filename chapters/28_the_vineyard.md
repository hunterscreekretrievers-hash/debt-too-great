# 28. The Vineyard

*October 2013. Elm Street, St. Paul Lutheran and the road home from Cedar Gap.*

The envelope came on Friday, the eleventh of October, in the same heavy cream stock as every envelope Clayton Rusk had ever mailed her. Ruth carried it in from the box on Elm Street by one corner, the way a woman carries a dead mouse by the tail.

"Well," Ruth said. "Let's see what the king wants now."

Mr. Rusk regretted the unpleasantness of the summer, which he called a misunderstanding among professionals acting in good faith. He wished to make a final offer. His clients had found comparable acreage outside Blanco, with river frontage and a newer house, and would deed it to her plus the difference in cash, in exchange for the Vellmer homeplace. If she preferred, they would pay cash alone, by wire from Chase, at a figure typed in bold and underlined twice.

Ruth read the figure a second time, and then the name of the bank. She had never kept a dime at Chase. In 1944 the Resistance knew the Chase branch in Paris was open for business under the Germans and had frozen the Jewish accounts. On the nineteenth of July that year three trucks had stood on the verge at the bridge below Dieulefit, and the officer from Lyon had spread his map across the hood of the lead truck, a hand's width above the name on its grille. The name was OPEL, and Opel belonged to General Motors of Detroit. Without American oil and American trucks Hitler never gets to Paris, and not one of those companies paid a nickel for it. The rest of that ledger she had typed long ago on a page of her own. Every one of them would do it again for whoever paid. Her own savings sat in Omaha, where nobody froze anything.

Then she put the letter in the drawer under the telephone, with the rubber bands, the dead batteries and the warranty card for the water heater.

The figure was more money than Fritz Vellmer had seen in all his years of cutting stone. He had quarried every stone of that house out of the hill across the river in the summer of 1908, with a hand drill, a sledge and a mule that bit, and cut *F. V. 1908* into the cornerstone by the kitchen door while the mortar was green. When Ruth was four, her mother pressed Ruth's hand into the wet lime of the new smokehouse step. The print was still there, a child's hand with the fingers spread like a little star, under eighty-five years of dust and mud-dauber nests.

A better vineyard, the letter said. Newer house.

She stood at the counter with her palm flat on the drawer, and her thumb went to the bare third finger of her left hand and stayed there.

"No," she said to the empty kitchen, and Honey thumped her tail once on the linoleum.

---

Sunday came up cold and bright behind the first norther of the fall, which had roared down off the Panhandle overnight like a freight train with no headlight, and the square bell tower of St. Paul Lutheran caught the sun. When the bell started at a quarter to ten, Ruth began buttoning her coat.

She took the last pew on the right, where the varnish had worn through to bare pine under her right hand, as she had every Sunday since the first Sunday of February 1956. Honey lay down with her muzzle across the toe of Ruth's shoe.

Since August, people had been stopping her on the church steps to shake her hand. Across the aisle sat Dale Kunkel, in a tan sport coat, the man whose words Rusk's office had typed into an affidavit. The back of his neck stayed red through the whole first hymn.

Pastor Mark Albrecht climbed into the pulpit and set his reading glasses on his nose. He was forty-four, built like the offensive tackle he had been at Texas Lutheran, with big red hands that made the pulpit Bible look like a hymnal. When he was fourteen, the bank took his father's farm near Hye, and he stood in the yard and watched an auctioneer sell his mother's sewing machine off a hay wagon for eleven dollars while his father sat in the pickup with both windows shut.

"I'm leaving the lectionary this morning," he said. "Somebody can write the bishop. First Kings, chapter twenty-one, out of the King James, because it sounds like a man telling you the truth."

"*And it came to pass after these things, that Naboth the Jezreelite had a vineyard, which was in Jezreel, hard by the palace of Ahab king of Samaria.*"

He told them who Ahab was, a king with ivory in his house who looked out his window at the little vineyard next door and wanted it for a vegetable garden. Then he read the offer.

"*Give me thy vineyard, that I may have it for a garden of herbs, because it is near unto my house: and I will give thee for it a better vineyard than it; or, if it seem good to thee, I will give thee the worth of it in money.*"

"Any banker in this room would call that a fair deal," Pastor Albrecht said. "Now listen to Naboth."

"*And Naboth said to Ahab, The LORD forbid it me, that I should give the inheritance of my fathers unto thee.*"

He took off his reading glasses. "Naboth wasn't being stubborn. Under the law of Moses that land belonged to God, and God had handed it to Naboth's family in trust. Selling that dirt would have been selling his father's bones. So Naboth says the most dangerous word a little man can say to a big one, and the word is *no*."

Ahab went home, turned his face to the wall and would not eat, a grown king sulking like a boy who lost at marbles. Then the queen came to him.

"*Dost thou now govern the kingdom of Israel? arise, and eat bread, and let thine heart be merry: I will give thee the vineyard of Naboth the Jezreelite.*"

"Jezebel didn't send soldiers," the pastor said. "Soldiers make noise. She sat down at a desk and wrote letters in her husband's name, sealed with his seal, to the elders of Naboth's own town. Call a fast, she told them. Set Naboth up front. Find two worthless men to swear he cursed God and the king."

He held up his thick fingers and counted the steps. "A letter on good paper. A seal. A prayer meeting. Two witnesses. Then rocks. Every step legal, done in daylight by respectable people, and not one elder asked a question."

Ruth looked at the window above the altar, Christ in the garden in thin wavy glass, and saw a different room: thick paper, a seal, yellow roses in a vase, a man in a good suit reading her name in a polite voice. She had sat in a room like that once on the fifth floor of 84 Avenue Foch, and in August Clayton Rusk had tried to build another one in the Pedernales County courthouse, and a Texas judge had looked at her clock and sent her home.

"They carried Naboth out of the city and stoned him with stones," the pastor said, "and Second Kings tells us his sons too, so no heir would make trouble later. Then Ahab goes down to look over his new property.

"Somebody's waiting for him in the vines."

He read Elijah's part, and his voice dropped.

"*And Ahab said to Elijah, Hast thou found me, O mine enemy? And he answered, I have found thee: because thou hast sold thyself to work evil in the sight of the LORD.*"

"*Thus saith the LORD, In the place where dogs licked the blood of Naboth shall dogs lick thy blood, even thine.*"

Under the pew Honey lifted her head with her odd ear cocked, and a child two pews up turned around and giggled.

"Of Jezebel," Pastor Albrecht said, "*The dogs shall eat Jezebel by the wall of Jezreel.* Years later her own servants threw her out a window, and when they went to bury her, *they found no more of her than the skull, and the feet, and the palms of her hands.* The dogs left the hands that wrote the letters."

He closed the big Bible.

"Here's the hard part, and I won't put a Sunday dress on it. Naboth never got his vineyard back from the king. Naboth got stoned in a field outside his own town, and his boys with him, and the Bible doesn't fix that for him in this life. God let it happen. I don't know why. Anybody who tells you he knows why is selling something."

Ruth felt the words go into her the way a splint goes onto a broken bone, hard and welcome.

"Here's the other hard part. When Elijah finished, Ahab tore his clothes and put on sackcloth and *went softly*, and God said, *Seest thou how Ahab humbleth himself before me?* God held back part of the judgment because the man repented. Even Ahab." He looked out over them. "Jezebel never put on sackcloth. She painted her face and stood in that window insulting the man who came to kill her. Only one of the two ever said he was sorry."

He prayed. Across St. Paul the knees went down in a long rustle like wind through dry corn. Ruth stayed sitting, with her hand on the dog.

---

On the steps afterward Pastor Albrecht held her long hand in his big red one a second longer than the others.

"I didn't preach that at you, Miss Ruth," he said quietly.

"I know where you were aiming, Mark." She nodded toward Dale Kunkel, who stood at the bottom of the steps turning his hat brim through his fingers. "Dale knows where you were aiming too. Give him time. You left out one thing."

"What thing?"

"Naboth's wife. The Bible never says what became of her."

The pastor looked at her. "No," he said. "The Bible never names her."

On the way home she stopped at the Mustang Grill, with *BEAT CEDAR GAP* soaped green and gold across the front window, for a cup of coffee she would leave half full. Bonnie had Thursday's paper folded open on the counter to the letters, and she read one out loud to the rancher at the end of the counter: *"On the twenty-second of September, after the Sunday service, two men walked into the churchyard of All Saints in Peshawar, where the families sat on the lawn at lunch, and blew themselves up among the plates. Baghdad in 2010. Alexandria in 2011. Peshawar this fall. Count the churches. Lucie, Pedernales County."*

"That woman's been writing this paper since Carter," Bonnie said, "and nobody in this county can tell you her name."

Ruth blew across her coffee and said nothing.

Over the counter the television was turned to CNM. A crowd in Washington was shoving black metal barricades aside at the World War II Memorial, and the words across the bottom of the screen said SHUTDOWN PROTESTERS STORM MEMORIAL.

"Two weeks ago those same barricades stood in front of old men from Mississippi who flew up on an Honor Flight to see their own memorial," Ruth said. "That day the word was *veterans*. Today the word is *protesters*."

"The news is the news, Ruth," Bonnie said.

"So was everything in Goebbels's papers." Ruth set down the cup. "Every day in Berlin a man stood up at a press conference and told the papers of a whole country what to print, and the papers printed it. Hitler wrote the method into his book. The broad mass of a people, he wrote, falls victim to a big lie more easily than to a small one, *einer großen Lüge leichter zum Opfer fällt als einer kleinen.* He wrote that his enemies did it, and then he did it for twelve years. Over the gate at Dachau they hung *Work makes you free.*"

Bonnie set the coffeepot back on its burner.

"The lie is half the trick, Bonnie. The other half is the word they pick for you. In April two brothers bombed the finish line of the Boston Marathon and killed three people, and the one they caught wrote inside the boat where he hid, *we Muslims are one body, you hurt one you hurt us all.* At Fort Hood in 2009 an Army major shouted *Allahu Akbar* and killed thirteen people. They sentenced him to death in August, and the Army still calls it *workplace violence*."

The rancher at the end of the counter chuckled into his pie. "Miss Ruth's been watching too much cable."

"I don't get cable on Elm Street," Ruth said. "In 1933 I got Berlin on my daddy's shortwave. Berlin was plenty."

Bonnie looked up at the screen a long while and did not change the channel.

At her gate Ruth tipped her head back to the hard blue sky over the water tower.

"You let him die, though," she said out loud. "Naboth. You let them do it."

The sky stayed blue and empty. A grackle walked across her yard and looked at her with one yellow eye. Nothing else answered her. She went in and fried two eggs in her mother's black skillet and gave Honey the second one.

---

The Mustangs beat Cedar Gap that Friday, 35 to 14, and the Martinsburg people in the visitors' bleachers stared at the scoreboard the way a man stares at a winning lottery ticket he bought to break a twenty.

A year earlier these boys had lost ten straight. Now they stood six and two, three and nothing in district. DeShawn Hollins ran for 186 yards. Brody Vossberg had nine tackles and a long purple bruise on his forearm that he told Teddy came from practice, and Teddy looked at it a long time, taped it, and wrote something in the notebook in his shirt pocket. Luis Garza kicked five extra points, and every time the ball went through, he looked up into the visitors' stands at the place his parents were not, because his parents did not drive county roads after dark.

On the bus home the heater roared on Lyle's side and did nothing for anybody else. No. 7 smelled of grass stains, wet socks, Icy Hot and the Whataburger Teddy had bought for the linemen.

The boys stayed loud for the first twenty miles, the way boys are loud after a win nobody promised them. Lyle said, "Gentlemen," once, in a voice no louder than a man asking for the salt, and the noise dropped by half.

Past Blanco the road went dark between the cedar breaks, the headlights tunneling like a lantern down a mine shaft, and the boys fell quiet one by one.

Tyler Kott dropped into the seat across the aisle. He had a face that had never learned to hide a thing. Around his left wrist, where Teddy had taped him before the game, he had written in black Sharpie on the white tape: *JK 2012*.

Honey stood up, crossed the aisle and laid her chin on Tyler's knee.

"She does me that way a lot," he said.

"She picks," Ruth said. "She doesn't pick everybody."

"My brother would've liked tonight," Tyler said after a mile. He said it to the dog. "He played center too. Number fifty-five. Coach gave me his number." He turned his wrist so the tape showed. "Jacob. Army. He got hit in Kandahar last September. An IED. They told my mom it was quick." He laughed a little, without any fun in it. "Everybody says it was quick. Like that's the main thing."

"How old was he?"

"Twenty-one." He rubbed his thumb across the tape. "My mom won't let anybody drive his truck. It sits under the pecan tree. Every Sunday I go start it so the battery won't die, and she watches me from the kitchen window, and neither one of us says a word."

Ruth nodded. Outside the window a deer's eyes caught the headlights in the bar ditch and were gone.

"I had a brother killed in a war," she said.

Tyler raised his head.

"Walter. My oldest brother. He was on the *Oklahoma* at Pearl Harbor. She rolled over with four hundred and some men inside her, and they buried him in Hawaii under a stone that says Unknown. He was thirty-one. He taught me to whistle through my fingers. His wife was carrying their baby."

"Dang," Tyler said softly. "How old were you?"

"Seventeen."

"Where were you when the news came?"

Ruth touched the bare place on her wedding finger and watched the little cross swing from Lyle's mirror on its bead chain.

"I'll tell you the one thing I know," she said. "Then the thing is yours."

"Yes, ma'am."

"A day's coming, and soon, when you go a whole afternoon without thinking about Jacob. Somebody will say something funny at practice, and you'll laugh, really laugh, and halfway through you'll remember him. You'll feel like you killed him all over again by forgetting him for one afternoon." She turned and looked at him with her grey eyes. "You won't have killed him. Grief tells that lie to everybody. Jacob would have laughed at the same joke, louder. You keep laughing. You keep starting that truck."

Tyler Kott held her eyes. His big jaw worked. Then he put his face down in the dog's neck, and his shoulders shook twice, hard, and stopped. Honey stood still and let him cry into her fur.

Only Lyle saw it, in the long mirror over the windshield, and he took the next cattle guard slow so the bus would not rattle the boy.

Tyler lifted his head and wiped his face. "You never answered me," he said. "Where you were that day."

"No," Ruth said. "I didn't answer it."

He grinned, a lineman's grin, wide and crooked. "You're a hard old lady, Miss Ruth."

"So the lawyers tell me."

The lights of Martinsburg came up out of the dark, the water tower first. When the bus turned into the lot, a line of pickups began to honk, and the boys woke up yelling all over again.

Lyle opened the door, and the cold rushed up the steps.

Tyler stood, ducked his head under the low roof of the bus and stopped by her seat. "Sunday's coming," he said.

"I can count to Sunday."

"I'm going to start the truck." He hesitated. "Maybe I'll drive it around the block. One time around the block."

"Ask your mother first," Ruth said.

"Yes, ma'am."

He went down the steps into the headlights, and three boys jumped on his back at once, and he carried all three of them across the parking lot without breaking stride.
