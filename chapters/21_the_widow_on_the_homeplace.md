# 21. The Widow on the Homeplace

*The Vellmer homeplace and Martinsburg, Texas, 1972 to December 2009*

Kate Hartmann turned thirty in February of 1972, and that spring she found the front half of a verse.

For years Kate had handed out the back half to anybody who would hold still: the drunks on the gurneys at the Robert B. Green, the girls in her Sunday school class, a cabdriver once at three in the morning. *I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly.* One night on her dinner break, with her Bible open on a steel tray in the nurses' lounge, Kate read the sentence in front of it. *The thief cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy.*

Kate underlined it in ballpoint so hard the pen scored the next page.

Driving home at seven in the morning past the shuttered bars on Commerce Street, Kate saw her whole ward plain for the first time. Every overdose, every knifing, every baby in the burn unit had a thief behind it, and the families in the waiting room never blamed the thief. The waiting room blamed God. Kate decided that morning that anybody who lays the thief's work at God's door has given the thief the game, and she never changed her mind.

From then on Kate prayed for Mama Ruth every day of her life. Kate knew nothing of her aunt's war. Nobody in the family knew it either. Kate knew only what a nurse sees: four round white scars on the inside of the left forearm that Mama Ruth said came from a stove, the crooked knuckles on the right hand, a woman who woke at four and sat in the dark, who came to church and sat in the last pew and went out the door on the final hymn. Kate knew a wound when she saw one. She prayed the way the widow in the eighteenth chapter of Luke kept coming back to the unjust judge, *always to pray, and not to faint*, and she kept no count of the years.

---

The homeplace telephone was a party line, and a woman who had learned her trade in the Drôme did not discuss her business on a party line, so every Wednesday afternoon at a quarter past two, from the fall of 1974 on, the mail lady stood in the telephone booth outside the Martinsburg post office with a roll of quarters and called a broker in San Antonio. In 1988 the telephone company took the party lines out of the river bottom, and after that she made the Wednesday call from her own kitchen with the door shut. The booth itself came down in the nineties.

Since the fifties Ruth had read the bank president's *Wall Street Journal* at the sorting case before she put it in his box, the way she had once read German troop movements, for the thing the writer was afraid to put in print. In 1969 *Forbes* ran a story under the headline "How Omaha Beats Wall Street," about a quiet man named Warren Buffett who bought while the crowd ran for the exits. Ruth understood him on the first page.

By October of 1974 the market had fallen almost in half, and Berkshire Hathaway traded near forty-two dollars a share. Ruth had saved about twenty thousand dollars since 1947. She signed a note for five thousand more against her cattle, in the worst cattle market in decades. The bank president took off his reading glasses and told her she had lost her mind on both counts. Ruth thanked him for his concern and signed her name, *Ruth M. Vellmer*, in a tall slanted hand.

That Wednesday she bought 595 shares. She never sold one, and she kept buying a few a year out of her paycheck until a single share cost more than a month's pay.

---

In 1975 Billy Graham's film company made a picture of Corrie ten Boom's book *The Hiding Place*, with a Houston actress named Jeannette Clift playing Corrie. At the premiere in Beverly Hills somebody threw a canister of tear gas into the theater, and the canister was marked with a swastika. The crowd went out coughing into the street, and Graham preached to them on the sidewalk while the police hunted for whoever threw it. The papers carried what Corrie said that evening. *What we have to do is love these people who hate us.*

Ruth read the story at the sorting case at six in the morning, thirty years after Ravensbrück, and put the paper in the bank president's box with the rest of his mail.

Kate drove up from San Antonio that Saturday with two tickets in her pocketbook.

"It's a Christian picture, Mama Ruth. About a Dutch lady in a concentration camp. You never go anywhere."

Ruth looked at the tickets a long time. Then she got her coat.

Ruth and Kate sat in the middle of a full house in San Antonio with a sack of popcorn between them that neither one touched. On the screen two sisters were searched and herded into a long wooden barracks, and the platforms went up three high, and the straw crawled. When the older sister began to thank God for the fleas, Ruth's right hand closed on the armrest. Kate looked down at the crooked knuckles gone white as candle wax and could not read them. She laid her own hand over them, and Ruth let it stay.

When Betsie died on the screen, a woman behind them cried out loud. Ruth sat with her eyes open and her face shut like a barn door. Nobody in that theater knew that the woman in the grey coat had slept across the aisle from those two sisters in Barracks 28.

Ruth said nothing on the drive home. Past Boerne, with the sun going down red over the cedar hills, Kate said one thing.

"*In every thing give thanks.* I don't know how she did it, Mama Ruth."

Ruth watched the fence posts go by the window and heard a Dutch voice in the dark, and then the same verse in German, passed down the bunks like a hymn sung in a round. She did not answer. At the cattle guard she squeezed Kate's hand once and got out of the car.

---

Katharina Vellmer died in the spring of 1976, at eighty-eight, in the back bedroom of Lena's Sunday house, with the window open on Lena's roses.

Ruth sat with her mother three nights. On the last night Katharina began the prayer as she had taught it to six children at the kitchen table on the river, *Vater unser, der du bist im Himmel*, in a voice gone thin as onionskin, and Ruth said it with her. At *Dein Wille geschehe* Ruth's voice stopped where it always stopped. Katharina's voice did not stop. *Wie im Himmel, also auch auf Erden.* The old woman said it through to the amen without a catch, the way a woman walks a road she has walked every day of her life, and she was gone before morning.

Ruth closed her mother's eyes with two fingers, the way she had closed a sailor's on the ward at Pearl Harbor. Then she sat by the bed with her hand on the quilt, and she could not follow her mother past *Dein Wille geschehe*.

---

Casing the route in the second week of August, Ruth found a fat envelope in Kate's round hand in the pigeonhole for her own box on the river road. At noon, in the shade of the pecans at a low-water crossing, Ruth opened the envelope with the grasshoppers ticking against the windshield. Inside lay a full page torn from a newspaper, with seven young women's faces across the top of it.

On the last night of July a wall of water had come down the Big Thompson Canyon in Colorado after a foot of rain fell in a few hours, and more than a hundred and forty people died. Thirty-five women from Campus Crusade for Christ had been at a retreat at a guest ranch on the river near Loveland. The police came to move them to higher ground. Two of the cars carrying them went under, and seven of the women drowned. Bill Bright, who ran Campus Crusade, had bought the page to tell how each of the seven had lived for Christ.

Kate had clipped a card to the corner, with the thief from John and the widow from Luke copied on it and one line of her own under them. Ruth read Kate's line with the Jeep idling, put the card in her shirt pocket and finished the route.

---

In October of 1976 Ruth read Jeremiah Denton's *When Hell Was in Session* at the kitchen table with a Number 2 pencil. Her mother's German Bible lay on one side of the lamp, and Kate's card leaned against the sugar bowl on the other. Ruth read slowest over the pages where the prisoners tapped through the walls of their cells, five rows of five letters, so a man alone in the dark could tell the next man he was still alive.

For thirty years the church people had handed Ruth verses the way a druggist hands a woman an aspirin across the counter. Let go and let God. The Lord never gives you more than you can handle. Pray more, read your Bible more, and you'll feel better in the morning. A woman who had looked through the washroom glass at the dead of Ravensbrück did not feel better in the morning. The aspirin had been the stumbling block all along, not the Lord. Every easy answer passed across a church table had pushed Ruth one pew farther back, until the last pew by the door was the only one left.

Ruth closed the book on the pencil and waited for spring. All winter Kate's card rode in her shirt pocket on the route. On the last Friday of April, 1977, forty years to the week from the spring her father hauled stone below the dome, Ruth finished the route, rolled her old Army blanket around a canteen, a flashlight, Denton's book and Kate's card, tied the bedroll with baling twine, and drove the pickup north out of the river bottom toward the granite country, with the bluebonnets going by in the bar ditches.

She had flown over Enchanted Rock every Sunday afternoon for fifteen years. She had not set foot on it since the spring of 1937, when her father built a rancher's chimney below the dome and brought Sam Mayfield along to carry water. The ranch family that owned the rock still let people climb it. Ruth left the green Ford in the cedar flat at the foot, as near as she could judge to the place where the two families had camped, and laid her blanket on the ground.

At sundown the whole face of the dome turned the color of the inside of a peach, the way it had when she was thirteen.

In the night the rock began to talk. A long low groan came off the dome above her, then a crack like a rifle shot far off, then the slow deep creak of ship's timbers. Ruth lay on her back in the dark and heard her father's voice from under his hat. *The sun heats it all day. At night it cools, and the skin of it shrinks faster than the heart, and it moves. Every stone talks when it moves.* At thirteen she had thought the groaning was the loneliest sound in the world. Forty years on, Ruth knew the sound for what it was: a thing too big for its bed, settling.

She climbed it at first light. The granite was cold and gritty as sandpaper, and the pools in the hollows held rainwater and the same tiny shrimp darting in the green. Three holds, three holds. Her knees reported every one of them. At the crown of the dome she sat down where a barefoot boy with a balsa wing in his overalls had once sat beside her to watch the sun come up, and she began to count, the way a surgical nurse counts the instruments before the surgeon closes the wound.

A schoolyard in Martinsburg in 1937, and her own nose bleeding for a Black boy the rest of the schoolyard had left alone. The burn ward at Pearl Harbor. The gorge below Dieulefit, and the trucks. The fifth floor of 84 Avenue Foch, the tub of cold water, and four cigarettes put out on her arm. A Norwegian woman who stood in front of her at the selection and went in her place. Violette Szabo sent to Torgau on a good arm while Ruth stayed behind on a bad one. A farm girl in the dark of Barracks 28 who went quiet at a Name, and a room that for one breath was not empty. A clerk's mistake that let Corrie ten Boom walk out of the gate.

Every time, the thief had come to steal, and to kill, and to destroy. Every time, Ruth Vellmer had walked out alive.

Ruth had never once said God built the camp. Her quarrel with Him had been His silence, and that is a different quarrel. Now, with Kate's card in her shirt pocket, the thief had a name, and the name was not God's.

Ruth Vellmer had not cried since the war. She had not cried for Sam in Malmö, or for Betsie on the washroom floor, or at her mother's bedside the spring before. On top of Enchanted Rock, with the wind steady out of the south, the dam broke. She cried the way the rock had groaned all night, from somewhere too deep for argument, for Sam and Betsie and Violette and Gaston and the Norwegian woman whose name she never learned, and for the girl of twenty-one who had carried all of them home in a shoebox and never once set the box on the ground. She cried until her shirtsleeve was wet to the elbow. The psalmist said God keeps count. *Put thou my tears into thy bottle: are they not in thy book?* That morning Ruth believed every word of the verse. A God who kept a bottle had been there to fill it.

C. S. Lewis had put the rest of it in the paperback he made out of his wartime radio talks, *Mere Christianity*, which sat on the shelf over her kitchen table. *A man does not call a line crooked unless he has some idea of a straight line.* Ruth had seen more of the crooked line than any preacher in Pedernales County. Nobody who had stood in the yard at Ravensbrück needed an argument that evil was real. On top of the rock she saw the other half of the argument. A thief breaks only into a house with something in it worth taking, and a woman who has watched the thief work her whole life has been standing beside the treasure from the beginning.

Ruth Vellmer had never been a spectator to any of it. She had climbed every ladder on the homeplace by six, climbed this rock at thirteen with a splinted nose, and carried eggs past German rifles at twenty. Every time she went toward the fight, Somebody had gone in ahead of her. God had met her at the point of the spear and walked her out the gate. Nebuchadnezzar looked into his furnace and counted one man too many: *Lo, I see four men loose, walking in the midst of the fire, and they have no hurt; and the form of the fourth is like the Son of God.* Ruth had carried four cigarette burns out of Avenue Foch and a shaved head out of Ravensbrück, but the fire had never been allowed to keep her, any more than the lions had been allowed to keep Daniel. The king's men had shut the den and sealed it with a stone, and in the morning Daniel answered from the dark. On the wall of a French church in 1944 she had seen Him hung in front of a red flag like a man stood up to be shot, and she saw now that He had been the first one to stand where she stood, and that the Norwegian woman and Violette and Betsie had only been standing where He had stood first.

The sun came out of the east red and lit the granite under her until the rock glowed, as it had glowed under two children in 1937. Ruth took Denton's book out of the blanket roll and found the page about praying in a dark cell when the praying seemed to do no good at all. In the margin she wrote one word.

*Yes.*

That night, at home, she slid Kate's card into the back of her King James beside the Tozer clipping, folded the page with the seven faces behind it, and knelt by the iron bed. She began the prayer in her mother's German. At *Dein Wille geschehe* the words stopped where they always stopped. Ruth stayed on her knees a long time anyway. For the first time in thirty-three years she knew Who had been holding her up all the way to *Dein Wille geschehe*, and she knew He would wait for the rest.

Nothing in Martinsburg looked any different from the street. Monday morning the mail lady cased her route before daylight and squared every flag, the same as always. The grief had not gone anywhere, and neither had the hurts, and she still kept her own company the way other women kept a garden. Tozer had explained the chisel to her, and a woman who has been under the chisel does not go looking for crowds.

The war had burned one other lesson into her, as deep as the four scars on her arm. In France the friendliest man in the café could be the one who sold you to the Gestapo, and the warmest handshake at the market could belong to the man who wrote down your bicycle. *Beware of false prophets, which come to you in sheep's clothing, but inwardly they are ravening wolves.* Six verses later, in the same chapter of Matthew, Jesus said that many would call Him *Lord, Lord* on the last day and list the wonderful works they had done in His name, and He would answer them, *I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity.* Some of the wolves would be standing in pulpits. Ruth had met the wolves in their wool. A woman who has learned to read a face for the wolf underneath reads every face that way afterward, the faces in the pews included, and most of what she reads she keeps to herself. Thirty-four years later a yellow dog would take that job off her hands.

Inside, though, the woman in the last pew was free, and no blow from any pulpit or fellowship hall would ever take that from her.

In 1978 a Chattanooga heart doctor named Maurice Rawlings put out a book called *Beyond Death's Door*. One of his patients had dropped on a treadmill in the doctor's own office, and every time the pushing on his chest stopped, the man came to screaming that he was in hell and begging the doctor not to let him go back. Rawlings had no use for religion when that morning began. The man was a mail carrier. Ruth read that page twice at the kitchen table and thought of a farm girl's laugh in the dark of Barracks 28.

---

On the Wednesday after Thanksgiving in 1979, the twenty-eighth of November, the fellowship hall at St. Paul smelled of turkey soup and coffee from the big steel urn. Ruth was fifty-five. Since the spring of 1956 she had come to the Wednesday supper and the Bible class after it, sat at the end of the third table, washed the cups when it was over and said almost nothing.

For twenty-four nights every television in the county had carried the same pictures. On the fourth of November students in Tehran had gone over the wall of the American embassy and taken the people inside hostage, and every night since, the crowd in the street outside had chanted "Death to America" for the cameras with their fists going up together like pistons. On the twentieth, armed men seized the Grand Mosque in Mecca. The Ayatollah went on the radio and said America had done it. The next day a mob in Pakistan believed him, poured over the walls of the embassy in Islamabad and set it on fire. A Marine corporal named Steven Crowley died there, with an Army warrant officer and two Pakistani men who worked in the building.

The young pastor's lesson that night was the whole armor of God, out of the sixth chapter of Ephesians. At the end he asked whether anybody had a word on the news.

Ruth set her cup down in its saucer.

"Thirty-eight years ago tonight," she said, "the Grand Mufti of Jerusalem sat down with Adolf Hitler in Berlin. The twenty-eighth of November, 1941. Hitler told him that when the German armies reached the south, Germany's one aim there would be the destruction of the Jews living in the Arab lands under British protection. In 1943 the Mufti went to Bosnia for the SS and helped raise a division of Muslim volunteers. The Germans named it Handschar, after a Turkish sword. I read the files once."

Nobody moved a spoon.

"Those crowds on the television chant on a signal, the way the crowds at Nuremberg chanted for Hitler. A man on the radio tells a city the Americans defiled its holy place, and by the next afternoon a Marine is dead in a burning building, and the man who told the lie never says a word of sorrow. Berlin chose words over facts in 1933. The papers learned to write *protective custody* when they meant Dachau. When a government starts choosing words over facts, a church had better read its history." She folded her hands on the oilcloth. "Two Pakistanis died in that embassy beside our boys, and a couple of good ones do not wash a whole crowd clean. My quarrel is with the doctrine of holy war the Mufti carried to Berlin, and the record it has kept from 1941 to this Wednesday night."

Behind the pass-through a spoon rang once against the soup pot, and nobody turned toward the sound.

"Mark this, though," Ruth said. "On the sixteenth of April, 1945, General Patton had better than a thousand people from Weimar marched up the hill to Buchenwald, because every one of them swore the camp was news to them. They filed past the ovens and the bodies stacked like cordwood, and the women held handkerchiefs to their faces and said they had never known a thing. That smoke had blown over their gardens for eight years. A people that keeps quiet in front of a tyrant has already picked a side, and it is the side of the oppressor. God will judge the silence the same as the deed. Evil wins when good men do nothing."

Henry Vellmer stood up at the second table.

He was sixty-seven, the lineman's shoulders gone soft under a cardigan. Since the co-op retired him he had found the Lord a second time, louder. He taught the men's class and carried a Bible in a zippered leather cover that he laid open beside his plate every Wednesday like a man setting out a pistol. Henry could find any verse in the Book faster than another man could find his car keys, and he had never once found one that told him to take his seat.

"Sister," Henry said, and the hall went quiet down to the forks. "*Judge not, that ye be not judged.*" He licked his thumb and turned a page. "*Be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another.* Love your enemies. Bless them that curse you. What came out of your mouth tonight was hate, Ruthie, and hate has no chair at the Lord's table. Christ died for that Ayatollah the same as He died for you." He looked down the tables to see whether the crowd was still his. "We all know you went through some things over there. Whatever the war did to you, you carried it home in your suitcase, and you've spent thirty-four years unpacking it on decent people. Lay it down at the cross. Forgive, and hold your peace. *The tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity.* Don't you set this church on fire, Sister."

Ruth looked at her brother a long time. She saw the platform of the Southern Pacific depot in San Antonio in July of 1945, and Henry in his co-op khakis walking right past a ninety-pound woman because he did not know her face.

She said nothing. She folded her napkin, washed her cup and saucer at the pass-through, set them on the shelf with the others and walked out to the Jeep.

Paul stood before the Sanhedrin and said, *I have lived in all good conscience before God until this day,* and the high priest ordered him struck on the mouth for saying it. Ruth had taken her blow from the second table. Paul's words were still hers.

Nobody said amen to Henry. The old families looked into their soup, and Mrs. Grieshaber shut her eyes the way a woman does in a dentist's chair. The young pastor closed in prayer for the hostages and for Corporal Crowley's mother and for everybody at these tables. Nobody stood up for Ruth either, and that silence was the thing she carried home.

David had written the supper down three thousand years earlier. *Yea, mine own familiar friend, in whom I trusted, which did eat of my bread, hath lifted up his heel against me.* Henry had eaten turkey soup one table over from her every Wednesday since the co-op retired him.

In the gravel lot Lyle Mosher caught her at the Jeep, turning his hat around by the brim. He was seventeen and already sang bass in the choir.

"Miss Ruth. You were right in there. Every word."

"Thank you, Lyle."

"I should have stood beside you."

"You're standing now," Ruth said. Bess was waiting on the seat with her grey chin on the dashboard, thirteen years old and deaf in one ear, and the whole way down the river road the old dog kept her nose in Ruth's free hand.

At the homeplace Ruth knelt by the iron bed out of habit and began the prayer in her mother's German. At *Dein Wille geschehe* the words stopped where they had stopped in a tent in Maryland thirty-five years earlier. In the hall the party-line telephone rang two longs and a short, the Moellerings' ring, and Ruth listened to it ring for somebody else. "You're busy tonight," Ruth told the ceiling. "Half the county is on the line with You." She stayed on her knees until the ringing quit. Then she went to the kitchen table, read Habakkuk through, and underlined one line in pencil. *Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables.* One verse above it the prophet stood his watch. *I will stand upon my watch, and set me upon the tower.* Ruth thought of the top two courses of the bell tower at St. Paul, laid by her father's hands, and of a girl of twelve standing on the roof of that tower in 1936.

Eighty miles away, that same Wednesday night, the missionary society of Mount Zion Baptist met in the church basement on the East Side of San Antonio and read its prayer list aloud, as it did every Wednesday. Eli Mayfield's wife had put a name on that list the week Hattie Mayfield was buried in 1964 and had never once let anybody strike it. *Miss Ruth, the lady who was good to Mother Hattie.* Across town, at ten o'clock, Kate Hartmann knelt by her own bed with her husband asleep and said the same name to God. Neither woman knew the other was praying. Ruth knew nothing about either one of them.

She never went back to the Wednesday supper. She came on Sundays only, to the last pew on the right, and went out the door on the final hymn. For thirty-four years she did not say the Mufti's name aloud, or Hitler's, or anything else a brother could call hate. The clippings went into the back of her King James instead of out of her mouth: the hostages let go on the twentieth of January, 1981, after 444 days, and three decades later the Baghdad cathedral where gunmen killed worshippers at Sunday Mass on the last day of October 2010, and the Coptic church in Alexandria bombed at its midnight New Year's service in 2011. She showed them to no one.

Henry's daughter, Joyce Lange, had sat at the second table that night, and she told the story at the altar guild for a generation the way her daddy told it: Aunt Ruth went way out there at a church supper, something from the war, some kind of issue, and after that she quieted down and pulled back from people. In 2006 a newcomer from Austin named Shari Whitcomb heard it over the brass polish and kept it.

---

What she would not say aloud, she put on paper.

In the first week of January, 1980, the new TIME came through her sorting case, nineteen copies for her route, and every copy carried the same face. The cover named the Ayatollah Khomeini Man of the Year for 1979. Ruth cased them before daylight, and at the ninth copy her hands stopped over the pigeonholes.

In the week she turned fifteen, in January of 1939, the same magazine had filled the rack in the drugstore on the square with Adolf Hitler on its cover, named Man of the Year for 1938. Fritz had stood at that rack a long time and said nothing in either language.

Ruth's handwriting was the trouble. Every family on her route had taken mail from that tall slanted hand for thirty-three years, and the postmaster knew it better than his own. On the first Saturday of the year Ruth paid cash at a typewriter shop in Kerrville for a secondhand Royal portable, grey as a dove, with onionskin and carbon paper.

She typed at the kitchen table four nights running, with two fingers: a white sheet on top, then the carbon, then the onionskin, so every word went down twice. The keys struck like hail on a tin roof. Three tries went into the fire. She kept the fourth.

> *To the Editor:*
>
> *On the second of January, 1939, TIME put Adolf Hitler on its cover and named him Man of the Year for 1938. Eight months later his army went into Poland. This month the same magazine has named the Ayatollah Khomeini Man of the Year for 1979.*
>
> *Both men told the world their plans. Hitler put his in a book in 1925. Every night since the fourth of November, the crowd outside our embassy in Tehran has shouted "Death to America" with the Ayatollah's blessing. The biggest news magazine in the world put both men on its cover after both men had told the world their plans.*
>
> *The two stories are one story. In Berlin, on the twenty-eighth of November, 1941, Hitler gave the Grand Mufti of Jerusalem his word: once the German armies reached the south, Germany would have one aim in the Arab lands, to destroy the Jews living there under British protection.*
>
> *In April of 1945 General Patton marched the people of Weimar up the hill to Buchenwald. Every soul in that line swore the camp was news. The smoke had blown over their gardens for eight years.*
>
> *When a man tells you what he means to do, believe him the first time.*
>
> *Lucie, Pedernales County*

On a separate slip she typed one line for the editor alone: *Print this over the name Lucie, or burn it.* The next Saturday she dropped the envelope through the slot at the Kerrville post office, where every R. V. money order had gone out since 1947.

On Thursday, the seventeenth of January, the Martinsburg paper came to the post office in bundles, and Ruth cased her route's share before daylight. Page four, under LETTERS, between a complaint about stock-show parking and a thank-you from the altar guild, stood her words, every one, over the name Lucie. She put her own letter in every box on her route, squared every flag and said nothing.

That night she laid the clipping and the onionskin carbon in a manila folder, wrote LUCIE on the tab in pencil, and slid the folder into the bottom of the footlocker under her iron bed, beside the square of oilcloth. The folder would grow thirty-four years thick.

That same winter Ruth buried Bess under the lavender row along the south fence, and she did not keep another dog for thirty-one years.

---

In January of 1980 Lena lifted a letter from Eli Mayfield out of her fiddle case and handed it across her kitchen table, rosin dust on its corners. Isaiah and his wife Gloria were expecting their first in March, and if the baby was a girl, Isaiah meant to name her Ruth.

*He says it is for the lady who was good to Mama all those years. He is his uncle's spit and image.*

Ruth took every scrap of fabric she owned out of the cedar chest, a yellow from one of Margaret's dresses, a blue from Kate's confirmation dress, a red from a bandana Fritz had worn to the fields, and pieced a Lone Star, eight long diamonds running out from the center like light from a lamp. She sewed every evening of February.

The telephone rang on the second of March. "Seven pounds eleven ounces, Miss Ruth," Eli said. "A girl. They named her Ruth."

At six-thirty Monday morning she set the box on the counter at the post office, addressed to Hackberry Street, before the other carriers arrived. On top of the folded star lay a card with three words.

*For the namesake.*

Ruth never once held the child. She sent ten dollars every Christmas with the same three words for the rest of her life.

---

Ruth ran the route for the last time on the last Saturday of March, 1986, thirty-nine years after her first Monday in the Willys, without one piece of mail in the wrong box.

At box after box a family stood at the end of the drive, and one boy held a sign lettered in shoe polish: THANK YOU MISS RUTH. Every man of them had put out his cigarette before the Jeep came in sight. She handed each family its mail, squared each flag and drove to the next box.

On Monday she woke at half past four in the dark anyway, with no route to run.

Five years later, in 1991, Ruth drove twenty miles up the road to a history conference at the Nimitz Museum in Fredericksburg and sat in the back row while Kazuo Sakamaki, America's first Japanese prisoner of the war, wept over the midget submarine he had run onto a reef off Oahu the week Walter died. Ruth kept her seat until the room was empty.

---

By the nineties the Sunday-night telephone calls from San Antonio carried a new church in them. Kate and Bill had joined one out on Loop 1604, in a building that had been a dinner theater, where people used to eat prime rib and watch a show. Kate said three thousand people came on a Sunday, and the preacher had acted on that same stage before he ever preached from it.

"An actor," Ruth said.

"Mama Ruth, those are the best sermons I ever heard in my life."

Ruth let the line hum awhile. "Sit where you can see his hands, Katie." Then Ruth asked whether the grapefruit tree had set fruit.

---

Eli Mayfield died on a Tuesday in October 1999, in his own bed. He was seventy-two. Isaiah called at six in the morning, and Ruth stood in the kitchen after she hung up until the coffee boiled over on the stove.

At Mount Zion Baptist she sat in the last pew by the door, where she had sat for Hattie thirty-five years before, the only white face in the church. She saw a boy of ten in his brother's cut-down overalls, running along the Pedernales in the summer of 1937 with a balsa airplane held over his head, making the engine noise with his mouth, and Sam turning on the trail to say, *Eli, you're scaring the fish,* and Eli making the noise louder. Eli could not follow his brother into the sky over Germany, so he spent thirty-one years putting engines into airplanes at Kelly Field.

Ruth drove the eighty miles home and cried at the gate of the homeplace in the dark.

For thirty-nine years Ruth had driven every road in Pedernales County, and she knew every one of them down to its last low-water crossing. That night she sat in the truck at the gate with the engine off and could not find the Lord on any of them. *Oh that I knew where I might find him!* Job had looked forward and backward and to the left hand and the right, and Ruth looked down the river road and up it, at the dark barn, and at the cattle guard shining under the headlights she had left burning. "You took the boy who made the engine noise," she said. "Sam's brother. You took the last one who remembered the river." Ruth sat until the headlights went yellow. Then she said the next verse aloud, because Job had said it first and she was too tired to argue with Job. *But he knoweth the way that I take.*

Bill Hartmann died in February of 2005, in his recliner in San Antonio, of a stroke so quick that Kate found him gone before the two cups of decaf in her hands were cold.

Ruth stayed nine days. On the ninth night Kate came out to the back patio, where Ruth sat in her coat looking at a grapefruit tree.

"Thirty-eight years," Kate said. "How did you stand being alone all this time?"

The fruit hung yellow in the dark like lanterns somebody had forgotten to light.

"One day," Ruth said. "Then the next one. That's all anybody ever gets, Katie."

---

After that the house got bigger.

Henry had died in 1987 without taking back a word. Anna went in 1993, Ernie in 1996, Lena in 1998. Ruth was the last Vellmer of the six. She sold the last of the cattle in 2001. At seventy-seven she still climbed the windmill ladder to grease the head.

Then came the doctors, one after another, each with a prescription pad.

Old Dr. Steinhagen had doctored the Vellmers for forty years and kept her on four pills.

In 2009 he retired, and a medical group out of San Antonio bought his practice and sent up a younger doctor two days a week. Every visit lasted eleven minutes. The pills began to pile up behind the four, and anybody watching would have said an old woman was going downhill. Her hip ached in the cold, and he wrote a capsule for the hip. The kitchen floor began to slide under her in the mornings, and he wrote a little yellow pill for the dizziness. She had not slept well since 1944, and he wrote her a pill for sleep.

By fall a long plastic pill box sat on the kitchen counter with fourteen little doors, blue lids for the mornings and yellow for the nights.

Ruth began to lose things. Her car keys turned up in the refrigerator. One afternoon she found herself at the windmill in a housecoat with an oil can in her hand and no idea how long she had stood there. When an English word went out from under her, a German one came up in its place, her mother's kitchen words, *Schüssel, Löffel, Schrank*, and she would hear herself say them on the telephone to Kate and stop.

"Mama Ruth," Kate said one night, "you told me that already. Twice. I'm coming up Saturday."

In November of 2009 Ruth left the stove on under a pot of beans and drove into town. When she got home the kitchen was full of smoke. She stood in the yard and watched the smoke roll out of her father's house, and she knew her river years were done.

Kate found her a white frame house with a deep porch on Elm Street, two blocks off the square, and Ruth moved in the week before Christmas. The house had a beige telephone on the kitchen wall with a line of its own. Ruth still lifted a receiver the way she had lifted one for forty years, listening first for somebody else's breathing. Back in 2005 Kate had pressed a flip-up cell phone into her hands, and Ruth still kept it charged in her pocketbook and answered it for Kate and for nobody else. The computer moved to town with her too. Kate had set it up at the homeplace in the summer of 2000, with a dial-up line that squealed like a calf at a branding, and on Elm Street it sat on the kitchen table, where Ruth read the San Antonio paper, *The Wall Street Journal* and the BBC every morning with her coffee.

The first thing Ruth read on the computer, that summer at the homeplace, was a London obituary for Vera Atkins, born Vera Rosenberg to a Jewish family in Romania, and Ruth knew at last what had lain underneath that clipped Baker Street English. In September of 2010 the BBC carried a short piece from Torquay, in England, where council workers clearing the flat of an old woman found dead alone came on her medals and learned that Eileen Nearne had been a wireless operator in occupied Paris and a prisoner at Ravensbrück. Ruth read the piece twice and sat a long time with her coffee going cold.

Ruth never typed one word into that machine. She kept the homeplace and drove out each day to sit an hour on its porch. Her father's nativity scene went up on the Elm Street porch before she had unpacked a single dish.
