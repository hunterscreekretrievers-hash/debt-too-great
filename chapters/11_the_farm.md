# 11. The Farm

*Congressional Country Club, Bethesda, Maryland; Union Station, Washington; and the Farm, Clinton, Maryland. October 1943 to February 1944*

The fairways were full of tents.

Ruth saw them from the back of an Army truck in the last week of October, pyramidal tents pitched in ranks down a golf course the way Fritz set hay shocks across a field, smoke curling from their stovepipes and frost white on the grass between them. Beyond the tents rose a clubhouse bigger than the courthouse in Martinsburg, a long pale building with rows of tall windows, the kind her father would have circled twice to study the masonry. On the eighteenth green a man in fatigues was blowing up a tree stump.

The stump went up in a brown fountain. The bang reached the truck a half second later and slapped the canvas. Across from Ruth, a dark-haired New Orleans girl named Marguerite clapped both hands over her ears and laughed.

At the gate a captain with a clipboard took her name and gave her another one. Here she was Anna, no last name, and she would not ask anyone's real name or tell anyone her hometown.

"Anna," Ruth said. Anna was her sister's name. She did not tell him that either.

---

The knife was double-edged and narrow, seven inches of blade, a pattern two Shanghai policemen named Fairbairn and Sykes had drawn up, and the man who taught them to use it was a former Marine Raider with a broken nose and one cauliflower ear. The students called him Sergeant Ear behind his back. He faced two women and twenty-three men on a cold fairway beside a canvas dummy stuffed with straw and said that nothing he was about to show them had anything to do with fair.

"You're in a dark street in a country that wants you dead," he said. "If a man gets his hands on you, he'll kill you and go home and eat his supper. You kill him first. Quietly. Then you keep walking."

He showed them where: low in the back, angled up into the kidney, where the shock was so great a man could not scream; the side of the neck; the inside of the thigh, high, where the big artery ran. He drew that last one on the dummy with a grease pencil, a red line from groin to knee.

"He'll bleed out in less time than it takes to say the Lord's Prayer," he said. "I timed it once."

Nobody laughed.

He came at them at night out of the trees by the fourteenth hole and dropped them on the frozen grass one after another. He put Ruth on the grass twice in the first week. The third time she heard his boots in the leaves a half step before he reached her, dropped, turned, caught his knife wrist in her left hand and drove the rubber blade up under his ribs with her right, and she held the wrist so hard that in the beam of his own flashlight his fingers went white and opened.

"Where'd you get a grip like that, Anna?"

"My daddy cut stone."

"Don't tell me that," he said. "I didn't ask for your life story. Do it again."

---

The students shot every morning before breakfast in a ravine below the clubhouse, at plywood silhouettes that popped up from behind brush piles on pulleys. Henry had taught her to shoot a .22 on the Pedernales standing sideways with one eye shut. Here they crouched with both eyes open and pointed the heavy .45 like a finger and fired twice, fast. One silhouette wore a hat with a flower in it, and the first time it came up a boy from Pittsburgh hesitated, and the instructor told him the woman in the hat had just sold him to the Gestapo.

By December Ruth could put two rounds in a plywood face at ten yards before she had decided to fire. Her hands stopped shaking after the first week. The steadiness frightened her worse than the shaking.

In the second week of November the instructors had laid every student's belongings on blankets on the fairway and held up anything that said America: a Philadelphia laundry mark, a pack of Luckies, a wristwatch made in Waterbury, Connecticut. The captain lifted Ruth's bundle of letters by its fiddle string and read the return address on the top envelope, a frame house on the East Side of San Antonio, and studied her face.

"Every one of these hangs you," he said. "Every one hangs whoever wrote it. Before you go over, they go in a locked drawer."

"Yes, sir."

He laid the bundle back on the blanket.

---

Sam's letter came through Lena on the twenty-third of December, forwarded from the Sunday house in Martinsburg to the post-office box in Washington.

She read it standing in the mail line, and then she went to the captain with the clipboard and asked for a twelve-hour pass for Christmas Eve to see a cousin off on a train. The captain looked at her the way Miss Draper had looked at her over the fountain pen in Honolulu, and wrote the pass.

Union Station rose at the foot of Capitol Hill with its white stone arches pale in the low winter sun like a cathedral built for trains. Inside, the great hall went up and up into a coffered ceiling of gold leaf, so high that the noise of the crowd rose into it and never came down, and all around the balcony stone legionaries stood guard behind stone shields held low and square across their hips, looking down at the floor where the war was going home for Christmas. Sailors slept on the benches with their heads on their sea bags. A WAC sergeant cried into a pay telephone. A Marine with one sleeve pinned up waited under the big board, reading the departures as if one of them might take him back where he had lost the arm.

Sam's train was posted thirteen minutes late.

Ruth stood under the board in her good coat and watched the stairs from the lower platforms, where the trains from the South arrived. When the gateman called the Atlanta train, the stairs filled with people. In the middle of them, a head taller than most, came a Black officer in a long olive overcoat with a gold bar on each shoulder and, where the coat hung open, silver wings over his heart. His cap sat square on his head. He climbed the stairs the way he had climbed Enchanted Rock, in long easy strides, his eyes going over the crowd and stopping on her.

He did not know where to put his hands. He had told her so. He took his cap off, and put it back on, and took it off again and held it against his chest.

"You came," Sam said.

"You grew."

"Three years of growing, Ruth. You cut your hair."

"Second Lieutenant Mayfield," she said.

He shut his eyes a moment, there in the middle of Union Station, with the Christmas crowd breaking around the two of them and closing again at their backs.

"Say it again. Nobody in Texas will call me that."

She said it again.

They had fifty-two minutes. He told her about the Jim Crow coach from Atlanta and the dining car where an officer of the United States Army could not buy a sandwich.

"From here north I can sit in any seat on the train," Sam said. "This station's the line, Ruth. You're standing on it."

She wanted to buy him a hot meal. The restaurant off the concourse had white cloths on the tables and a Christmas wreath in the window, and a hostess in a black dress met them at the rope. She looked at Ruth, then at Sam, then at Ruth again.

"I can seat you, miss," the hostess said. "Not the two of you together." **[VERIFY: whether the Union Station restaurant in Washington served Black patrons in 1943; the scene holds either way, since the hostess refuses a mixed table]**

Sam had already turned away from the rope. Ruth stood there with her mouth open and her purse in her hand, and the hostess looked through her toward the next customer, and Ruth followed Sam back out under the board.

"I told you," he said, without heat. "I know it better than you."

They talked fast under the board, the way people talk with a clock running: Lena's fiddle, Eli's height, Margaret and Kate, and the yellow-winged airplane, which he had built again in the barracks at Tuskegee out of balsa from a hobby shop in Montgomery and given to a cadet's little brother.

A military policeman came across the floor toward them through the crowd, a white sergeant in a white helmet liner with MP on his armband and a nightstick on his belt. He did not salute the bar on Sam's shoulder. He looked at Sam the way Fritz Vellmer had looked at him on the river ledge in 1940, and then he looked at Ruth.

"This boy bothering you, miss?"

Ruth opened her mouth.

"No, Sergeant," Sam said, before she could speak. His voice was quiet and very even, the voice from the schoolyard. "The lady asked me the time. I told her." He turned his shoulder to her. "Merry Christmas, ma'am."

He walked ten steps off across the floor and stood alone with his cap under his arm, looking up at the board, a stranger.

The MP looked at Ruth. Ruth had a pass in her purse with a false name on it and orders she could not explain to anyone in a white helmet. One question, one name written in one notebook, and a captain in Bethesda would send her home to the Pedernales by the next train. She knew it the way her feet knew to freeze at a rattler's buzz in the cedar brake.

She said nothing. Ruth Vellmer, who had walked across a schoolyard at thirteen and put herself between Otto Lindauer and Sam Mayfield and taken a fist in the face for it, stood in Union Station with her mouth shut while a man in a white helmet called her friend a boy.

"Merry Christmas, miss," the MP said, and touched his helmet and walked away into the crowd.

---

Ruth found Sam at the top of the stairs to the platform where the Detroit train was boarding, and she took his sleeve and pulled him down three steps, out of the light, into a stairwell of concrete and steam and a bare bulb in a wire cage, with the trains breathing below them.

"You made me a stranger," she said.

"I made you safe."

"I stood there. I didn't say one word for you."

Sam looked down at her in the light of the caged bulb, and the look on his face was older than either of them.

"Now you know," he said. "Now you know the feel of it. Standing there."

She kissed him.

She had climbed every windmill and barn roof and power pole in the Pedernales valley, and she kissed him the same way, without asking anybody, with both hands on the lapels of his Army overcoat. He held very still for one second. Then his cap fell on the step and rolled down two more, and his hands came up and held her by both shoulders, the way they had held her at thirteen when she stepped back into him with her nose broken, and he returned the kiss.

When they stopped, he picked up his cap and turned it in his hands.

"There's states that would hang me for that, Ruth."

"Then stay out of them."

He laughed, low, in the stairwell, the first time she had heard him laugh since the river.

"I'm sorry," he said. "About the sergeant. About walking away from you."

"Don't be sorry. Be careful."

The conductor called the Detroit train. Ruth tore a corner off her streetcar transfer and wrote the number of the post-office box on it with his pencil and folded it into his hand.

"Write me there," she said. "Lena's done enough."

"Every week."

"Every week, Sam Mayfield. I mean every one."

He grinned at that, his mother's words in her mouth, the slow crooked grin that had started, barely, on top of Enchanted Rock. Then he went down the stairs to the train, straight and unhurried, and at the bottom he turned around and looked up at her once.

"I'm going to fly one of those fighters over there," he said. "Then I'm coming home. You watch."

"I'll watch."

She stood in the stairwell until the train was gone.

---

On the first Sunday of the new year she knelt by her cot in the dark tent, out of habit, the way she had knelt by her bed in the limestone house since she was old enough to fold her hands. She began the Lord's Prayer in German, as her mother said it.

*Vater unser, der du bist im Himmel. Geheiligt werde dein Name. Dein Reich komme. Dein Wille geschehe . . .*

She stopped. Thy will be done.

She thought of Walter, who lay now under a stone in Hawaii that said Unknown. She thought of the sailor she had prayed over on December 7 with her hand on his chest while the burned skin came away under her fingers, and of the red grease-pencil line on the straw dummy from groin to knee. She thought of Sam walking ten steps away from her across a marble floor under a ring of stone soldiers with shields, and of her own shut mouth. If all of that was His will, she would not say the words. If none of it was His will, then He had stood by and let it happen, and she had no prayer for that.

Nothing answered. The stove ticked. Somebody in the next tent was snoring.

Ruth rose off her knees and climbed into her cot and pulled the Army blankets up to her chin, and she did not finish the prayer, then or for many years.

---

In January they moved her to the Farm, an old frame farmhouse in the Maryland tobacco country near a crossroads called Clinton, with a smokehouse and a barn gone silver with weather at the end of a dirt lane through bare woods. There they learned what the instructors said would keep them alive longer than a pistol: how to follow a person through a town, and how to know when someone was following them, and how to meet a stranger in a café and know from three words whether he was a friend, and how to write between the lines of a letter in lemon juice and bring the words up brown over a candle.

Above all they learned how to lie.

The lying was taught by a soft-spoken Viennese with a cold pipe clamped in his teeth. He gave Ruth a cover and made her live in it. She became Anna Weber, born in Colmar in Alsace, a nurse's aide with a German mother and a French father. She learned Anna Weber's parish priest and the street where Anna Weber's mother bought bread, and she learned them so well that one morning at breakfast she called the Viennese man by the name of Anna Weber's uncle.

"Good," he said around his pipe. "Now we see if you keep it when it hurts."

The Farm's own Gestapo came for her at two in the morning in the second week of February. **[VERIFY: mock Gestapo arrest and interrogation exercises in OSS training at the Farm, Clinton, Maryland (RTU-11)]**

Two men in grey-green German uniforms pulled her off the cot with a flashlight in her face, shouting in German, and marched her barefoot across the frozen barnyard into the cellar under the farmhouse, a stone room with a dirt floor and one bare bulb, and stood her against the wall.

She stood eleven hours. They asked her name, her mother's name, her priest's name, the street where her mother bought bread, again and again, in German and French and English, and when she answered they slapped her with an open hand hard enough to make her ears ring. When her knees buckled they threw a bucket of well water over her and stood her up again. Her nightgown froze stiff at the hem. Her feet went white, then blue, then past feeling. Sometime in the morning one of them bent her left little finger back toward her wrist, slowly, until the knuckle creaked, and asked her in English where she had been born.

She looked at the bulb and thought of Fritz on the scaffold with his trowel, setting one stone on another, plumb and level, all morning in the August heat, never hurrying. He set one stone. Then he set the next stone.

"Colmar," Ruth said, in German. "Ich bin in Colmar geboren."

At one in the afternoon the Viennese came down the cellar steps with a blanket and a cup of coffee and closed her fingers around the cup because she could not close them herself.

"Eleven hours," he said. "You never broke cover. I will tell you the truth now, Anna. My men stopped at slaps and water and one finger. The real ones will start where my men stopped."

Ruth drank the coffee. The coffee burned her mouth, and she drank it anyway.

"When do I go?" she said.

---

On the last night at the Farm, around the kitchen stove, a lanky Virginian instructor told them about the woman with the wooden leg, the way men tell ghost stories at a deer camp. A Baltimore girl, he said, who had run agents for the British out of Lyon with the Gestapo putting her face on wanted posters, and who had walked over the Pyrenees into Spain in the snow on a wooden leg she had given a man's name.

"The Gestapo called her the limping lady," the Virginian said. "Her name is Virginia Hall. I'm told she means to return to France." **[VERIFY: the "limping lady" nickname for Virginia Hall and her status (SOE, joining OSS in March 1944) in February 1944]**

"On one leg," Marguerite said.

"On one leg."

Ruth sat with the coffee cup in her good hand and the splinted little finger held out stiff from the others, and listened. Out in the winter woods a screech owl was calling, a long falling whinny like a horse in a far pasture. In the morning she would go to New York and a ship, and after the ship, London, and she would carry the bundle of letters tied in Lena's fiddle string as far as they would let her carry it.
