# 29. Playoffs

*November 2013. Waidner Field, U.S. 281 and a stadium in San Antonio.*

The Mustangs finished the regular season eight and two and second in district, and on the Monday after the last game somebody climbed the Martinsburg water tower in the dark and painted *PLAYOFFS 2013* across the tank in green letters four feet tall. The city manager called it a disgrace at the Mustang Grill register, then drove out and sat in his truck under the tower for ten minutes, studying the letters like a man reading his own name in the paper.

Nobody in town under thirty had seen a Mustang playoff team. The last one had played in 1984, when the Mustang Grill still had a cigarette machine by the door.

---

The bi-district game was at home against a team from down toward Hondo that had beaten the Mustangs 48 to 6 the year before and crowed about it all week on the internet.

Ruth got to Waidner Field at five-thirty because Dixie Beck had asked for her.

The concession stand sat under the home bleachers, a cinder-block room painted green that smelled of popcorn oil, burned coffee and the vinegar Dixie used on every surface. Frito bags stood in rows of ten like ammunition boxes in a supply tent. Dixie ran the room with a whistle on a lanyard that had never once touched her lips.

"Baby, you're on nachos," she said to a freshman girl. "Baby, you're on the drinks, and if you give anybody a free Coke because he's cute, I'll know it." She turned. "Miss Ruth, thank God. Can you count money?"

"I counted money for the government," Ruth said. "Once."

"Then you're on the box."

So Ruth sat by the cash box with Honey under the counter and made change, Frito pies at two-fifty, coffee a dollar for the old men whose knees would not take the bleachers, and the whole town came through the line. Inge Lochte and Doris Ahrens came through together. Doris laid her exact change on the counter and kept her hand on it.

"Ruth, we were wrong," she said. "That woman from Austin sat in my front room and ate my kolaches and told us it would help you."

"I know what she told you," Ruth said. "Get your Frito pies before they go cold."

Inge was crying before she reached the jalapeños. Behind them Harlan Moellering held out a five-dollar bill, his hat brim crushed in his other fist.

"Frito pie," he said. "I chained my east gate last week, Miss Ruth. Those surveyors won't cross my place again."

Ruth made his change. "Enjoy the game, Harlan."

He took the paper boat and walked off without the plastic spoon, and Dixie said, "Baby, that poor man's going to eat Fritos with his fingers all night. He's been sick over August since August."

By kickoff people stood three deep along the end-zone fence, and moths that should have been dead by November spun in the lights like ashes over a brush fire. When the Mustangs broke through the paper banner, the roar set dogs barking on every farm within a mile.

Ruth watched the first half through the service window, between customers, the way a woman watches a storm through a kitchen screen.

The visitors were big. They scored on their first two possessions, and the old men along the fence went quiet the way old men go quiet when they have watched the same thing happen for forty years.

Then the Mustangs did what Coach Winslow had made them do every Tuesday since August. They ran DeShawn Hollins straight up the middle behind Tyler Kott, and they ran him there again, and again, and again. Four yards. Three yards. Six. Two. Halfway through the second quarter the whole town saw the reason, when the big defensive tackle across from Tyler stood up after a play with his hands on his hips and his mouth open, sucking air like a horse run too hard, and failed to get back down in his stance in time. DeShawn went through the hole where the tackle should have been and was not touched for forty-one yards.

The Mustangs scored twice before the half. At 14 to 14, Dixie grabbed Ruth by both shoulders.

"Baby, go watch your boys. I'll take the box."

"You'll lose count."

"I'll lose count," Dixie said. "Go."

Up in the stands people made room for her on the fifth row, and a rancher she had not spoken to in ten years over a fence line on the river road handed her a stadium cushion without a word.

The lead changed hands twice, and each time the old men along the fence shifted their weight from one bad knee to the other. The visitors kicked a field goal. Caleb Waidner hit Brody Vossberg on a tight end screen, and Brody carried two defenders into the end zone on his back. The other team scored again. With four minutes left the Mustangs were behind 24 to 21 and had the ball on their own thirty-one.

Sam Mayfield had tried to explain football to her once, the fall they were fifteen, drawing the plays with a willow stick on a sandbar of the Pedernales, and she had listened to his voice and not his words. Seventy-four years later the boys on bus No. 7 had finished the lesson, and now she could read the numbers.

First and ten. A run for two.

Second and eight. Incomplete, the ball sailing over a receiver's hands into the dark past the lights.

Third and eight from the thirty-three. Caleb stood in the shotgun, and Coach Winslow spat a mouthful of shells and held up two fingers. Caleb took the snap and dropped back, the whole pocket caved in front of him like a dirt bank in a flood, and he ran out the right side with a linebacker clawing at his jersey and threw the ball across his body, the one throw Coach had forbidden him a hundred times. DeShawn caught the pass at the forty-five, cut back, and was tackled at the visitors' thirty-eight.

First down, Mustangs. The bleachers shook under Ruth's feet.

They ran it behind Tyler four straight times. On second and goal from the four, DeShawn walked into the end zone standing tall. Luis Garza kicked the extra point, the Mustangs led 28 to 24 with fifty-one seconds left, and Luis turned to look up into the stands.

His parents were not there. His face went flat for half a second before he jogged to the sideline.

The other quarterback threw three passes in fifty-one seconds. The last one went to the end zone, high and arcing through the lights, and a Mustang sophomore safety named Kurt Hoerster, who weighed a hundred and forty pounds in his pads, jumped and knocked it down with the tips of his fingers and fell full length on his back in the grass. The clock read zeros.

The town poured past Ruth over the rail and onto the field like the river over a low-water crossing. The band played the fight song again, worse.

The boys found Lyle first, in his brown jacket at the end of the stands, and Tyler Kott and two linemen lifted him off his feet. Lyle's hat fell in the grass. He laughed with his whole face, a sight no boy on the team had ever seen, and when they set him down he put the hat back on at exactly the same angle.

---

The area-round game was the next week, at a neutral stadium in Kerrville.

Behind No. 7, down Main and past the courthouse square, came the town: pickups with green-and-gold streamers on the antennas, a cattle trailer with twenty kids in blankets on hay bales, the volunteer fire truck with its lights going. The headlights stretched back over the hills like a rosary somebody had dropped across the county.

"Lyle," Ruth said, "how many?"

Lyle checked the mirror. "More than a man can count from behind the wheel."

Halfway down the aisle, three boys leaned over a seat to read Luis Garza's phone. The team they had beaten the week before had a page on the internet, and somebody on it had called the Mustangs a word Lyle would not have allowed on his bus.

Ruth turned. "Put that phone in your pocket, Luis."

He put the phone in his pocket, and the boys looked at her.

"One day somebody will put a word on you that isn't true," she said. "Before you believe it, find out who's being paid for saying it."

"Nobody's paid for that, Miss Ruth," Tyler Kott said. "Some kid typed it."

"Then that one's free," Ruth said. "The ones that cost you will come from people who draw a salary."

DeShawn tugged out one earbud. "That kid thinks he's in the right, though."

"Every mob I ever saw thought so," Ruth said. "In November of 1938 the papers in Berlin said the synagogues burned because honest Germans rose up on their own in righteous anger. The fire trucks stood by and watered the houses next door."

Caleb sat alone near the middle with his face against the window. His father was in the state prison at Huntsville for a 2007 robbery, and Caleb had told her in October that the man listened to Mustang games on a transistor radio whenever the weather carried the signal that far. The letter in his helmet said so. Caleb had never written a word in return.

Honey got up from the aisle at Ruth's feet, walked back to Caleb's seat and lay down across his shoes. He put his hand on her back and kept it there all the way to Kerrville.

The Mustangs won that game too, 31 to 17, in a cold wind that blew the punts sideways. Brody Vossberg had two sacks and a fumble recovery. His father stood at the fence the whole fourth quarter screaming at him through cupped hands. The boy never once turned his head toward the fence. During a timeout Teddy Rausch pulled Brody's helmet to check a cut on his chin, and Ruth saw the same old tiredness settle on Teddy's face that she had seen on the bus in August, before he taped the gauze down and slapped the boy back onto the field.

After the game, in the parking lot, Brody's father caught him by the arm beside the bus. Ruth stood on the bus steps. She saw the man's hand close on the boy's bicep hard enough to whiten the knuckles, and she saw Brody, who was six foot one and could have broken his father in half, stand there and take it.

Honey came down the bus steps past Ruth without a command. She walked up to Mr. Vossberg and stood a foot from his leg with her head low, and she growled. Ruth had never once heard her growl. The sound came out of the dog's chest so low a man felt it in his shins before his ears caught it, like a diesel idling on a distant road.

Mr. Vossberg let go of the arm.

"Your dog's got a problem," he said to Ruth.

"No," Ruth said, from the top of the steps. "Not my dog."

He met her grey eyes for a second, then walked to his truck. Brody climbed onto the bus without a word and sat in the last seat. Honey followed him and lay in the aisle beside his seat.

---

The regional game was the Saturday night after Thanksgiving, in a San Antonio stadium that held more people than Martinsburg and the three towns around it, against a school with three times the enrollment that had not lost in two years.

"Sport," Coach Winslow said to Caleb, "they put their pants on one leg at a time."

"Yes, sir," Caleb said. "They just got more legs."

Ruth sat in the front row in Lyle's bus blanket. The air smelled of rain on concrete and diesel from the freeway. Kate had driven across town with me, and the two of us sat four rows behind her. A booster mother had pushed a Mustang cap onto my head.

The Mustangs lost.

They lost the way good teams lose to better ones, late and by inches. At 21 to 21 the bigger school drove eleven plays, nine of them runs, and scored with 1:50 left. Twenty-eight to twenty-one.

The Mustangs got the ball back on their own twenty-two.

A swing pass to DeShawn for nine, a sneak behind Tyler Kott, first down at the thirty-five.

First and ten. Incomplete. The clock stopped at 1:12.

Honey rose against Ruth's knee, her odd ear standing straight.

Second and ten. Caleb threw down the left sideline, and the receiver caught the ball and was knocked out of bounds at midfield. 0:58.

First and ten. A draw to DeShawn for six. Timeout, Mustangs. 0:41.

Second and four from the other team's forty-four. Caleb dropped back, nobody was open, and he ran for it, straight up the middle, nineteen yards before two of them drove him into the turf at the twenty-five. Timeout, Mustangs. 0:29.

Ruth was on her feet with no memory of rising. Behind her, all along the lower deck, the people of Martinsburg stood in the drizzle, a long wall of green and gold, and not one of them took a seat.

First and ten from the twenty-five. Incomplete. 0:24.

Second and ten. Caleb hit Brody over the middle for eleven yards, and Brody was dragged down at the fourteen with no timeouts left and the clock running. Caleb sprinted to the line waving his arms, the boys threw themselves into their stances, and he spiked the ball into the turf with three seconds left.

Second and ten from the fourteen. One play.

By the kicking net Luis Garza stood with his helmet in his hand, looking up, and Ruth followed his eyes to the end of the third row: a small man in a work jacket and a slight woman with a shawl over her head, holding each other's arms. They had driven ninety minutes after dark on the interstate. Luis pulled on his helmet.

Coach Winslow kept the kicking team on the bench. A field goal would not tie the game. He sent in a play, and Caleb took the snap and rolled right, and he threw for the corner of the end zone where DeShawn was running with a defender on his hip.

The ball climbed into the lights and seemed to stop there, a brown spot against the rain.

The defender got a hand on it.

The ball fell on the green turf, end over end, two feet beyond DeShawn's reach, and lay there.

For a few seconds the Mustang side of the big stadium went as quiet as a church at the end of a funeral.

The Mustangs did not fall down, and Ruth carried that detail for the rest of her life. Some of them knelt. DeShawn lay on his back in the end zone with his arms over his face mask until Tyler Kott walked over and pulled him to his feet. Then the boys walked back to their own sideline, slowly, and when they got there they turned around and looked up into the stands.

The town was still standing.

Nobody had left the stands. A few hundred people from a town most of San Antonio could not have found on a map stood in the drizzle and clapped, steadily, for a team that a year before had not won a single game. Ruth clapped with them, and Honey sat with her odd ear cocked and her tail sweeping the wet concrete.

Then Tyler Kott looked up and saw her in the front row, and he took off his helmet, and so did the rest of them, one at a time, until all of them were standing on the sideline bareheaded in the rain, looking up at the old woman in the long coat and the yellow dog.

Ruth's hands had no idea what to do with themselves. She lifted one, a little, the way a woman waves to a troop train pulling out of the depot.

Behind her, she heard Kate say to me, "What on earth has she been doing up here all fall?"

Nobody answered her. When Ruth turned around, I was looking at her the way I had looked at her once before, on a San Antonio curb in 1968 with my new diploma in my hand, as if I had just understood that I would never know the whole of her.

---

The bus rode home up U.S. 281 in the dark. Teddy walked the aisle with tape and a bag of ice, touching a shoulder here and there. Coach Winslow sat across from Ruth with an empty sunflower-seed bag crumpled in his fist.

Somewhere past Blanco he spoke to the windshield.

"Miss Ruth."

"Coach."

"Best season of my life," he said. "Lost it anyway."

"I lost a few of my best seasons myself," Ruth said.

He looked over at her then. In the green glow from the dashboard his face looked older than forty-nine. "You ever going to tell me where you counted money for the government?"

"No," Ruth said.

He laughed once, quietly, and faced the road again.

When the bus came over the last hill, every porch light on Main was burning, and all four banks of lights at Waidner Field, and the pickups lined up in the lot honked the way they had honked in October, for a team coming home with a loss.

Ruth was the last passenger to leave the bus. On the bottom step she looked out at the boys walking into their mothers' arms, and at Luis Garza beside a dented Corolla with his father's hand on the back of his neck.

"Lyle," she said. "Thank you for the ride."

"Next year, Miss Ruth," Lyle said.

"Next year," she said, and stepped down onto the cold asphalt, and Honey stepped down beside her.

After that night in San Antonio, Ruth Vellmer never saw another football game.
