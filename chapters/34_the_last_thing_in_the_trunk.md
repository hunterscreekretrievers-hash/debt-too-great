# 34. The Last Thing in the Trunk

*Christmas morning, 2026. The cabin on the Vellmer homeplace.*

The fire is nearly dead. One coal at the back of the grate breathes red when the cold comes down the chimney, and the lamp on the mantel has burned so low the glass is going black.

Ben sleeps in Lily's lap, under the roof where a girl of eight once shook all night against Ruth's ribs and made no sound. Lily fakes sleep so nobody sends her to bed. Daniel snores in the recliner. Ruthie sits on the hearth in Gloria's old afghan.

Marcus has scrolled for an hour. Now he lays the phone face down on the quilt, between the nurse's cap and the *Life* magazine.

"Grandpa."

"Listening."

"I checked everything." He says it the way a boy confesses he took the truck without asking. "Chester Nimitz. Dorie Miller. The *Oklahoma*. The boy with the laundry truck. Violette Szabo. The ten Boom house. Ravensbrück. Eighty-four Avenue Foch. On March twenty-fourth, 1945, the Tuskegee Airmen flew all the way to Berlin with the bombers and shot down three German jets, and the Army gave the whole group a Distinguished Unit Citation for it. Every name. Every single one."

He holds up one finger. "Every name but one, Grandpa. I found the mission and the jets and the four boys squinting by the red tail. The Red Tails have a roll, and I read the whole thing twice. No Samuel Mayfield is on it. The Army wrote down every other man. Where did the Army write down Uncle Sam?"

Lily's eyes open wide.

No answer comes out of the old man's mouth. His two hands go up to his collar instead.

The grey cardigan has stayed buttoned to the throat all night. Since supper Ruthie has ridden her daddy about it, told him he looks like Mister Rogers, asked was he cold with a fire going. Each time he has told her he is cold. He is not cold.

Old fingers are slow on the top button. The second goes easier. A thumb hooks the little ball chain at the throat and draws it up out of the undershirt, and the thing on the end of it rises into the last of the lamplight and turns, slow, like a leaf on a spider's thread.

A dog tag. United States Army. A strip of stamped steel a little smaller than a matchbook, rectangular with the corners rounded off, a notch cut into one end and a hole punched through the other for the chain. The letters stand up out of the metal in raised rows. A coat hem, a girl's tongue, a camp seam and twelve years against an old man's breastbone have worn the tops of them smooth. SAMUEL J MAYFIELD runs across the first line. Under it comes O-826174, the O in front for an officer's number, then T42 43 for the years of his tetanus shots, then the letter O for his blood type. Down in the bottom corner, alone, sits the letter P, for Protestant.

The metal is warm.

Lily whispers, "Oh," the way a person says it in church.

Marcus leans close. The tag lies on the flat of the old man's palm, and the boy reads it without touching it.

"Mayfield," he says. "Samuel."

His lips move over the number. That boy has memorized it already, and before the sun clears the hills he will have typed it into something.

"Uncle Sam's," Ruthie says to the fire. "Daddy. How long have you had that?"

"Twelve years. Since a parking meter on Main Street."

"The handkerchief," Lily whispers. "At the will."

"The handkerchief."

Marcus has not moved. "How did Miss Ruth get Uncle Sam's dog tag?"

"He gave it to her on their wedding night."

Nobody in the cabin breathes. Ruthie's empty cup slides off her knee and rolls a half turn on the hearthstone.

"Wedding," Ruthie says.

"In July of 1944, at night, with the shutters closed, a French pastor married a Black fighter pilot from the Pedernales to a white girl from the same river, in the Protestant temple of a village in the hills of the Drôme. The French Protestants call a church a temple. Afterward the two of them climbed to a stone barn above the village, with lavender hung to dry from the rafters. Every soldier wore two tags. Sam kept one for the Army. The other one he laid in her open hand."

The rest of that road came out of the diary tonight, in pieces, in Lily's careful voice. Laid end to end, the pieces run this way. Ruth carried that tag in the toe of her stocking up the stairs at 84 Avenue Foch, sewed it into the hem of her grey coat that first night, and after the cigarettes she held the coat in her lap with her fingers on the tag through the cloth. She carried it under her tongue for a week at Ravensbrück and kept her jaw shut when a guard split her lip against it. She sewed it into the seam of her striped dress with a thread pulled from a blanket, and it rode against her leg through roll calls in the snow and the December Betsie died and the February the shots came from behind the wall. On the ferry to Malmö she tore the seam out whole, closed her fist on the tag, and did not open that hand for two days.

Lily looks down at the strip of grey-and-blue cloth on her knee, at the small, uneven stitches along one edge she took for wear, and sets two fingers on them.

"Here's the seam," she says.

"The very seam."

She holds the strip a long time. Then she slides out from under her brother, lays his head down on the quilt as gently as a woman sets bread in a pan, and kneels at the open trunk to put the cloth back where it lived. The trunk has been empty since ten o'clock. Her hand goes flat to the bottom boards to smooth the strip into place, and her fingers stop.

"Something's stuck to the floor of it."

A square of oilcloth lies tight against the boards, gone the same brown as the wood, so that a person reaching in by moonlight would never see it. Lily works a fingernail under one corner. The oilcloth comes up with a dry tearing sound, and inside waits a single sheet of heavy paper, folded twice and dark along the folds.

She brings it to the lamp in both hands.

The writing is an old French copperplate, in ink faded to the color of a dry oak leaf. Across the top, in the same hand, runs *Église Réformée de France*, and under it the name of a village in the hills east of the Rhône.

"*Mariage*," Lily reads, and raises her eyes. "Marriage."

Lower down, two names. Marcus reads them, because his French has come from a phone all night and Lily's has come from nowhere.

"*Samuel Mayfield, aviateur américain, né au Texas.*" He swallows. "*Ruth Marie Vellmer, née au Texas.*"

A brown water stain has crept across the date, and the ink shows through it the way a stone shows through shallow water: *le 29 juillet 1944.* Under the names the two of them signed, his hand steady, hers new at its own name, and below them the pastor signed in a hand that runs off toward the edge of the sheet like a man in a hurry, and beside his name two witnesses signed, one with the name Marthe, one in big careful capitals like a man who does not often hold a pen. At the very bottom the pastor wrote out a single verse of Scripture in French.

Marcus works it through his phone, word by word, and reads the English out slow.

"Where you go, I will go. Where you live, I will live. Your people will be my people, and your God my God."

"Ruth in the Bible said that," Lily says. "She said it to her husband's mother."

Nobody answers her. Ruthie closes her eyes.

Marcus sets the paper on the quilt as if it might break. "The racist grandmother," he says, slow, the way a boy reads a new word off the spelling list. "She was married to a Tuskegee Airman."

"She was his wife, son. One more thing, and then you've heard it all."

The letter comes out of the inside pocket of the grey cardigan, folded in thirds, soft as cloth at the creases. The lamp is too low to read by, and the old man does not need the lamp.

> *Isaiah,*
>
> *Eric has read you the will. Here is what I left out of it.*
>
> *On a night in July of 1944 I married your Uncle Sam. A French pastor married us in his church in the hills of the Drôme, with the shutters closed and two farm people standing up with us. His wife lent me her ring. A guard at Ravensbrück took it. Sam gave me his tag that night. The tag is yours now.*
>
> *We had three days. Then the Germans had me, and Sam went back to his airplanes. He was killed over Germany in March of 1945. Your grandmother wrote me so in Sweden.*
>
> *I told nobody. In Texas a marriage like ours was a felony until 1967, and I would not set that load on your grandmother. After the law changed I still said nothing. My silence after 1967 was pride, and I will answer for it.*
>
> *The money orders to your grandmother were mine. So was your scholarship. A wife sees to her husband's mother and to his people. Nobody owes me a dime.*
>
> *I leave you the land because you are family. You were family before you were born.*
>
> *My diary is at the bottom of the trunk. Read it when you are ready, or let somebody read it to you.*
>
> *Ruth*
>
> *I signed my married name once, on a church paper in France. I would like to sign it one more time.*
>
> *Ruth Vellmer Mayfield*

The letter goes back into its thirds.

For a long moment nobody in the cabin moves. A coal pops and rolls out onto the hearthstones and lies there glowing, and nobody reaches for the poker. The wind finds the gap under the door and lifts the corner of the rag rug. Marcus is the one who finally rises. He nudges the coal back into the grate with the toe of his boot and stays crouched by the fire a long while, his back to the room, his shoulders square as a pallbearer's.

"Boaz knew a woman like that," the old man says. "He told the first Ruth so in a barley field at Bethlehem. *It hath fully been shewed me, all that thou hast done unto thy mother in law since the death of thine husband.*"

Ruthie raises her face out of the afghan, doing arithmetic the way her mother did it over a bank statement. "Uncle Sam was Grandpa Eli's brother."

"Sam was Eli's older brother."

"So Miss Ruth was your aunt."

"My aunt by marriage. Your great-aunt, baby girl."

Lily turns the words over, looking for the back side of them. "Then she's our great-great-aunt."

"By marriage."

"Every Christmas card," Ruthie says. "Ten dollars. *For the namesake.*" Her voice breaks and mends. "Thirty-nine years she drove the mail in this county, and not one soul knew her married name. She was family the whole time."

"The whole time."

Marcus studies the tag. Then he touches it with one finger, on the P, and folds his hand shut.

"You asked me where the Army wrote him down, son," the old man says. "The Army wrote Sam Mayfield on this tag, on the telegram Grandma Hattie opened in April of 1945, and on every page of the file it kept on him in the government's military records building outside St. Louis, and on the twelfth of July, 1973, a fire on the sixth floor of that building burned four of every five Army files from Sam's war, the Army Air Forces in with them, and to this day nobody holds a complete list of the lost files. The roll you read twice is seven years younger than you, the first roll of the Tuskegee pilots a person could search on a telephone, put together by historians out of the paper still standing and published in the fall of 2018, seventy-three years after the jets. The men who built it call it complete. In 1949 a military board called the *Oklahoma*'s unknowns non-recoverable, too, and in 2015 the government dug them up out of the Punchbowl and started giving them back their names."

Marcus opens his hand and looks at the folded letter. "Miss Ruth wanted to sign her married name one more time, Grandpa. The Martinsburg paper can print the whole name, Ruth Vellmer Mayfield, with Uncle Sam's beside it, and this time the county can read both."

The old man looks at the boy a long while.

"The Monday after Christmas, son, you and I will carry the marriage paper and the tag to Main Street ourselves, and the town can carry the rest."

The tag goes back against my chest, outside the undershirt this time. The cardigan stays open.

Lily folds the marriage paper into its oilcloth again and kneels at the trunk to lay it back where Miss Ruth laid it. Her palm presses the bottom board, and the board gives under her hand like a loose stair tread.

"Grandpa. The floor moves."

Getting down on one knee takes an old man a while. The bottom of that footlocker is a false floor of pine set an inch above the true one, fitted so close to the sides that a knife blade would hardly find the seam. A courier in the Drôme rolled her messages into the hollow handlebar of a bicycle. A courier in Texas built her trunk the same way.

The board comes up on Lily's fingernails. Beneath it lies a manila folder gone soft and brown, tied shut with grocery string, and beside the folder a stack of typed pages pressed between two shirt cardboards. Across the folder, in the same small square capitals as the margin of her Bible, runs one word.

LUCIE.

Lily unties the string. Inside lie newspaper clippings, dozens of them, each one pinned to an onionskin carbon of the same letter, typed on a machine whose small *e* drops a hair below the line. Every clipping comes from the Martinsburg paper. Every letter is signed the same way: *Lucie, Pedernales County.*

"Who's Lucie?" Ruthie asks.

"The Germans called her Lucie," Marcus says. "That was her name in France."

Clipped inside the front cover is a leaf torn out of one of her plate notebooks, with a date across the top in the tall slanted hand and two lines under it in pencil.

*Thursday, Nov. 21, 2013. T. Leland telephoned. Cairo has asked Kew for Lucie.*

Thirty-three days after Miss Ruth wrote that line, a white Dodge came up Elm Street with its lights dark.

The carbon at the bottom of the stack is the oldest. Marcus holds his phone's light over it, and Lily reads it out in her careful church voice. The clipping pinned to it is dated Thursday, January 17, 1980, seven weeks after the Wednesday supper.

Lily reads the signature, and nobody in the cabin says a word.

Kader. The old man reads the name twice. El-Maadi found refuge in Egypt. Samir Darwish was born in Alexandria, Egypt, in 1982. Her page holds a boy's name and nothing more, and the old man lays it face down on the quilt and keeps the rest of that thought behind his teeth.
