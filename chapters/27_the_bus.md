# 27. The Bus

*Martinsburg, Texas, August and September 2013*

Waidner Field sat at the bottom of Elm Street, four blocks below Ruth's house, in a limestone hollow where the creek bent around a mott of pecans. From her porch swing in August, with the cicadas grinding in the hackberries like a hundred little sawmills, Ruth could hear the whistles.

The two-a-days had started the first week of August, at seven in the morning and six in the evening, and the coach's voice came up the hill behind the whistles like a rifle shot across open water.

On the evening after the hearing Ruth clipped the leash on Honey and walked down the hill.

---

The Mustangs had gone 0 and 10 in 2012. Their coach had resigned in December, and in March the school board had hired a man from Lubbock named Ray Winslow, forty-nine, twenty years in the Air Force, a good many of them in France, and a strength coach in the years since his discharge. The county paper ran his photograph on the sports page, a square man with a grey crew cut and a neck as wide as his head, under the headline WINSLOW: "WE'LL HIT SOMEBODY."

Ruth stood at the chain-link fence at the corner of the end zone, with Honey sitting against her left leg, and watched him make them hit somebody.

He had them in two rows on the goal line, and at the whistle the two boys at the front came into each other with a sound like two sacks of feed dropped off a tailgate. The boy who got driven back went again, and again, until his legs learned their trade. The coach walked between the rows with his hands in his back pockets, spitting sunflower shells into the grass.

"That's it, sport. That's it. Again, sport."

Under a canopy by the water table, a heavyset man with a roll of white tape on his wrist and a cigarette tucked behind his ear filled paper cups from an orange cooler. In an hour and a half he never once lit the cigarette.

After practice the coach walked to the fence, still spitting shells.

"Ma'am. Something I can do for you?"

"I'd like to ride the bus," Ruth said.

He took the sunflower seeds out of his mouth.

"The team bus."

"To the games and home again."

"Ma'am, I don't believe we've met."

"Ruth Vellmer. I live up the hill on Elm Street." She put her long fingers through the chain-link. "I've lived in Pedernales County eighty-nine years, Coach, except for the war, and I have never once been to a football game. My eyes won't let me drive at night anymore. I'll sit where I'm told and I won't say a word."

From the water table the heavyset man laughed out loud.

"Teddy," the coach said without turning his head.

"Coach, that's Miss Ruth," Teddy called. "You just lost that argument, and nobody's told you yet."

---

The athletic director asked the superintendent, who asked the school's insurance company, which sent a form. Dixie Beck carried it up to Ruth's porch in a green-and-gold booster T-shirt.

Dixie had met Jody Beck on the Ferris wheel at the county fair at sixteen and married him at twenty in St. Paul with her hair teased high enough to need its own pew. She had run the front office at the Pedernales Electric Co-op since the summer after high school. Dixie Beck knew every meter in the county, who paid late and why, and which widow on which back road had quietly been carried through a hard winter.

"Baby, I need your name on this," Dixie said. "Says if the bus goes off a bridge you won't sue anybody." She squinted at Honey, who lay across Ruth's feet with her white blaze to the sky. "Lord, she's pretty. Does she bite?"

"Only when somebody has it coming."

"Then she'll suit this town fine." Dixie sat down on the swing and fanned herself with the form. "Baby, I have sold eleven thousand Frito pies for those boys and watched them lose every Friday of last fall. You know what they need?"

"A better offensive line."

Dixie laughed so hard the swing chains rattled against the hooks. "They need somebody to see them," she said. "Somebody grown who isn't yelling."

Ruth signed the form on top of the envelope already in her lap, the one with the co-op's name in the corner.

Dixie saw it and groaned. "Baby, don't tell me. You're calling the office Monday."

"I'm telling you now." Ruth unfolded the bill. "Henry strung wire up this river in 1939 for a co-op the people owned, and when the line reached our kitchen we stood and stared at one bulb like the star over Bethlehem. Now look. Three lines of charges before I burn a single light. Last year natural gas sold for the lowest price since 1999, and power in this country still costs more than it did in 2006. The gas gets cheaper, and the light gets dearer."

"Baby, I don't set the rates. I just answer for them." Dixie fanned harder. "I answered that phone in 2007, too, the week the members had to sue to find out about the general manager's two-million-dollar retirement plan. Three years later a jury convicted the man of theft."

"Theft," Ruth said. "Henry would have climbed the pole and cut the wire himself."

"My Travis says one day nobody'll need a wire. He's got a picture of that Tesla car taped inside his locker."

"Tesla." Ruth folded the bill in thirds. "The man that car is named for died alone in Room 3327 of the Hotel New Yorker in January of 1943. Two days later the government's Office of Alien Property Custodian took his papers and trunks, and Washington sent a professor from MIT named John G. Trump to go through them."

Dixie stopped fanning herself with the form.

"Professor Trump wrote that the work was speculative. I worked for the government in Washington later that year, and men in the service I joined talked. Nobody carts off a dead man's trunks because they think they're full of nothing."

"Baby, you sound like my Jody after two beers."

"Your Jody's right more often than you tell him."

---

Lyle Mosher had driven Mustang buses for thirty years and had never raised his voice on one.

He was fifty-one, tall and a little round-shouldered from the big wheel, with a long quiet face like a deacon in an old photograph and the same brown canvas jacket in every season. A little brass cross on a bead chain hung from the mirror over the windshield.

No. 7 was a 1999 Blue Bird with green vinyl seats patched with duct tape. On the last Friday in August Lyle stood at the bottom of its steps and watched Ruth Vellmer come down the sidewalk with a pocketbook over one arm and a yellow Lab on a leash.

"Miss Ruth," he said.

"Lyle."

The two had known each other forty years without trading a hundred words. He held her pocketbook while she climbed the steps, then pointed to the first seat on the left, behind his own.

"Heater only works on my side," he said. "Come November, you'll want that seat."

"It's ninety-eight degrees, Lyle."

"Yes ma'am. November'll be here directly."

She took the seat. Honey lay down in the aisle at her feet, chin on her paws, the ear that never lay flat standing up like the flag on a rural mailbox.

---

The boys came out of the field house at a quarter past three in their game pants and green jerseys, and they stopped on the bottom step one after another like steers smelling a new pen.

"There's a old lady on the bus."

"There's a *dog* on the bus."

"Get on the bus," Coach Winslow said behind them, and they got on the bus.

They filed past her, thirty-four boys ducking their heads, smelling of Icy Hot, new tape and cheap body spray. One small one said, "Ma'am," and turned red to the ears.

Ruth unclipped the leash. "Go see them," she said, low, and Honey rose.

The dog went down the aisle and smelled every one of them, shoes, knees, hands, working the bus the way a cutting horse reads a herd, one head at a time.

At the fourth seat on the left a lean boy with a quarterback's arms sat alone against the window. A letter was folded inside his helmet, the white edge of it showing above the padding. Honey smelled his hand, sat down in front of him and looked at him a long time. Then she lay down across his cleats. The boy looked at the dog and did not move his feet.

"Caleb," Teddy Rausch said low from the seat across from Ruth. "Waidner. Quarterback. The field carries his granddaddy's granddaddy's name. His daddy's in Huntsville."

Halfway back, the center, a big rawboned boy with a farm neck, was writing on the tape around his left wrist with a Sharpie. When the dog reached him he capped the marker and held his hand down to her, and she licked the tape, and he laughed once, the way a man laughs when somebody presses on a bruise.

"Kott," Teddy said. "Tyler. His brother Jacob played center here too. Killed in Afghanistan last fall."

Near the back sat the only Black boy on the bus with a starter's number, a tailback, small and thick through the thighs, eyes shut, earbuds in both ears. Honey put her chin on his knee. He opened his eyes, took out one earbud, looked at the dog and smiled, the first smile Ruth had seen on the bus.

"DeShawn Hollins. Lives with his grandmother past the water tower. She picks him up after every practice and sits in the car with the doors locked till he's in the seat beside her." Teddy touched the cigarette behind his ear. "A drunk on 290 killed the boy's mama. His grandmother don't trust the dark."

The kicker was a skinny Mexican boy, a sophomore, turning a cell phone over and over in his hand. Honey lay down beside him and leaned her weight against his shin. "Luis Garza. Best leg I ever saw on a sophomore. His folks don't come to the away games," Teddy said, and stopped there.

One seat from the back sat a junior with the build of a grown man and the face of a child, in expensive cleats and long sleeves under his jersey in ninety-eight degrees. When Honey came to him he put his hand on her head, and she pressed her whole side against his legs and stood there, braced.

"Brody Vossberg," Teddy said, and something old went across his face, and he looked out the window. "Linebacker. His daddy hauls cattle."

Then the dog came to a senior sprawled across a whole seat with his feet in the aisle, a big good-looking boy with a white grin. Wade Haigler had been saying something low about the kicker that made the boy across from him snicker. Honey stopped and looked him over, head to boots, the way Ruth's mother used to look over a ham at the butcher's counter. The boy stopped grinning.

Then the dog turned her back on him, walked up the aisle and lay down at Ruth's feet.

Wade Haigler laughed. "Dog don't like me."

"Dog don't like you," somebody said, and a few boys laughed, and Lyle pulled the lever, and the bus rolled out of the lot.

A few miles out of town Honey rose from Ruth's feet unbidden, walked back to the fourth seat on the left and lay down across Caleb Waidner's cleats. She stayed there all the way to Pinto Falls.

Up and down the aisle, thirty-four boys sat bent over thirty-four telephones with their faces lit blue from below, and three of them laughed at the same second at the same video. In 1933 Fritz Vellmer had run an aerial wire from the windmill to the kitchen for a battery shortwave set, and on clear winter nights the homeplace pulled in Berlin, a man's voice shouting through the static in the German of Ruth's own Sunday school. That same year Berlin put a cheap radio on sale, the Volksempfänger, the people's receiver, for seventy-six marks, with 301 stamped on the case for the thirtieth of January, the day Hitler took office, so every kitchen in Germany would hear the one voice. Ruth watched the blue faces and said nothing.

Two weeks later Coach Winslow kicked Wade Haigler off the team for taking forty dollars out of a sophomore's locker, and the sophomore was Luis Garza. Nobody on bus No. 7 laughed about the dog after that.

---

They lost the first game, at Pinto Falls, 27 to 6.

They lost it in the first quarter on two fumbles, and after halftime the boys quit hitting, the way a mule quits pulling once the plow is hung on a root. Coach Winslow never raised his voice at the boys who fumbled. He took each one by the face mask, said something meant for that boy alone, and sent him back onto the field.

On the bus home nobody talked. Caleb Waidner sat alone against the window with the letter in his hand, unread.

Past the county line Coach Winslow came up the aisle and sat down across from Ruth.

"Well, ma'am," he said. "Now you've seen one."

"Now I've seen one."

"What's your verdict?"

"They quit at halftime," Ruth said. "They expected to lose, and they didn't care to be surprised."

He stopped cracking seeds and looked at her.

"Air Force," Ruth said. "France."

"Yes ma'am. Twenty years. Six of those on exchange tours with the French, at their air base outside Colmar." He cracked a seed. "Loved it over there. Old men playing *boules* in the square under the plane trees." He looked out at the dark. "*On ne lâche rien,*" he said. "The French pilots used to say that. We let go of nothing."

Ruth opened her mouth to answer him in French.

The words sat ready on her tongue in the singing French of the Drôme that Odile Fabre had drilled into her behind shut doors in a stone barn, with lavender drying from the rafters. *Rien,* Odile would say, and rap the table with one knuckle. *Again. You speak French like a grammar book, and the Germans shoot grammar books.* Sixty-nine years had passed, and Odile's voice still answered the first time Ruth called it.

Ruth closed her mouth.

"That's a good thing to say to a team," she said instead.

---

After Pinto Falls, Lyle quit letting her walk the four blocks to the field house. On every away Friday he brought No. 7 up Elm Street empty at ten minutes to three, before the boys came out, and set the air brake at her gate with a long sigh, like a cow settling into straw.

By twenty minutes to three Honey sat at the front window with her nose on the glass and her one ear up, watching the corner of Elm and Third, and she was there before the bus ever turned onto Elm Street, every time. Nobody had taught her to read the kitchen clock. On the one open Friday in October, with no game and no thermos on the counter, she slept the whole afternoon on the linoleum and never once went to the glass.

Lyle rode with a sausage biscuit in wax paper on the dash, and the sausage was his wife's own, homemade and smoked, the best in the county. One Friday in September he climbed down to carry Ruth's pocketbook up the steps, and when he climbed back behind the wheel, the wax paper lay flat and empty on the dash. Honey sat on the first seat with her chin up, chewing slowly, and looked Lyle dead in the eye until the biscuit was gone.

Lyle looked at the dog. The dog looked at Lyle.

"Well," Lyle said.

The next Friday he brought two.

---

The second Friday they played the Sandy Creek Wildcats at Waidner Field.

The home bleachers were half full at kickoff and full by the fourth quarter, once the telephones started ringing. Ruth sat behind the bench in a folding chair Teddy carried up from the field house.

The Mustangs ran the ball straight up the middle behind Tyler Kott on the first play, and the second, and the third, and the fourth. The boys who had been driven back three yards on the goal line in August were the ones driving now. Tyler came off the ball low and moved a two-hundred-eighty-pound boy out of the hole like a man walking a refrigerator across a kitchen floor. DeShawn Hollins went through the hole behind him, again and again, four yards, five yards, six.

Sandy Creek led 13 to 7 with three minutes left when DeShawn went through the hole one more time and nobody laid a hand on him for fifty-two yards.

Thirteen to thirteen.

Luis Garza ran onto the field with his kicking tee in his hand. Before he set the tee down he looked past the light poles to the chain-link fence at the corner where Ruth had stood in August. Out in the dark beyond the lights stood two small figures, a man and a woman, their fingers hooked through the chain-link. They had come on foot and kept out of the reach of the lights, where nobody in the stands could see them and a man and wife without papers felt safe.

Luis looked at them for one second. Then he set the tee on the grass.

The kick went end over end through the middle of the uprights and kept going into the dark.

Fourteen to thirteen.

The Mustangs held Sandy Creek on four downs, Caleb Waidner took a knee twice, and when the clock ran out the whole team stood staring at the scoreboard as if a man from the school board might walk out and change the numbers. Then they started yelling. Teddy Rausch threw his cigarette high into the lights and caught it.

Coach Winslow stood on the sideline with his hands in his back pockets and his eyes shut, his lips moving.

At the corner fence the two figures were gone.

---

That night Lyle had No. 7 parked at the field house with the gear aboard, and the boys came up its steps yelling for their bags and went off again into the arms of their mothers.

Caleb Waidner came up the steps last, without his helmet. He stood in the aisle by Ruth's seat, his face still streaked with eye black.

"She lay on my feet," he said. "Last week. The whole way to Pinto Falls."

"I saw her."

"Teddy says she grades people." He shifted his weight. "My grandma had a dog like that, when I was little. Wouldn't go near my dad."

He stood there a moment longer, a boy with a sentence stuck in his chest. Then he fetched his helmet and the folded letter from his seat, and on his way down the aisle he bent and laid his hand on the dog's head without stopping, the way a boy touches a doorframe for luck.

In the mirror Lyle watched him go down the steps. The little brass cross on its bead chain swung once and hung still.

"Miss Ruth," Lyle said, "I believe that's the first one."

"The first win?"

"The first anything," Lyle said, and pulled the lever, and shut the door.
