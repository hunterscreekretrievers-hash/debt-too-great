# 20. The Back Row

*St. Mary's University, San Antonio, Texas, Sunday, May 26, 1968*

On a Tuesday in June of 1964, three months after Grandma Hattie was buried, the envelope came in the noon mail.

Heavy cream paper. A bank's name engraved in the corner, the trust department of a downtown San Antonio bank, the kind of building a colored boy from the East Side walked past with his eyes front. Mama brought it to the kitchen table holding it by the edges like a hot pie plate and set it down in front of her son without a word, because the envelope had his name on it, typed, *Mr. Isaiah Mayfield*, and nobody had ever called him mister in his life.

Inside was one page. A private scholarship, the letter said, established by a donor who wished to remain anonymous, for a graduate of Phillis Wheatley High School of good character who intended to complete a four-year college degree. Tuition, books and fees, paid directly to the institution of his choice, for four years, renewable each fall on presentation of his grades. The trust officer's signature took up half the bottom of the page in blue ink.

Daddy read the letter three times on his feet. Then he sat down and read it again.

"Who'd do a thing like that?" Mama said.

"Somebody with money to spare," Daddy said, and folded the letter along its creases and handed it back across the table. "Don't you go wasting it."

Eli Mayfield worked thirty-one years on the flight line at Kelly Field, putting engines back into airplanes that other men flew, and he had never once been inside a college building except to deliver ice. He said that sentence to his son one more time in the fall of 1964, standing in the parking lot at St. Mary's University with a suitcase in each hand, and one more time at Christmas, and one last time the next fall, and never needed to say it a fifth time. **[VERIFY: St. Mary's University, San Antonio, admitting Black undergraduates by 1964]**

---

Marcus looked up from the phone.

"Wait. Somebody just paid for all of it? You never learned who?"

"I never asked the bank. A boy doesn't go poking at a gift horse's teeth, not when the horse is carrying him."

"But you learned eventually."

"Nearly fifty years later, in a lawyer's office on Main Street in Martinsburg."

Marcus started to say a name and stopped. He looked at the footlocker. He looked at the quilt with everything from the footlocker spread across it, the cap, the letters, the photograph of the six Vellmer children. Then he looked back at the phone and didn't say anything at all, and that was the right decision, because the story wasn't there yet.

---

The twenty-sixth of May, 1968, was a Sunday, and it was ninety-one degrees in San Antonio by ten in the morning.

The commencement was held outdoors on the lawn in front of the main building, with folding chairs set in rows and a wooden platform at the front under a canvas awning that did nobody any good. The graduates sat in the front rows in black gowns and black caps, sweating like field hands under all that cloth. The families sat behind them, the men in suits with their jackets folded over their knees, the ladies in hats and gloves fanning themselves with the programs, a thousand paper fans going at once like grasshoppers in dry grass.

That commencement fell seven weeks after Memphis. Folks forget that. Dr. King had been shot on the balcony of the Lorraine Motel on the fourth of April, and more than a hundred American cities had burned that week, and on the East Side of San Antonio grown men had sat on their front steps in the evenings with the radio on and wept and not cared who saw them do it. A colored boy walking across a stage to shake a white priest's hand that spring was walking with the whole country's eyes on the back of his neck, and every Black family on that lawn knew it. Every boy in a black gown knew one thing more. His student deferment ended the minute the dean read his name, and the Selective Service board on the East Side was reading the same list.

Daddy and Mama sat in the fourth row of families, right on the aisle so Mama could see the platform. Mama had on a new hat, lavender, with a little net, and she had cried twice before the procession even started. Daddy wore his only suit, the brown one, and he held his hat on his knee with both hands like a man gathering the reins of an unbroke horse.

A diploma has a sound. Nobody tells you so, but every graduate hears it. A dean reads a name off a card into a microphone that squeals, and the name goes out across a lawn full of folding chairs and comes back off the brick of the building, and a boy who has been sitting on that name for twenty-two years stands up in a black gown and finds out his legs will still carry him.

"Isaiah Joseph Mayfield."

The walk across that platform took eleven steps. I can still count every one of them. The priest's hand was cool and dry and smelled of talcum powder. The diploma came rolled tight and tied with a ribbon. On the way back down the steps, one look out across the lawn for the lavender hat, and there it was, and Mama was on her feet with both hands over her mouth, and Daddy was sitting dead still with his hat crushed flat in his fist.

Then, past them, at the very back of the crowd, in the last row of folding chairs set out on the grass where the shade of the live oaks began, sat a navy hat.

---

"The lady from the funeral," Lily said.

"The very same lady."

"How did you know her?"

"Because she sat the exact same way. Back straight as a plumb line. Gloves in her lap. Hymnal open in both hands at Grandma's funeral, and a program open in both hands that day, and not fanning herself with it. A thousand people on that lawn fanning themselves, and the one lady in the back row sitting perfectly still in that heat like she'd been in hotter places and didn't think much of this one."

Lily thought about that. "Had she been in hotter places?"

The fire popped. Two fingers went to the top button of the grey cardigan.

"Oh, baby," the old man said. "You have no idea."

---

The rest of the ceremony ran together, names and heat, names and heat. When the last name was read and the priest gave the benediction and the graduates threw their caps, which the dean had specifically told them not to do, every family on that lawn surged forward at once, and the folding chairs went over like dominoes, and there was Mama with her arms around her son's neck and her lavender hat knocked sideways, crying into a black gown, and Daddy behind her with his hand out to shake like this was a business arrangement, which it never was, and then his other hand on the back of his son's head, pulling it down to his shoulder, and holding it there.

"Your Uncle Sam," Daddy said into the side of his son's head, and didn't finish the sentence, and didn't need to finish it.

Over Daddy's shoulder, in the back row, the lady in the navy hat was standing now, walking away across the grass toward the street, not fast, in that navy dress and that navy hat, a tall woman by herself going against the crowd the way a fish goes up a creek.

"Back in a minute, Mama."

Running in a graduation gown is like running in a wet bedsheet. Halfway across the lawn the cap came off and stayed in the grass. The lady in navy was almost to the curb when she heard running feet behind her and turned to face the boy.

Up close she looked older than the woman at the funeral in 1964. Forty-four years old, with grey in the hair under the hat and lines at the corners of her eyes from twenty-one years of squinting down caliche roads into the sun, and those eyes were grey too, grey as river rock, and they looked at a boy of twenty-two out of breath in a black gown the way a rancher looks at rain after a dry summer.

"Miss Ruth," the boy said. "Ma'am. Thank you for coming."

She didn't ask how he knew her name. She looked at the diploma in his hand with the ribbon still on it.

"May I see it?"

He gave it to her. She untied the ribbon and unrolled the paper with those long fingers, held it up in the sun, and read the whole thing, every word, the Latin and all, the way a buyer reads a deed. Then she rolled it up again, exactly as it was, and tied the ribbon in the same bow, and handed it back to him.

"Your uncle would have been proud of you, Isaiah," she said.

Nine words, the whole sentence.

"Yes, ma'am. Daddy says so too." A boy of twenty-two has no more sense than that. "Won't you come back and have some punch? Mama would want to meet you proper. There's a reception in the hall."

"No," she said. "Thank you. I have a long drive." She looked past him, across the lawn, at the crowd around the lavender hat. Something went across her face then and was gone. "Tell your daddy I said he did well. Tell him Hattie would have been proud too."

Then she put out her gloved hand, and he shook it, and she turned and walked to a green Ford pickup with Pedernales County caliche on the fenders, parked under a live oak at the curb, and got in, and drove away down the street without looking back, and that was all.

The boy stood there on the curb in his gown with the diploma in his hand until Mama came across the lawn with his lost cap and slapped him on the arm with it.

"Who was that?"

"Miss Ruth. From the funeral."

Mama looked down the street where the truck had gone. Then she turned to her husband coming up behind her, and something passed between those two that was not meant for a son's eyes.

"Well," Mama said. "She came."

"She came," Daddy said.

Nobody in the Mayfield family said one more word about it, that day or for years.

---

"That's it?" Marcus said. "She drove all the way down there for nine words?"

"She drove eighty miles each way in a pickup with no air conditioning in ninety-one degrees, sat in the back row for two hours in a navy dress, and drove home. For nine words." The fire had settled down to coals. "Then she went back to her cattle, and at six-thirty Monday morning she was casing mail at the Martinsburg post office like any other Monday."

"Why'd she say your uncle? Why not your dad?"

Marcus is a sharp boy. He gets that from his mother, who got it from her daddy's side, and all of them got it from Hattie.

"That," the old man said, "is the question."

"So what's the answer?"

"The answer is that a boy of twenty-two stood on a curb in San Antonio and heard that sentence and thought it was a kind thing from an old friend of the family. A white lady from the country who'd known Uncle Sam when they were children, being polite at a graduation, a nice thing. That was all the meaning he heard in it. For forty-six years, that was the whole of it."

"Then what?"

"Then the sentence meant something else."

Marcus waited. Lily waited. Even Ben, who was sliding down Lily's shoulder by inches toward sleep, opened one eye and waited.

The old man gave them nothing more, not yet.

"Grandpa," Lily said finally, very quietly. "You're mean."

"Your grandmother told me so about once a week, God rest her." The poker went into the coals and stirred them up until a little flame came back to life. "There's an order to a story, baby. Miss Ruth taught me that. You don't open the last box first."

Out in the yard the moon had come around to the south side of the cabin, and the light came in the little window over the kitchen sink now and lay across the floor in a long white bar, as if somebody had left a door open in the sky.

The diploma has hung in a frame in the front hall on Hackberry Street since Gloria and I bought the house in 1972. Gloria dusted it every Saturday of our married life, and the ribbon still holds the bow Miss Ruth tied, because nobody in that house would ever untie it again.

"What came next?" Lily asked. "After college?"

"Selective Service. The induction notice came in June, three weeks after that lawn. Fort Sam Houston taught a college boy to be a combat medic, across town from his mama's kitchen, and the 25th Infantry Division took him to Cu Chi for 1969." The fire ticked. "I came home in 1970 and married your grandmother. The Army kept me twenty years after that, in work I'll tell you about before morning. Then sixteen years on the city ambulance, and that ambulance saw every kind of hurt a city can hand a man. I paid taxes, went to church, raised your mama." The old man shrugged. "I lived a life. Turns out that was her whole wish. The money had one purpose, and that was it."

"The scholarship money."

"All of it," the old man said, and left that alone.

He picked up the diary off the quilt and opened it on his knee to the place where the ribbon marked it, though the entries had stopped in 1946 and the rest of the pages were empty, and he looked at the empty pages a while as if he could still read them.

"She went home that afternoon," he said, "to the Pedernales. She went on living out there by herself for forty-one more years with a stack of pill bottles growing on the kitchen counter, and a man in a Cadillac coming up her drive."
