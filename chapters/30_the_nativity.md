# 30. The Nativity

*Late November to mid-December 2013. Elm Street and the Exxon on U.S. 290.*

Fritz Vellmer carved the figures in the winter of 1919, the winter after four men from Austin stood him on his own porch and made him kiss the flag, and he carved them by lamplight with a pocketknife and two gouges that had crossed the Atlantic in his grandfather's tool roll, out of a dead limb off the big pecan in the river bottom.

Mary knelt with her hands folded, her face a little long, because Fritz had modeled it on his mother. Joseph held a staff and wore a carpenter's square in his belt, which no Gospel mentions and Fritz put there anyway. An ox lay beside a donkey with one ear cocked forward. One shepherd was old and bent; the other was a boy with a lamb across his shoulders. Three kings wore robes painted with the last of the red and blue barn paint. The Child lay in a manger no longer than Ruth's hand, the straw cut into the wood in tiny strokes, the baby's two fists raised beside His face the way a newborn holds them.

---

On Thursday, the twenty-first of November, a week before Thanksgiving, the kitchen telephone rang at a quarter past four in the afternoon while Ruth stood at the sink peeling potatoes.

"Miss Vellmer?" The voice belonged to an old man, careful, with Virginia under it. "My name is Thomas Leland. My father was Arthur Leland. In August of 1943 he came to see you at the Naval Hospital at Pearl Harbor."

Ruth laid the paring knife on the drainboard.

"He spoke of you twice in my hearing," the man said. "Both times at Christmas, both times after the second glass. I spent thirty-four years at Langley, Miss Vellmer, and I'm retired now. Old friends still telephone me when something crosses a desk with my father's people on it."

"Tell me the rest."

"In August of 2008 the National Archives at College Park opened the OSS personnel files to the public, thirty-five thousand of them. This September somebody came into the reading room and pulled one. *Vellmer, Ruth M., Texas.*" He let the name sit on the line. "This October somebody wrote to the National Archives at Kew, in London, and asked for the file on the courier of the STONECUTTER circuit. Not by your name. By Lucie. The letter gave a return address in Cairo."

Outside the window over the sink a grackle walked the clothesline with its tail cocked. Honey lifted her head off the linoleum and watched Ruth's back.

"Who?" Ruth said.

"I don't know yet. I've handed all of it to the Bureau, the College Park slip and a copy of the Kew letter. They'll open a file." He paused. "Files move slowly."

"I carried the mail thirty-nine years, Mr. Leland. I know the pace of a file."

"Your OSS file carries the address you gave my father in 1943. A rural route below Martinsburg."

"I haven't slept out there since 2009."

"That's something."

"It's something," Ruth said.

"Is there anyone who can come and stay with you?"

"I have a dog."

He laughed once, short and surprised, and for a second the line held a small balding man in a tan suit in a hospital commander's office, laughing the same way. "My father would have liked that answer."

"Your father was a careful man," Ruth said. "I'm glad he raised one. Thank you for calling, Mr. Leland."

She hung the receiver on its hook and kept her hand on it until the kitchen clock had ticked a full minute.

Then she went to work.

From the cedar chest in the back bedroom she took the oldest of her grey wool coats, the one she had worn on the mail route the last ten winters she drove it, and from the back of her jewelry box a roll of ten twenty-dollar bills and a grey card in waxed paper gone the color of tobacco. She opened six stitches in the coat lining with her seam ripper, laid the bills flat against the wool with the card behind them, and closed the lining again with stitches no bigger than grains of rice. She folded the coat square into a canvas bag with a change of clothes and the spare flashlight and set the bag on the closet floor beside the door, where her hand would find it in the dark.

From the top shelf of the same closet she took down a cigar box. Inside, wrapped in an oiled rag, lay a Colt .32 pocket automatic, the same model she had fired beside Violette Szabo in a basement range in the West End in the spring of 1944. She had bought its twin in a San Antonio pawnshop in 1946 and kept it clean ever since, the way some women keep their mother's silver. She broke it down on a spread newspaper on the kitchen table, oiled every piece, put it back together, loaded the magazine and laid the pistol in the drawer of her nightstand, under her reading glasses.

From the drawer under the telephone she took a new spiral notebook. On the first page she wrote the date, and under it the first license plate that came down Elm Street after supper: the propane man's truck, which she had known for eleven years. She wrote it down anyway.

Last, one at a time, she turned the four kitchen chairs around to stand with their backs to the far wall, every one facing the door. The legs scraped on the linoleum. Honey watched from under the table with her chin on her paws.

Ruth ate her supper standing at the counter, where she could see the street.

She told nobody, not Kate and not the dog man in Houston. She did not put off Christmas either. Fritz Vellmer's daughter did not keep a Christ Child in a box on account of a letter from Cairo.

---

The Friday after Thanksgiving Ruth set the figures out on the Elm Street porch. She had set them out every year since 1950, the first Christmas she had Kate, on the homeplace porch until 2009 and on this one since the move. Fritz had built the stable too, of cedar pickets and rusted tin, and Ruth strung it with the big old-fashioned bulbs, red, green, blue and orange, the kind hot enough to raise a blister. She laid hay from the homeplace barn and set each figure where her mother had set it. Honey sniffed every one, the ox twice, then lay across the top step guarding the shepherds.

At dusk two little girls going by with their mother stopped at the gate.

"Is that baby Jesus?" the smaller one asked.

"That's Him," Ruth said.

"Why's He so little?"

"Because He was that little," Ruth said. The mother smiled and walked the girls on toward the square.

---

In the small hours of Tuesday, December third, Ruth woke at ten minutes to two.

Honey woke first. Her nails scrabbled on the bedroom floor, and then came a bark Ruth had never heard from her, high and hard as a slap. Glass broke on the porch. A young man laughed, loud and drunk. A truck door slammed.

Ruth took the Colt out of the nightstand drawer and the steel flashlight from the closet shelf. She did not hurry. People who hurry toward a noise in the dark run straight into whatever made it.

By the time she got the porch light on, the truck was rolling. She saw it for three seconds under the streetlight at Elm and Third: a white Dodge pickup with a cracked tailgate and the left taillight dark, three heads in the cab. As the truck swung around the corner, an arm flung something out the passenger window, and Ruth heard the small hollow knock of wood skipping on asphalt.

Honey stood on the top step with her hackles raised, growling at the empty corner.

Ruth turned the flashlight on her porch. The bulbs lay smashed. The stable lay on its side with the tin roof folded like a bent hat brim. Joseph lay in the flower bed with his head snapped off at the neck. The old shepherd had been stomped into the boards, his staff broken in three pieces. Two kings had been thrown against the clapboards, and Fritz's red and blue paint lay in flakes on the floor. Somebody had urinated on the ox, and the smell came up sharp and sour off the hay. Across the porch boards, in black spray paint, crooked capitals two feet tall spelled out three words. One of them was a word men use for women, and its second letter was backward.

Mary still knelt, untouched, at the back of the wreck. The manger in front of her was empty.

Ruth walked to the corner of Elm and Third in her housecoat and slippers, the pistol a cold weight in one pocket and Honey pressed against her left knee. The flashlight found the Child in the middle of the street. A tire had rolled over Him. The manger was split in two, and the little body her father had cut in 1919 was crushed flat along the grain, with one carved fist still whole. Against the curb, in a skim of dead pecan leaves, lay the lamb, snapped off the boy shepherd's shoulders.

She stood under the streetlight with the pieces of the Child in one palm and a wooden lamb no bigger than a walnut in the other, and the stars over the water tower burned hard and many, the way they burn over a pasture in a freeze.

"Well," she said aloud. "Where were You?"

Nothing answered her. Honey leaned her whole weight against Ruth's shins, warm through the housecoat.

She went home and called the sheriff's office.

---

The deputy came at a quarter to nine that morning.

Deputy Kyle Brandt was twenty-six, with a fresh haircut, a creaking gun belt and a round tin of snuff worn into his back pocket, and he stood on her walk with his hat in his hand, looking at the wreck on her porch.

"Kids, probably," Deputy Brandt said. "We get a lot of this around the holidays."

"Those weren't kids," Ruth said. "Three grown men in a white Dodge pickup. Cracked tailgate. Left taillight dark. They turned north on Third."

He wrote that on his pad, took four photographs with a little silver camera, looked at the words on the boards and did not read them aloud.

Ruth held out her palm with the broken Child on it. "Whoever did this wanted to break something dear to somebody."

Deputy Brandt looked down at the pieces in her palm. "Ma'am, I'm sorry," he said, and Ruth could see that he meant it, and that Texas had handed him a smaller tool than the job.

"The law calls this criminal mischief. Under fifty dollars, it's a Class C, same as a speeding ticket."

"My father carved those figures in 1919."

"Yes, ma'am. But for the report I've got to put down a replacement cost."

"Nobody can replace them."

"I'm going to put down under fifty," he said, gently, and he did, and he tore off the yellow copy and handed it to her with a case number written across the top in blue ink. "If you see that truck again, you call us."

He sat in the cruiser a minute, typing the white Dodge into his report.

---

Her people came to rebuild the stable without being asked.

Lyle came first, that afternoon, with a red toolbox, a bottle of Titebond and a box of new bulbs from the hardware store on the square. He said nothing about the words on the boards. He painted them out in three coats of grey porch enamel while Ruth swept glass.

Tyler Kott came at four, straight from school, with DeShawn, Brody and Kurt Hoerster, the sophomore safety. They stood the stable upright and hammered the tin flat over a sawhorse. DeShawn crawled through the flower bed until he found Joseph's head under the lantana and held it high like a man who has found a ring in the grass. Dixie Beck came at five with a pan of King Ranch casserole under foil, looked once at the porch, said, "Baby, I am going to find out who did this," and went into the kitchen to heat the oven.

Fourteen hours before, under a streetlight, Ruth had asked the Lord where He had been that night. Now the boys bent over her father's tin at the sawhorse. She went to the kitchen to find the glue.

Ruth herself glued Joseph's head back in place and held the joint with her long fingers for ten minutes by the kitchen clock, the way her father had taught her to hold a stone until the mortar set. The seam showed. She did not try to hide it.

Before daylight on Wednesday, the fourth, Ruth set the grey Royal on the kitchen table, rolled in a white sheet, a carbon and an onionskin, and typed with two fingers while her coffee went cold at her elbow. This time not one try went into the stove.

That afternoon Ruth drove out to the homeplace and sawed a dead pecan branch the thickness of her wrist. She carved for six evenings at the kitchen table with her father's pocketknife, the blade honed so many times over ninety years that it had worn to a narrow crescent of steel. By the second hour each night the crooked knuckles of her right hand ached, the two bones Margaret had broken in a Honolulu blackout in 1942, and she soaked them in warm water and went back to work. On Thursday, in the daylight between two of those evenings, she drove the forty-odd miles to Kerrville with Honey on the seat beside her and dropped a plain envelope through the brass slot at the post office, the way she had mailed every Lucie letter for thirty-four years. On the sixth night she cut the baby's two fists raised beside His face.

She kept the old Child's whole fist in her apron pocket while she worked. The new Child came out pale as bone. She set it in the manger on Monday, December ninth, and sat on the porch swing in her coat with the new bulbs burning and a half moon standing high and white over Emil Klier's roof.

---

She saw the truck again on Tuesday, the tenth, at the Exxon on U.S. 290.

She had stopped on her way home from Kenji's place outside Comfort, and the white Dodge sat at the next pump, the cracked tailgate facing her, three men around it.

The one pumping gas was short and thick through the chest, with no neck to speak of, black hair spiked with gel, a gold chain outside his shirt and a chipped front tooth that showed every time he laughed. The knuckles of his right hand were knotted with old scar. His name was Karim Nasser, and he was twenty-six.

The big man leaning on the bed rail was Samir Darwish, thirty-one, the oldest of the three. He stood six foot two with a soft belly over his belt, a beard trimmed close to the jaw and heavy black-framed glasses that he pushed up his nose with one forefinger, slowly, the way some men stroke a mustache.

The third man sat sideways on the open passenger door with one boot on the running board, smoking. He was twenty-eight, slim and very neat, in a pressed black jacket, with a narrow handsome face, a thin white scar through his left eyebrow and a heavy gold-colored watch on his wrist. His name was Tarek Haddad. Ruth did not yet know his name.

Ruth hung up the nozzle and walked to the tailgate of the white Dodge with Honey at her left knee.

"You took the baby," she said to the three of them.

Karim Nasser laughed, and the chipped tooth showed. "What baby, lady?"

"The one you threw in the street on Elm."

Samir Darwish pushed his glasses up his nose and looked at the gas pump.

Tarek Haddad took his time. He looked at her coat, her shoes, her grey hair and the yellow dog, and blew smoke out the side of his mouth.

"Old woman," he said. His English was good, quick and clean. "Go home. Play with your dolls."

"I know who you are now," Ruth said to him.

"You know nothing about me."

"I know your face," Ruth said. "I've seen it on another man."

He smiled at that. He had very good teeth. "Where, grandmother? On television?"

Ruth did not answer him. She took a pen from her coat pocket and wrote the license plate on the back of her left hand in front of all three, letter by letter, the ink catching in the creases of her skin. Honey pressed against her leg with her head low and her lip lifted off her teeth on one side, silent.

Tarek watched her write the last letter. He was no longer smiling.

---

At home Ruth copied the plate into the spiral notebook and onto the back of the yellow report and called Dixie Beck, who called back in two hours. "Baby, Roy at the feed store used to buy his tires from the pretty one with the watch. Tarek Haddad." She spelled it. "The short loud one is Karim, and the big one with the glasses is Samir. Roy says not one of those three is supposed to be in this country at all."

On Deputy Brandt's voicemail Ruth left the plate, the gas station, Tarek Haddad's name spelled twice, and the first names Karim and Samir, and she offered to come in and sign a statement. She called the same facts in to the sheriff's dispatcher, and the log took down every word: a smashed manger, a threat at a gas pump, a license plate. The log held nothing else, because she had said nothing else. She had learned in 1933, with Berlin on her father's shortwave, that whoever keeps the record writes the story afterward, and she gave the record nothing but facts.

Deputy Brandt called her back on Thursday.

"Ma'am, I ran the plate. Truck's registered to the man who owns the tire shop. He says that truck sat locked in his yard all night on the third, keys on a nail in his office."

"He's lying."

"Yes, ma'am, that's possible. But I can't arrest a man for buying gas." Ruth could hear him working the snuff in his lip. "A Class C on a truck the owner swears was locked up, the county attorney can't take that to a jury. I asked him."

"Roy at the feed store says none of those three men is here legally."

"Ma'am, we don't do immigration. That's federal."

"Nobody asked you to do immigration, Deputy. Type his name into the computer in your car."

"I'll look into it, ma'am."

He typed the name into a supplement to the report and ran it for Texas warrants, and it came back clean.

Ruth had known none of the three men's histories at the pump, and the federal drawers held every one of them.

Karim Nasser was the fourth son of a cell-phone seller in the old market of Tripoli, Lebanon. He had paid a smuggler in Monterrey in March of 2008 and crossed the Rio Grande below Laredo at night on an inflated truck tube, and no Border Patrol agent ever laid eyes on him. In October of 2010, outside a bar on Sixth Street in Austin, he broke a bouncer's jaw in two places with a ringed fist, and the police took him to the Travis County jail in handcuffs. Immigration faxed the jail a detainer asking Travis County to hold him forty-eight hours, not counting the weekend. On a Sunday morning a jail clerk processed his bond, and Karim Nasser walked out into the sunshine with the detainer still lying in his file. He never went back to court.

Samir Darwish had come from Alexandria, Egypt, in 2004 on a student visa to study petroleum engineering in Houston. He failed thermodynamics twice. The college dropped him in 2007, the visa died with the enrollment, and Samir Darwish stayed and drove a wrecker, first in Houston and now for the tire shop. In June of 2013 a state trooper stopped him at two in the morning on U.S. 290 east of Johnson City, weaving across the center stripe, and he blew a .14 on the roadside. He made bond out of the Blanco County jail at six the next morning, before anybody there looked at his status, and drove home in the same white Dodge.

On Elm Street, Honey slept under the kitchen table with her nose toward the door.

Tarek Haddad had come from Zarqa, Jordan, in 2005 on a student visa to study engineering in San Antonio. Tarek quit going to classes inside a year and stayed when the visa lapsed. In 2009 an immigration judge in San Antonio ordered him removed from the United States. For four years he had mounted tires for cash and slept in a rented trailer behind a tire shop on the Johnson City highway, and no officer of the United States had ever knocked on that trailer door. The final order of removal sat in a federal file with his name, his photograph and his date of birth, and nobody had taken it out of the drawer. In 2011 the government counted close to half a million orders like his, lying in the same drawers.

In 2009 the word handed down to county deputies all over Texas ran the same every time: immigration belongs to Washington. One call from a federal desk in San Antonio might have put Tarek Haddad on a bus to the detention center at Pearsall before Christmas. Nobody at that desk made the call. Three men stood on the record in three places, and in every one of those places, far from Elm Street, somebody had already signed a paper and gone home.

That same Thursday, the twelfth, the Martinsburg paper came out, and Ruth read it at the kitchen table with her reading glasses on and Honey's chin on her slipper. On page four, under LETTERS, every word she had typed stood over the name Lucie.

She read it twice. The second time she saw grief's mistake.

For thirty-four years Lucie had written about Berlin and Tehran and a courtroom in Dallas, and never once about a porch. Now the paper had printed a father, a pecan Christ Child carved in the winter of 1919, and a street. One porch in all of Pedernales County held that manger, and half the county had driven past it since Thanksgiving.

She cut the letter out along the column rule, and on Saturday she drove it out to the stone house and laid it with its onionskin carbon in the folder at the bottom of the footlocker, under the marriage paper. That night she sat up in the dark front room until two, with the Colt in her lap and the dog across her feet, and nothing came down Elm Street but the wind.

---

At two in the morning on Tuesday, December seventeenth, under a full moon bright enough to throw shadows on the street, Emil Klier, the retired postman across Elm Street, got up to quiet his barking dog and saw a white pickup idling at Ruth's curb with its headlights off and one taillight dark. Ruth had seen it too, from behind her bedroom curtain, and she called it in, and the deputy who came down Elm Street twenty minutes later found the curb empty and gave her a second case number, for a suspicious vehicle.

After the deputy's taillights turned onto Third, Ruth did not go back to bed. She sat in a kitchen chair facing the door, with the Colt in her lap under a dish towel and Honey across her feet, and she watched the door the way she had once watched a road in the Drôme. Toward four o'clock Ruth spoke to the dark kitchen. "*Awake, why sleepest thou, O Lord?*" The kitchen clock ticked. Elijah's line came up behind the psalm, the line he threw at the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel, *either he is talking, or he is pursuing, or he is in a journey*, and Ruth stopped it in her mouth. "No," she said. "Elijah said that of Baal, and Baal was not there to hear it. You are here." The other psalm came next, the one her father said over the stock in a blizzard, and she let that one run to its end. *Behold, he that keepeth Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep.* "Then the two of us will sit up together," Ruth said, and kept her eyes on the door until the window went grey.

At first light Ruth let Honey out the front door. The dog went down the brick path, stopped by the lantana at the picket fence and stood over something in the frosted grass with her nose an inch off it and her tail held low and still.

A lump of raw hamburger the size of a man's fist lay against the pickets, where an arm had tossed it over in the dark. Honey looked at the meat. Then she looked back up the path at Ruth, and she did not touch it.

The dog man had trained that refusal into her when she was a pup: a week of meat dropped on the ground in front of her and one low word, *leave it*, until a yellow puppy learned that food on the ground belongs to nobody. Honey would rob a kitchen counter blind. She would not take one crumb off the ground.

Ruth knelt in her housecoat and smelled the meat. Under the raw beef rose a sweetness that had no business in hamburger, thick and sugary, the smell of the green puddle under a mail Jeep with a split radiator hose on a July afternoon. Thirty-nine years on a rural route teach a carrier that smell, and the dead barn cats lying beside the puddles. Antifreeze.

She fetched a bread sack from the kitchen, turned it inside out over her hand, picked the meat up through the plastic and tied the sack in a knot.

Deputy Brandt came at ten. He held the bread sack up to the light, wrote *possible poison, animal* on his pad under the second case number, and laid the sack in the trunk of his cruiser. Nobody ever sent it to a laboratory. The sack rode in that trunk until the last week of January, and then somebody threw it in a dumpster behind the courthouse.

Ruth stood at the gate a long time with her hand on the white blaze. All night she had sat in a kitchen chair telling the Lord He was asleep, and in the morning the poison lay untouched in the frost. In Barracks 28 Betsie ten Boom had thanked God for the fleas that kept the guards out, and Ruth had thought her a fool. "Good girl," Ruth said to the dog. Then she said the rest to the Lord, sixty-nine years late, because Betsie was owed it. "Thank You for the fleas."

Her Christmas card to Houston told the dog man about the manger. The card said nothing about the meat.

On the Sunday before Christmas, Pastor Mark Albrecht stood on her porch and looked at Joseph with the seam in his neck and the pale new Child in the manger.

"Go down to Kate's in San Antonio through Christmas," he said. "Only until this passes."

"Kate's driving up Christmas morning. She'll want her turkey here." Ruth looked at the manger. "I've run from men in my day, Mark. I'm eighty-nine. I've got about one more run left in me, and I'm not spending it on three boys from a tire shop."

"Dixie told me about the one with the watch," the pastor said. "He's the one that worries me."

"He worries me too," Ruth said. "He's the only one of the three with a plan in his head."

She plugged in the lights. The bulbs threw red and green and blue and orange across the porch boards, across the old wooden faces of the shepherds and the kings, and over the yellow dog on the top step with her one ear standing straight, watching the street.
