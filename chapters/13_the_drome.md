# 13. The Drôme

*The hills of the Drôme, southern France, July 1944*

The Halifax lifted out of Blida, the airfield in the orange groves below Algiers, on the night of Saturday, the eighth of July, and for four hours Ruth sat on the ribbed floor of the fuselage with a parachute pack against her spine in the red light. **[VERIFY: 624 Squadron RAF Halifaxes from Blida dropping SOE agents into southern France, July 1944]**

The hatch in the floor swung open, and the wind came up through the hole like a fist through a screen door. Far below, under a moon two days past full, the Rhône lay across the dark country like a dropped silver chain, and east of the river the hills of the Drôme rose ridge behind ridge, black with oak and pine, folded one against the next the way Katharina Vellmer folded sheets.

The light turned green, the dispatcher's hand came down on her shoulder, and she went.

The canopy cracked open, and the bomber drained away east and left her in a silence so big she could hear the harness creak. She hit and rolled through something that scratched her shins and smelled so sweet that for one confused second she stood in front of Mama's linen drawer.

The smell was lavender. She had come down in a field of it.

A big man in a forester's corduroy jacket knelt on the silk. He smelled of tobacco and goat.

"Debout, petite."

Ruth answered in her schoolroom French, every verb in its place, and the forester winced as though she had stepped on his foot.

"Mon Dieu," he said, and gathered the parachute in his arms like a load of wash. "Odile is going to have work."

His name was Gaston Peyrol, and his wife was Marthe. By sunrise Ruth was asleep in their loft in Valfrède, high above the Jabron valley a few miles from Dieulefit, with a rooster going under the window like a cracked bugle.

---

Valfrède was forty stone houses stacked on a hillside like loaves on a baker's shelf, the color of bread crust, roofed in curved clay tiles gone orange with lichen. Below them the lavender ran down the slope in purple rows, so even that from the road the hill looked like a bolt of cloth laid out to dry. At the top stood the temple, as the French Protestants called their churches, a square stone barn of a building with plain windows, no steeple, pine benches, a pulpit with a Bible open on it, and above the pulpit, in black letters a hand tall, *DIEU EST AMOUR*.

The Protestants of these hills had been hunted by their own kings for a hundred years, and in the fifth summer of the war they hid the hunted. Gaston told Ruth the rest of it once, in her first week, over Marthe's soup. "Twelve hundred, fifteen hundred, nobody keeps a list. Jews, most of them, under names their mothers never gave them, in Dieulefit and every village around it, and not one sold to the Germans." Gaston wiped his bowl with a heel of bread. "The girl at the town hall, Jeanne Barnier, writes them born in towns whose records burned. Let the Boche write to a heap of ashes for a birth certificate." Nobody in Valfrède said one word of it twice.

---

Odile Fabre taught her French in the stone barn above the village.

The barn sat alone at the head of a goat path, its back wall the mountain itself, and from its rafters hung hundreds of bundles of lavender drying head-down, so the light through the roof tiles came in purple. In the next stall a brown nanny goat named Jézabel chewed whatever she could reach through the boards, Ruth's sleeve and Ruth's braid included.

Odile was thirty, the village schoolmistress, small and quick, with ink on her middle finger. Her husband, Henri, had been a prisoner of war in Germany since 1940, and no card had come from him since Easter. She wore his watch on her own wrist, buckled on the last hole, and never said his name.

"Again," Odile said. "*Je m'appelle Lucie Brenner. Je suis de Mulhouse.*"

Ruth said it again.

Odile shut her eyes like a woman who has just watched a cow step on a hen.

"You speak French like a grammar book," she said, "and the Germans shoot grammar books."

Her papers forgave the German under her French. **[VERIFY: Alsatians evacuated or expelled to southern France, 1939–1940]** Nothing forgave the grammar book, so Odile sanded the textbook off her. She made Ruth say *pain* until it rang out like a pan dropped on stone, *peng*, the way the women in the lavender said it.

"Less," Odile said. "Always less. A girl with nothing to hide says almost nothing."

By the second week Ruth rode a black bicycle to Dieulefit and Crest with messages rolled tight as cigarettes in the hollow handlebar and eggs in the basket, because a girl with eggs had a reason to be on the road.

---

On Sunday, the sixteenth of July, she rode down to a market town on the river with eggs for the curé.

The town's letter box was the curé's housekeeper, Madame Arnaud, a Catholic widow of sixty with more nerve than the bishop. She walked Ruth into the sacristy through the back door.

"They have taken the church for their soldiers," she whispered. "They put Monsieur le Curé in the street like a dog."

Through the crack of the sacristy door Ruth saw two hundred German soldiers in field grey with their helmets on their knees and their rifles stacked by the holy-water font. On the wall behind the altar hung the Reich war flag, as tall as a barn door, blood red, with a black hooked cross in a white circle and an iron cross in the corner, so that the wooden Christ hung in front of the red cloth like a man put against a wall to be shot.

The chaplain wore field grey with a silver cross on a chain at his chest, a man of forty with a long gentle face, and he read in Luther's German, the German of St. Paul on the Pedernales.

*Jedermann sei untertan der Obrigkeit.* Let every soul be subject unto the higher powers.

Then the men sang, two hundred voices deep and true under the stone vault.

*Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott.*

Katharina Vellmer had sung that hymn over the washtub. Ruth's lips moved with the men's. She heard her own voice in the sacristy, every word right, rising toward the red flag with the men who would go up into the hills that week.

She shut her mouth so hard her teeth clicked.

The chaplain raised his hand over the bowed heads and gave them the blessing the pastor at St. Paul had given the Vellmer pew every Sunday of her childhood. *Der Herr segne dich und behüte dich.* The Lord bless thee, and keep thee.

The soldiers filed out into the sun and climbed into trucks bound north for the Vercors. Madame Arnaud crossed herself.

"Then they're Yours," Ruth said in English, very low, to the empty altar. "Keep them."

She rode home and did not pray at the bridge, or at the German post, or at the top of the hill, where every day since the drop she had said the short hard prayers of a courier with a radio crystal in her basket. The Lord's Prayer she had still never finished. Now she said nothing at all. In the place where the praying had been she felt something clean and cold, like the first blue norther of December on the Pedernales, and she mistook the cold for strength.

---

On Wednesday, the nineteenth of July, three trucks stood on the verge at the German post by the bridge below Dieulefit, their canvas rolled back and their engines still ticking from the road.

The men in the truck beds were not German. They wore dark blue tunics and black berets like the Milice, with the SS eagle on their belt buckles, and they called to one another in Arabic and in the French of the Paris back streets. Gaston had warned her about them on Monday and spat after he said the name.

"The North African Brigade," he had said. "Some three hundred Algerians, raised last winter by Henri Lafont, the gangster who runs the French Gestapo out of a house in the rue Lauriston, and by an Algerian named Mohamed el-Maadi, whom his own men call Mohamed SS. Since March the brigade has left massacres behind it in the Dordogne. At Mussidan in June the SD picked out the men of the town and shot them, fifty-two dead in one day, with the brigade's Algerians standing beside the SD men."

Now the Paris Gestapo had lent a detachment of the brigade to Werner Knab's Sipo-SD in Lyon, where Klaus Barbie ran the Gestapo, for the sweep around the Vercors, and the sweep had come south into the Protestant hills, hunting Jews and maquis.

Ruth waited her turn with eggs in her basket and her papers in hand.

On the tailgate of the last truck sat a boy of about seventeen, a rifle too long for him across his knees, eating an apricot down to the stone. He watched her come up the road the way the boys on the courthouse square at home watched girls on a Saturday, and when her eyes crossed his, he grinned. A sergeant in the cab shouted at him, "Kader! Yallah!" The boy flicked the stone into the ditch and went on grinning.

The German who took her papers wore field grey with the black SD diamond on his left cuff, the liaison officer from Lyon, a tidy man with a fresh shaving nick on his chin. He read her card through, looked once at her face, and handed the card back to her.

"Danke, Fräulein Brenner."

The officer turned his back on her the way a man turns his back on a gatepost, spread a map across the hood of the lead truck, and gave the Feldwebel the route in German, quick and plain. They would move at five in the morning, through Dieulefit before the market woke, then east up the gorge road to the high farms. Lyon had the list of farms. Anything that ran out of the houses was a terrorist.

Ruth walked her bicycle ten steps, slipped the chain off the sprocket with the toe of her shoe, and knelt in the dust beside it. She fitted the chain back link by link and heard the route a second time when the Feldwebel read it back to him. When she stood, the boy on the tailgate was still watching her. Ruth gave him nothing, neither a smile nor a frown, and rode.

Gaston was splitting oak behind the house. He heard her out with the maul on his shoulder, leaned the maul against the block, took his corduroy jacket off the nail and went into the trees without a word to Marthe.

Ruth stayed. A courier caught with a pistol in her basket was a dead courier at the first checkpoint, so she carried none. She sat in the loft with the shutters open while the swifts screamed around the roofs until dark.

At a quarter past five the shooting came up the valley, flat cracks thrown off the rock and doubled, like planks dropped on a lumberyard stack, then one long burst, then nothing. The rooster down in the village had not yet crowed.

Gaston came home at noon, wet to the knees, and ate Marthe's soup before he said a word. The maquis had dropped an oak across the gorge road at the narrows and waited above it in the box and juniper. Two of the brigade lay dead in the road. The rest had backed the trucks down the gorge and away toward Lyon, firing into the hillside the whole way, and hit nothing but rock.

"One ran," Gaston said. "A boy. He went off the back of the truck and up into the scrub like a hare, and the others were shouting for him. Kader. Kader." Gaston turned the spoon in his fingers. "Nobody went after him. He was a child with a rifle."

Ruth saw the apricot stone go spinning into the ditch.

"He saw my face," she said. "He heard the German say my name."

Gaston looked at her, then at Marthe.

"Then Lucie Brenner takes the back roads for a while," he said, and bent to his soup again.

Not one refugee in Dieulefit was taken that summer, or in any summer of the war. Ruth had fired no shot. Two men lay dead on the gorge road because of what she had heard on her knees in the dust, and when she went looking inside herself for a prayer, for them or for the boy in the scrub, she found the clean cold place from the sacristy and nothing in it.

On the morning of the twenty-first of July German gliders landed at Vassieux, on the Vercors plateau, and some ten thousand German troops came up the roads from every side. By the twenty-third the plateau was broken.

In the last week of July the American fighter came down over the Jabron.

Ruth heard it from the hill road, a big engine running rough and hot and then not running at all. The airplane topped the ridge from the west, low, trailing white smoke and black, its tail painted red as a new barn. A man came out of it. The parachute broke open above the oaks across the valley and sank into the trees, and a half mile farther on, the fighter went into the hillside with a sound like a door slammed in a stone house.

Gaston found the pilot hanging forty feet up in a white oak with a sprained ankle and a burned hand, and climbed up with a pruning saw in his teeth and cut him free. That night Gaston hauled him into Valfrède flat on his back in the lavender cart under three feet of fresh-cut stems, and a German motorcycle passed on the road with its headlamp painted to a slit, and the rider never turned his head.

---

Ruth climbed the goat path behind Odile with a lamp and a basin of hot water.

The pilot sat in the empty stall with his back against the mountain wall and his bad leg straight out, still in his flying coveralls. Jézabel had her head through the boards, chewing his sleeve with her yellow eyes half shut, and he was letting her. His left hand lay palm down on his thigh in a loose wrap of handkerchief, the skin blistered white wherever the cloth did not reach.

The lamplight found a Black man in the straw. Gaston had said only *l'Américain*.

He looked up into the lamp with a long solemn face, the face strangers had always mistaken for sullen. Then one corner of his long mouth lifted, the way it had lifted one dawn on top of Enchanted Rock when she was thirteen years old with a cedar splint across her nose and both eyes black as a raccoon's.

"You look terrible, Miss Ruth," Sam Mayfield said.

The basin tipped. Hot water ran over her wrists and down her skirt, and she never felt it. The goat went on chewing.

"Your hand," Ruth said at last, in English, and the words came out in pieces. "Let me see your hand."

She knelt and unwound the handkerchief as she had unwound a hundred dressings on the burn ward at the Naval Hospital, with her eyes on the blisters, because she could not yet look at his face again. Odile hung the lamp on a nail, went out to the step and sang about a shepherdess, loud and off-key, so the two of them could hear her every minute of the watch.

---

Sam told her the rest with the lamp turned down to a bead: the farm strip on the Adriatic called Ramitelli, and a P-51 Mustang at last, with a red tail so the bomber crews would know who was guarding them. On his twentieth mission a flak burst over the Rhône had opened his coolant, and he had nursed the airplane east over the hills until the engine seized. **[VERIFY: no 332nd mission over southern France is recorded between 17 July and 6 August 1944; Sam's late-July shootdown is fiction per the bible]**

"I meant to make Corsica," he said. "I made a tree."

On the second night he took her hand.

"I'm sorry about the station in Washington, Ruth. Near an hour I stood by that board with you and never once could ask you to sit. Thirteen years old, I promised to fly. Nineteen years old, I couldn't buy you a cup of coffee."

On the evening of the twenty-eighth Odile's cousin came down out of the Vercors, a boy of sixteen with his feet bleeding through his socks onto Marthe's floor. The maquis had hidden their hospital in a cave in the cliffs, the Grotte de la Luire. On the twenty-seventh the Germans found the cave and shot the wounded who could not stand. They took the doctors and the Jesuit chaplain to Grenoble and shot them there two weeks later, and put seven nurses on a truck for Germany.

Ruth carried the news up the goat path to Sam, flat, the way Vera Atkins told things, and said the Americans would land in the south inside a month, and he would be hidden till then.

"What about you?" he said.

She did not answer.

He took a pocket Bible from his coveralls, its cover warped with sweat, and opened it to a page he had marked the fall they were fifteen, on a sandbar of the Pedernales, the afternoon he drew football plays for her with a willow stick and then read her this instead.

"*Whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God.*" He raised his eyes. "Your own book. Ruth talking."

"Ruth talking to her mother-in-law."

"Mama'd be your mother-in-law. She'd have you shelling peas by sundown."

"Sam. Your commanding officer would never sign, not for you and a white girl. In Texas it's the penitentiary. Two to five years. If we marry anywhere else, the marriage is void the minute we cross back into Texas."

"I know the law, Ruth. I grew up under it." He closed the Bible on his finger. "Texas isn't here. The Army isn't here. A man shot out of the sky gets to see what's left when everybody else is gone, and what's left is you, and the Lord, and a goat."

She laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle.

"I quit God," she said. "Twelve days ago. In a church with a swastika behind the altar."

Sam looked at her the way he used to watch the Army trainers out of San Antonio, steady, waiting.

"Then say the part of the verse you've got in you," he said. "The Lord can say the rest."

---

Pasteur Jean Chabrol heard them out the next morning in the temple with the shutters closed. He had kept eleven Jews in his parsonage attic since 1942. When Ruth finished, he gave one short laugh and shook his head.

"For a hundred years the King of France said a Protestant marriage was no marriage. My great-great-great-grandfather was married by a pastor of the Desert in a ravine below Anduze, at night, with a boy on the rocks watching for the dragoons, and the king's law called his children bastards until 1787." He stood. "Weddings the law refuses to see are an old trade in these hills. God saw that one."

They went up to the temple late on Saturday night, the twenty-ninth of July, under a half moon going down over the Rhône. Gaston and Marthe walked ahead in their Sunday black. Odile kept watch at the bottom of the lane. Sam came last on Gaston's stick, in a borrowed white shirt that would not button at the neck.

The steps at the temple door dipped in the middle like old saddles. The king's men had pulled the first temple down after 1685, and when the law relented, the grandchildren carried the same stones back up the hill. Candle smoke had browned the wall above the pulpit. A Huguenot cross was cut into the pulpit's wooden face, its little dove hanging head-down from the foot. No crucifix hung anywhere. One candle stub burned on a bare wooden communion table, and every shutter was barred tight.

At the door the pastor's wife, Madeleine, worked her own ring over her knuckle, pressed it into Ruth's left palm and closed Ruth's fingers over it. The silver was still warm from her hand, worn thin as wire on the palm side by twenty-six years of washing and kneading.

"Je la reprendrai après la guerre," she said. I'll take it back after the war.

Gaston went no farther than the sill, where he stood with his back to the room and his eyes on the road, the way a boy had once kept watch on the rocks below Anduze, and he left the door open a hand's width so Odile's whistle could carry. Through the gap came the night air off the slope, cool and heavy with lavender.

The flagstones struck cold through the soles of Ruth's shoes, and Pasteur Chabrol studied the two of them over the little flame.

"Parlez bas," he said. "Dieu entend très bien." Speak low. God hears very well.

Marthe drew a folded strip of white silk out of her sleeve and laid it in the pastor's hand. Odile had cut it from Ruth's parachute before Gaston buried the rest under the woodpile. Ruth passed the ring into her right hand. Chabrol laid Sam's right hand over her left, wound the silk twice around both wrists and tied it, and Marthe slid a sprig of lavender out of her own buttonhole and worked it into the knot.

The law of Texas made that knot a felony. The Germans in the valley would have settled it with a firing squad before noon.

Chabrol bent so close that the candle lit the grey stubble on his jaw, and asked Sam in French, slow as a schoolmaster, whether he took this woman for his wife.

"Oui," Sam said, the one word of French he had for it.

The pastor turned his ear to Ruth. Her own *oui* came out so low the flame never stirred, and then the rest rose out of her in English, in the King James she had learned on a hard bench at St. Paul.

"*Whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people,*"

Ruth got no farther.

Down in the village a dog began to bark, four hard barks thrown back off the stone walls. Nobody in the temple moved. Gaston's hand came up flat in the doorway without his head turning. The candle burned straight, and under the silk Ruth felt Sam's pulse, slow and steady as a mantel clock. Then the dog fell silent, and nothing moved on the road.

Chabrol waited, bent close. Sam said the rest for her, so low the pastor had to lean in to catch the words.

"*and thy God my God.*"

Ruth opened her right hand. Sam lifted Madeleine's ring out of her palm with the burned fingers of his left hand, the ones the wrapping left bare, and worked it over her knuckle onto the finger tied to his own. The silver went on a size loose, and Ruth curled her fingers around Sam's to hold it there.

"Voilà," Chabrol said. "Vous êtes mariés. Ne le dites à personne. Pas même à moi." You are married. Tell no one. Not even me.

Marthe unwound the silk and folded it into her sleeve with the lavender still in the knot. The pastor wrote on a sheet of the temple's paper by the candle stub, fast, his pen running off toward the edge: the two names, the village, *le 29 juillet 1944*. Sam signed with his good hand. Ruth signed her new name for the first time in her life. Chabrol signed, then Marthe, and Gaston stepped in off the sill to print his name in big careful capitals. At the foot of the sheet Chabrol wrote one line more and folded the paper twice.

Then the pastor knelt behind the communion table and pried up a flagstone with a pocketknife. In the dry earth beneath lay a shallow tin box holding the true names of the eleven in his attic. The folded sheet went in on top, and he set the stone back and scuffed dust into the cracks with his shoe.

Sam pinched out the candle. All the way up the goat path to the barn, under the last of the moon, Ruth kept her thumb on the ring at the base of her finger and turned it, one slow circle after another.

---

Afterward, in the barn, Sam lifted the chain over his head.

Two tags hung on it, the second on a short length of its own below the first, small notched rectangles of dull metal no longer than her thumb. He unhooked the second and held it to the lamp.

SAMUEL J MAYFIELD
O-826174 T42 43 O
P

"Name and number," he said. "Lockjaw shots, '42 and '43. O for my blood. P for Protestant, the Army's word for Mama's church. They used to stamp your next of kin on there too, till last summer."

"Why two?"

"One stays with me, whatever happens. The other one's supposed to go home."

He laid it in her palm and folded her fingers over it, the way Madeleine had folded them over the ring. Ruth threaded the tag onto a length of string, tied it at her throat, and felt the little rectangle settle against her breastbone, cool, then warm.

Sam turned the lamp down until the flame shrank to a blue seed and died. Over their heads the lavender gave its smell into the dark. In the next stall the goat shifted in her straw and sighed like an old woman, and the barn door, which had not shut true in a hundred years, swung to on its leather hinges and stayed shut.

*The diary has a page for that night, a loose sheet written small and right to the edges in Sweden the next spring and laid in afterward. The old man in the cowhide chair read it to himself tonight while Lily went out for wood, and slid the sheet back between the pages before she came in stamping the cold off her boots.*

---

Ruth and Sam had three days.

On Sunday night Sam taught her to dance. He had no music and one good ankle, so he hummed Duke Ellington under his breath, "Mood Indigo," the way it used to come out of the radio in Mr. Booker Odom's garage on East Commerce, and he moved her in a slow square on the barn floor between the straw and the lamp, his weight on the sound leg and her stocking feet on the cold stone. Jézabel watched over the boards, chewing her cud, one ear going with the tune. Ruth laid her cheek against the borrowed shirt and let him lead, the first time in four years she had given anybody the lead.

On Monday and Tuesday she cut lavender. Late July was harvest in the Drôme, and every woman in Valfrède who could swing a sickle was down in the rows, so one more refugee girl in a kerchief with her sleeves rolled, bent over the purple, drew no eye from the road. Marthe worked the next row and showed Ruth once how to gather a fistful of stems and cut low, just above the wood, and after that said nothing at all. The bees lifted off the blossoms ahead of the blade and settled again behind it. By noon Ruth's palms were black and sticky with the oil, and the smell had gone so deep in her hair that Mama's linen drawer would have hung its head.

Once, straightening with a sheaf in her arms, she looked up the hill. The door of the stone barn stood open a crack, and in the dark of the crack showed a white shirt that would not button at the neck. Ruth looked one second, the way Odile had taught her to look at nothing, and bent to the row again.

"The lavender cuts well this year," Marthe said, and never raised her head.

On Monday night they lay in the straw with the barn door propped open on the stars and made the only plans they ever made out loud. Chicago, Sam said, or Detroit, someplace the law would call them married, or right here in France. A house with a porch on the east side, Ruth said, for the morning. Sam said that after the war he would buy a yellow Piper Cub, the same airplane Mrs. Roosevelt rode in over Tuskegee with Chief Anderson at the stick, and fly it low up the Pedernales on a Sunday morning and waggle the wings over St. Paul, so the whole Vellmer pew would come out on the church steps and crane their necks at the sky.

"Mama would shade her eyes," Ruth said, "and pretend she never met you."

Sam laughed without a sound, and the straw rustled under his shoulders.

On Tuesday night neither of them said the word *last*. Sam turned the lamp down low, and then to nothing. Ruth lay with her ear on his chest and counted his heartbeat the way she had counted pulses on the burn ward at Pearl Harbor, lost the count, started over, and lost it again. Long before the light, the rooster down in the village went off like a cracked bugle, and Sam's arm tightened once across her back and then lay still.

On the morning of the second of August, Ruth stood in the barn door in the grey light with her coat over her arm. Odile's rule was the rule: nothing American on the body on the road, and nothing a girl named Lucie Brenner could not explain. The tag went in waxed paper among the throat lozenges in a little round tin, and the tin went in her coat pocket, where a girl could drop it in a ditch. Madeleine's ring had been on her finger three days. Ruth worked it back over the knuckle, threaded it onto the string at her throat where the tag had hung, and buttoned it out of sight under her blouse, because a ring on the hand of a girl with no husband on her papers was a question at every checkpoint.

"Promise me two things," she said.

Sam sat in the straw with the goat's chin on his shoulder. "Name them."

"Get back to your group. When the Americans come, go to them, and get yourself to Italy."

"I'll get to Italy. What's the other?"

"Tell nobody. Not your squadron. Not your mama. Nobody, until I can tell it myself."

He studied her face and nodded once.

"You have my word," he said.

She went down the goat path without looking back, because Odile had taught her that a girl who looks back is a girl with somebody behind her. The lavender hung wet with dew along the rows, and the bees were still asleep in their hives.
