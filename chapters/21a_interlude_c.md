# The Cabin: III

*The cabin on the Vellmer homeplace, midnight, Christmas 2026*

The diary closed on its ribbon, and for a while the fire did the talking.

The moon had moved off the quilt and onto the floorboards by the door, a white square with the shadow of the window cross laid over it like a brand on a hide. Daniel snored in the recliner, one boot on the floor beside him. Ben had gone to sleep sitting up against his grandfather's shin, a paper chain from the tree wound around his wrist.

Ruthie came across the room from the kitchen doorway, set her cup on the mantel, and sat down on the hearth stones with her back to the fire, the way she used to sit as a little girl when it was time for *Charlotte's Web*.

"Daddy," she said. "I never knew any of it."

"No, baby. Nobody knew a word of it."

Ruthie pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes and held them there. "Ten dollars every Christmas for thirty-four years. *For the namesake.* I used to tape them in a scrapbook. I ate at her table twice in my whole life, and both times I sat there thinking, who is this old white lady, and why does Daddy drive eighty miles to eat her pecan pie?" She dropped her hands. "*Ravensbrück*, Daddy."

"Yes."

"She had four little white scars on her arm. Right here." Ruthie touched the inside of her own left forearm. "In a row. The second time, at that table, she reached for the cream and her sleeve slid back to the elbow. I asked her. She said she'd been careless with a stove. She lied to me."

"She lied to everybody. Lied to Kate for sixty years, and Kate asked her straight out more than once. Lied to a whole town with a straight face every Sunday from the last pew." A hand went out and rested on top of Ruthie's head, the way it used to when she was small. "Miss Ruth told one lie to everyone she ever loved, honey, and she told it so none of them would ever have to carry what she packed in that trunk."

"That's not fair."

"No. Not one bit of it."

Ruthie looked over everything spread out on the quilt: the nurse's cap with the little brown spot on the band, the letters in German and French, the photograph of the six Vellmer children, the strip of grey-and-blue cloth, the Bible with something thin and yellow sticking out of the back cover. Her eyes stopped on the cloth and stayed there.

"Did Mama know any of it?"

"Your mama knew no more than the rest of us. A white lady from the Hill Country who sent money at Christmas, sat in back rows, and left before the punch." The fire popped. "Gloria liked her. Said she had the straightest back of any woman God ever made, and Gloria didn't hand out compliments for free."

---

Marcus hadn't said a word since the graduation story. He sat on the floor against the couch with his phone face down on his knee, looking at the photograph of the Vellmer children on the porch in 1925, at the oldest boy squinting into the sun with a baby on his hip.

"Grandpa. What happened to Walter?"

"You know what happened to Walter."

"No. I mean later. You said they never found him. He was an unknown." Marcus turned the phone over and looked at the screen, then turned it face down again. "Is he still down there? In the ship?"

"No. Walter came home."

Marcus lifted his head.

"After she was gone, four and a half years later."

---

Here is how that went, because Miss Ruth never got to hear it.

When the *Oklahoma* rolled over in Pearl Harbor, she took better than four hundred men down with her. By the time the Navy cut into her hull and righted her and brought the dead up out of the dark, most of them were past knowing, and in the end those boys went into the ground at the Punchbowl, the National Memorial Cemetery of the Pacific, up in the crater of a dead volcano above Honolulu, in forty-six graves marked Unknown. In October of 1949 a military board declared them non-recoverable.

Walter Vellmer lay under one of those stones for sixty-six years.

"Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency," Marcus read off the phone. "June 2015, they dug up all the *Oklahoma* unknowns. Took them to a lab. DNA, dental records, bones. It says they finished in 2021. Three hundred fifty-five identified." He raised his eyes. "Three hundred fifty-five, Grandpa."

"That's right."

"How did they know which one was him?"

"Kate."

Ruthie turned around on the hearth.

"The lab needed family blood. Every one of Walter's brothers and sisters was gone by then, and Miss Ruth with them. Walter had one child on this earth. In 2016 the Navy called the house in San Antonio, and a man came out with a kit, and Kate Hartmann sat at her kitchen table and let him swab the inside of her cheek. Seventy-four years old. She had never seen her daddy's face except in photographs."

"Did she call you?" Ruthie asked.

"She called me that same night. We were speaking again by then." A small smile went toward the fire. "She said it was paperwork. Said nothing would come of it."

The phone call came in the spring of 2018. The lab had matched a set of remains. Not all of him came home, only a few bones, and a few were enough. Boatswain's Mate Walter Vellmer of Pedernales County, Texas, was accounted for at last. The government took seventy-six years to put a name on one sailor.

Kate put down the telephone, went out on her back patio, sat down under the grapefruit tree, and didn't come in for supper.

The Navy flew him home that June. Six sailors in dress whites carried a little flag-draped casket down the ramp at the San Antonio airport as if it held the whole weight of a grown man. Forty motorcycles of the Patriot Guard rode in front of the hearse up Highway 87, a rancher outside Comfort stood in the bar ditch with his hat over his heart, the people of Fredericksburg stood bareheaded on Main Street in front of the old Nimitz Hotel, and in Martinsburg folks lined the street two deep with flags from the edge of town to the courthouse square.

Kate buried him in Martinsburg, in the churchyard at St. Paul, under the bell tower his father had topped out in 1911, twenty miles down the river from where Chester Nimitz was born, the hometown hero who had drawn Walter into the Navy. Gloria stood at the back of the tent on her husband's arm. A sailor went down on one knee in front of Kate's folding chair and said the words, and Kate held that flag in her lap the whole rest of the service with both hands flat on top of it, the way Miss Ruth used to hold her hand on a Bible.

Kate was seventy-six years old. The next year Kate was gone too. She got to bury her daddy first, and that is a mercy denied to many a child.

"Miss Ruth never knew he came home," Lily said. She had a sleeve over her mouth.

"No, baby. She went to her grave knowing only that her brother lay under a stone marked Unknown."

Marcus set the phone face down on the floor and pushed it away with one finger.

"She sat in that last pew for fifty-seven years," Ruthie said, "and God didn't even give her that."

The fire settled.

"No," the old man said. "He never gave her that."

Ruthie's words stood, because she was right, and because Miss Ruth would have let them stand. There's a kind of preacher who'll tell you God gave her something better. That preacher has never sat up a night in a hospital.

---

The paper chain came off Ben's wrist, gently, so he wouldn't wake, and went onto the quilt. Two fingers went to the top button of the grey cardigan to be sure it was still fastened at the throat.

Marcus looked at the diary. The back half was blank. The entries stopped in 1946.

"There's no more diary," he said.

"No. After 1946 Miss Ruth quit keeping one. The rest comes from people. From Kate. From a lawyer in Martinsburg, and a dog man from Houston, and a Japanese herbalist in Comfort. From a bus driver named Lyle. From a whole football team."

"A football team?"

"Twelve boys in green and gold. They carried her."

Marcus took his phone off the floor. He left the screen dark. He held the phone in both hands like a hymnal.

"Okay," he said. "Tell it."

"Sixteen years on the city ambulance teach a man the churches of San Antonio by their parking lots," the old man said. "Out on Loop 1604 sat one that used to be the Fiesta Dinner Theater. In the nineties we ran Sunday calls to that lot every August, old men who had stood too long in the sun waiting for the doors to open. Three thousand people went through those doors every week."

"Did Miss Ruth go there?" Lily asked.

"No, baby. Kate did, for years." The old man looked at the fire. "So did a lot of good people. None of them knew what that building would cost a dog man from Houston."

So another log went on the fire, and the story moved on to the spring Miss Ruth was eighty-seven, when she walked to a lawyer's office on West Main in Martinsburg over a county tax form, and a stranger with a yellow dog caught her as she pitched forward on the lawyer's front steps.
