# 32. Pallbearers

*Christmas morning, 2026, going on one. The cabin on the Vellmer homeplace.*

Past midnight the night stops being a story and becomes the room where we sit. The fire is down to coals and one oak log that refuses to quit. Daniel sleeps in the recliner with his fingers laced on his belly like a man laid out for viewing. Ruthie stands in the kitchen doorway with her coffee cup, her shoulder against the frame, the way her mother used to stand. Lily has the scrap of striped camp cloth folded in her lap like a church napkin. Ben lies on the quilt, losing to sleep an inch at a time. Marcus still holds his phone, but the phone rides face down on his knee now, and the screen has been dark for an hour.

"Grandpa," Lily says. "Did a lot of people come? To her funeral?"

"The whole town came, baby. That morning deserves a second telling, now that you children know a little of who was in the box."

---

Kate Hartmann called our house in San Antonio at a quarter to six on Christmas morning, 2013. Your grandmother Gloria, in her robe, answered the telephone by the icebox. The sound Gloria made into that receiver is still in the walls of our kitchen.

Kate told her three things, flat as a grocery list, the way a nurse learns to say the worst news in her plainest voice. Miss Ruth was dead. Somebody had shot her on her porch. Honey was alive at the vet on the Kerrville road, and Kate needed somebody to sit with her.

Lyle Mosher had carried the dog there. Emil Klier telephoned him from the porch before the deputies had the tape up, and Lyle came down Elm Street in his pickup in a T-shirt and house shoes, wrapped Honey in the horse blanket he kept behind the seat, and drove her out the Kerrville road with her head on his knee. The clinic out there still stood in the old filling station where Dr. Paul Ahrens had doctored the county's mules and heifers after the war, and his grandson Matt doctored them now. Matt Ahrens unlocked the door at a quarter past twelve in his house shoes too.

Under the big lamp he shaved the shoulder, went in with a pair of long forceps and lifted out the bullet, and the slug rang in the steel pan like a dime in a collection plate. He sealed it in a pill vial, wrote the hour on the lid, and at daylight handed it to a Texas Ranger.

"An inch," he told Lyle, and laid two fingers on the dog's ribs to show the place. "One more inch, and that bullet finds her heart."

Lyle asked for nothing but a place on the floor. He spread the horse blanket on the concrete beside the recovery kennel, lay down on it in his clothes with two fingers through the wire on Honey's paw, and slept there that night and Christmas night after it.

Kate told us to stay put until the federal men had cleared out of the house, so Gloria and I drove up the morning of the twenty-sixth and went back home that night to fetch Ruthie, Daniel and the boy.

Yellow tape still hung across the gate of the Elm Street house. The porch boards had been scrubbed, and the scrubbing showed, a pale square of wet wood under the swing and another at the head of the steps. The stable and every figure in it had gone to San Antonio in the trunk of a federal sedan, as evidence.

Kate found the envelope behind a 1994 church directory in the kitchen drawer under the telephone. Ruth's hand, tall and slanted, the same hand that had addressed a money order to Grandma Hattie every Christmas for seventeen years. Dated 1987. Five lines.

*Pine box. No eulogy. Job 13:15. Ein feste Burg. Put me next to Papa.*

"Twenty-six years," Kate said, "and she never changed a word."

That afternoon Coach Ray Winslow ducked under the yellow tape and stood on the porch with his cap in both hands. He could not look at the pale square under the swing. He looked at Kate instead.

"Ma'am," he said, "the boys want to carry her."

Kate said yes before he finished the sentence.

"How many boys?" Ben asks from the quilt, with his eyes shut.

"Twelve, son." Every senior and every starter who had ridden that bus with her, down to Kurt Hoerster, the hundred-and-forty-pound sophomore safety. Coach told them a casket has only six handles. Caleb Waidner, the quarterback, told him a handle has room for two hands, and two hands means two boys.

---

On Saturday, the twenty-eighth of December, the same north wind came down on the square as if it had business in Martinsburg, and by ten o'clock the sky had gone the blue that only comes behind a norther.

Lyle had driven bus No. 7 to St. Paul with the whole team aboard, and he parked it at the curb right in front of the church steps, where the hearse would have to go around it. Lyle wore his brown Sunday suit, the sleeves grown short on him over thirty years of county funerals.

Honey rode up front, on the first seat behind the driver, on a folded horse blanket.

The dog man from Houston came at half past nine. He went up the bus steps and knelt in the aisle in his good trousers.

He laid the back of his hand by her nose. Her tail thumped twice against the seat. The vet had shaved her right shoulder down to the bare skin and closed the hole with a row of black stitches like the lacing on a football, and the dog man ran his thumb along those stitches the way a horseman runs a palm down a foreleg, feeling for heat. Then he pressed a finger to her gum and watched the pink come up again.

"She's clean," he told Lyle. "No infection. She'll carry a limp a while." He looked at the dog again. "She did her job."

He took a small spray bottle out of his coat pocket, misted the row of stitches from one end to the other, and pressed the bottle into Lyle's palm: manuka honey and colloidal silver, mixed by his own hand.

"Going to leave her here," Lyle said. "Out of the wind."

"You can try," the dog man said.

Lyle dropped the window halfway for air and went to line up his boys.

"She came out the window," Ruthie says from the doorway. "Daddy, tell them she came out the window."

"She came out the window." A Labrador with a bullet hole in her shoulder went out a half-dropped bus window the way a calf goes over a fence, all at once and ugly, landed on three legs, shook herself once from the ears back, and limped up the church steps after the boys.

---

The Mayfields sat in the back pew on the left because the Mayfields came late. A jackknifed cattle trailer below Comfort held us forty minutes, and by the time we climbed the steps of St. Paul the whole county was inside, with men standing two deep along the side walls. Miss Tipton from the storage place stood among them with a handkerchief balled in her fist. Dixie Beck moved her husband and both her boys out of the back pew for a family she had never once seen in Martinsburg.

Gloria sat on the aisle in her good black coat. Ruthie sat beside her with a two-year-old asleep on her shoulder, and Daniel kept an arm along the pew behind them both.

"That two-year-old was me," Marcus says.

"You were that boy. Slept through the whole thing, son, with your mouth open, the way your daddy is sleeping in that recliner tonight."

"Figures," Marcus says, and for the first time all night he almost smiles.

Up front, alone in the fourth pew on the left, the Vellmer pew since before the First World War, sat Kate, seventy-one years old and a widow eight years.

An old Japanese man in a black suit too big in the shoulders stood against the back wall at the end of our pew with a straw hat over his heart, and never once took a seat. Before the service he set a small paper sack on the pew beside me.

"For the dog," he said, very quietly. "Please give it to the lady sitting alone up front."

Inside sat a jar of local honey with a hand-printed label: *For Honey. One spoon each morning.*

"She told me once she had been behind a fence too," Kenji Morita said.

Three rows ahead, a Martinsburg lawyer named Eric Rogers sat with his briefcase between his feet and his eyes on the floor through every hymn.

Then the doors opened behind us, and the cold came in, and they brought her.

Twelve boys in green-and-gold letter jackets carried a plain pine box, two to a handle. Tyler Kott had the front handle on the left, jaw shut, eyes on the altar. The year before he had stood at a grave while men in uniform folded the flag off his brother Jacob's casket, and he was the only boy of the twelve who already knew how to walk slow behind the dead. Caleb Waidner walked across from him. DeShawn Hollins had the middle handle on the right. Brody Vossberg had a bruise going yellow along his jaw, and Gloria looked at the bruise and then at me, and neither one of us believed it came from football.

The dog walked at the head of the casket on the left side, where Miss Ruth always kept her, toenails ticking on the old pine floor, the shaved shoulder working under the stitches. When the boys set the box on the bier below the altar, Honey lay down beside it with her chin on her paws and her one ear standing straight.

---

The service followed her five lines. No eulogy. Pastor Mark Albrecht was forty-four, and that morning he looked every day of seventy. He read one verse plain.

"'Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him.'"

He closed the book. "Ruth Vellmer argued with God out loud in my office more than once," he said. "She never walked out on Him. I'm not going to say anything else, because she told me not to, and I am more afraid of her this morning than I have ever been of anybody."

A few people laughed, and the laughing was worse than the crying.

Pastor Albrecht had never read the loose sheet from Sweden. The pastor said what he knew, and what he knew was true. Ruth had quit God once, in writing, in a sacristy. God had not signed the paper.

Then the organ started, and the old Germans in the back half of the church stood without being told. Men in their eighties held their hats and sang in the language their grandparents carried over on the boat. *Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott.* The young people sang the English, two languages going at once, as they had four nights before, with Ruth singing among the old ones. In the fourth pew, by herself, Kate sang the German, a half-beat ahead of the organist, which was exactly how Miss Ruth had sung it for eighty years.

From the back pew a man can see the whole church at once.

In the fifth pew, behind the Vellmer pew, an old man sat with an aluminum walker parked in the aisle and a hymnal open on his knees. The man was Otto Lindauer, ninety years old, the biggest of the boys behind the cotton gin in the spring of 1937. Grandma Hattie said that name the rest of her life, and never once kindly. Otto did not sing. When the hymn ended he pulled himself up on that walker, turned around in the pew and looked straight back at the Mayfields, and he held the look until his eyes ran. Then he bowed his head to us, the way a man bows at the altar rail. Seventy-six years late, the turn in that pew was as far down the aisle as an old man on a walker could come, and Otto Lindauer came that far. Remember him, children, when we get to the men.

Clayton Rusk came through the door during the second verse and stood in the back corner, not six feet from Gloria's elbow, in a camel-hair overcoat that cost more than Lyle's bus. He sized up that church the way he had sized up the Vellmer land all spring, pricing the pews. Up front, Honey lifted her head off the floor. She did not growl in church. She only looked at him, the way she had graded every boy on that bus in August.

Gloria's fingers closed on my arm. "Not here," she whispered.

That man went unhit that morning, children. Thirteen years the wanting has stayed with me, and thirteen years the man has stayed unhit, and the Lord can count that to my credit or to my shame, whichever way His ledger runs.

---

At the benediction, the funeral director, a careful little man named Hoffmann, reached for Honey's collar to lead her clear of the boys.

Lyle stood in the third pew. "Leave her where she lies," he said.

He did not say it loud. Thirty years on that bus, and Lyle had never once raised his voice. Hoffmann let go of the collar.

The doors opened, and that north wind came into St. Paul Lutheran as if it had been waiting at the door all morning. The gust took the pastor's white stole and stood it straight out sideways like a flag. The twelve came down those limestone steps with that pine box as level as a dinner table, and the yellow dog walked beside them on the left, hitching on the shaved shoulder and keeping pace.

Across the street a young sheriff's deputy stood beside his cruiser with his hat held over his badge and watched the boys carry her down the steps. His name meant nothing to any of us that morning. Before January was out, all of Pedernales County would know that name, and know the young man told the truth at the inquest when the truth cost him.

Down the block from his cruiser idled a white van out of Austin, its mast up and KVAN 36 NEWS painted on the door, and a woman in a red coat stood on the curb with a microphone, waiting for the boys.

The churchyard lies behind St. Paul, down a gravel lane under a row of live oaks. The boys never used the hearse. They carried her the whole quarter mile in that wind, and not one of them changed hands. Dale Kunkel and Harlan Moellering walked bareheaded behind the box, close enough to catch it if a boy stumbled. At the end of the lane the backhoe had opened the ground beside a granite stone that reads FRIEDRICH VELLMER, a stone Fritz cut himself years before he needed it, and beside that one, KATHARINA VELLMER. A green tent snapped and bellied over the hole.

They set her on the straps. Every boy laid a hand on the lid, one after another, and stepped back into a line. Tyler Kott held his hand over his heart through the committal. DeShawn's grandmother, under a black net veil she had bought for Dr. King's funeral in 1968, sang "Precious Lord" with no music, in a voice like a cello. Dr. King had asked for that song on a motel balcony in Memphis, minutes before the shot.

Pastor Albrecht said the old words. "Earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust." The norther carried off most of them.

Then people started back up the lane toward the fellowship hall, where Dixie Beck had Inge Lochte, Doris Ahrens and the whole St. Paul quilting circle taking orders from her like privates on KP and a place saved for Gloria at the head table. At the top of the lane the woman in the red coat caught DeShawn Hollins with her microphone and asked him what kind of woman lay in that pine box. DeShawn looked straight into her camera.

"She rode our bus," he said. "She sat up front every Friday, and she never once looked at me like I was anything but a Mustang. Put that on the news."

KVAN 36 never aired it. The station's story ran in January, ninety seconds about a Hill Country widow who had "profiled" her Middle Eastern neighbors, and DeShawn Hollins was nowhere in it.

Eric Rogers caught me on the gravel and shook my hand and held it.

"Mr. Mayfield," he said. "Miss Ruth left instructions that concern you. I'll call after the first of the year."

All the way up that lane my guess was a pie plate Gloria had left on the homeplace porch one summer. A man can be that wrong and still keep walking.

After the committal Otto Lindauer was waiting at the bottom of the church steps on his walker, in a north wind, with his hat in his hand. "I was the one at the gin," he said to me. "In 1937. I put your uncle against that iron and I said the word, and I have known it every day for seventy-six years. I'm asking your family's pardon. Nobody else is left to give it." A man can only forgive what was done to him and his, children. Uncle Sam was not there to answer, and Grandma Hattie was not either, so I answered for the Mayfield name. "Mr. Lindauer," I said, "the Mayfields forgive you." My hand came up and took his, the way Corrie's hand came up in Munich, and his hand was cold and shaking, and he held on a long time.

Behind us, the dog lay flat on the ground.

She lay at the very edge of the hole, on the red clay the backhoe had turned out, with her chin on her front paws and her stitched shoulder toward the wind. Kate called her. The dog man called her, low. Honey did not move off the clay.

"Did she stay all night?" Ben asks. His eyes have gone round as quarters for a boy who was asleep a minute ago.

"No, son. Lyle stayed with her."

An hour later Gloria and I found Lyle in his brown Sunday suit, sitting on the cold ground beside that dog with his back against the tent pole. One big red hand rested on her side. At the far end of the gravel lane, bus No. 7 sat idling with the door folded open and nobody aboard, the heater roaring on the driver's side the way it always did, blowing warm air on an empty seat.

Gloria asked him if he wanted to come in out of the wind.

"Directly," Lyle said.

When the sun got low and the cold came up out of the ground, he stood, stiff in every joint, and gathered that sixty-two-pound dog up in both arms the way he had gathered her off the porch on Christmas Eve. He carried her up the lane to the bus, set her on the first seat behind the driver, the seat where Miss Ruth had ridden all fall, folded the horse blanket over her and drove her back to Kate on Elm Street.

Kate stayed on in that house through the first weeks of January, packing it into boxes, and in the first of those weeks she laid the Bible from the nightstand in the trunk with the rest. The dog would not stay inside the house. The first morning Honey pulled the everyday grey wool coat Miss Ruth wore to the mailbox and the feed store down off its hook by the front door, carried it out to the porch in her soft mouth the way she had carried the mail, laid it on the boards by the swing and lay down on it. She stayed on that coat. She ate when Kate set the bowl on the grey wool, and she drank when Kate set the water beside it. Every morning at a quarter past eleven, when the carrier's Jeep stopped at the box, the dog lifted her head off the coat and watched the gate, and every morning the carrier walked the mail up the bricks herself and laid it on the swing, because nobody on that porch would fetch it.

On the second Sunday night in January, Lyle's pickup stopped at the gate. Lyle came up the steps, nodded to Kate in the doorway, knelt on the boards and lifted the dog, coat and all, the grey sleeves hanging down over his arms. He set her on the seat of his truck and shut the door easy, the way a man shuts a door on a sleeping child.

Kate stood in the doorway and let him take the dog.

Honey never slept on Elm Street again.

---

Lily smooths the camp cloth on her knee with the flat of her hand.

"Grandpa. The people in the church. Did they know? About Pearl Harbor, and France, and . . ." She touches the cloth. "This?"

They knew she rode the bus. They knew she was a Vellmer and stubborn as a mesquite stump, and that she had never married, and the old women would have told you over coffee, proud as if she were their own, that no man in Pedernales County had ever been good enough for Fritz Vellmer's youngest. They knew she left soup.

Nobody in that church had ever seen the nurse's cap, or knew she had sat in a chair on the fifth floor of 84 Avenue Foch, or knew whose letters lay tied with a faded blue ribbon in a footlocker under her iron bed in the stone house.

The Mayfields did not know either. Isaiah Mayfield knew a schoolyard story, seventeen Christmas money orders, and one sentence a white lady in a navy-blue hat had said to him at a college graduation in 1968, a sentence he could not make sense of for forty-six years.

Twelve boys carried her down those steps into the north wind with their hands on that pine, and a whole town stood in the cold and watched them do it, and not one soul there knew who they were carrying.

Marcus turns the phone over in his hands.

"What happened to the men?" he says. "The ones who did it."

Nobody in the cabin speaks for a long time. Ruthie, in the doorway, sets her cup on the counter.

"The men are the next part, son," the old man says. "The next part is no Christmas story."
