# 15. Ravensbrück

*Ravensbrück concentration camp, north of Berlin, August to December 1944*

The sand was what Ruth remembered first, fine and pale as sugar, the same sand that lay on the bottom of the Pedernales where the river slowed below the pecans. The women had spent a week behind the wire of a camp outside Saarbrücken. A Frenchwoman spat its name through the slats as the train pulled out, *Neue Bremm*, and when the cattle cars opened at last, they opened on pine woods and white sand. **[VERIFY: Szabo's group held at Neue Bremm, Saarbrücken, en route; arrival at Ravensbrück about August 22, 1944]**

Women spilled out of the cars into it, blinking, filthy, some of them barefoot. Guards in grey capes walked the line with German shepherds straining at their leashes, and every dog had been trained on women.

Beyond the pines a lake spread flat and blue, and across the lake a church steeple rose over the red roofs of a town. **[VERIFY: view of Fürstenberg and the Schwedtsee from the camp]** The women marched up a sand road to a wall topped with wire. Inside, long green wooden barracks stood in rows, the streets between them raked so smooth they looked combed. Red salvia bloomed in a flower bed in front of the commandant's building. Beside the salvia stood a pile of women's shoes taller than a man.

---

In the shower block the order came in German, shouted: strip, everything on the counter.

The block was a long concrete room with drains in the floor and a counter where prisoners in striped dresses took it all: clothes, shoes, photographs, a baby's knitted sock one woman had carried from Warsaw. A man in a white coat looked into mouths and between legs. Two women with hand clippers sheared heads, and the hair lay on the wet floor in drifts, black and brown and red and grey.

Ruth had the grey coat in her hands. The guard at the head of the line had turned to hit somebody, and Ruth had perhaps four seconds. She lifted the left front of the hem to her mouth as if wiping her face, bit through her own knots, and worked the tag out of the fold with her teeth and onto her tongue. Then she laid the coat on the counter.

A Polish prisoner behind the counter, a woman with palms scarred white, saw the coat, saw the shut mouth, and looked away on purpose. She folded the coat onto a pile with a hundred others. Ruth never saw that coat again.

She stood naked in the line with the tag under her tongue and the string at her throat. A guard with a broad pink face and yellow braids pinned around her head saw the silver shine under the bulb. The guard took the ring between two fingers and snapped the string with one jerk that burned a line across the back of Ruth's neck.

"Silber," the guard said, and laughed. "Für eine wie dich?" Silver. For one like you?

"Bitte," Ruth said. "Please. Es gehört nicht mir." It isn't mine.

The guard buttoned the ring into her own tunic pocket and patted the flap. Then she slapped Ruth across the mouth hard enough to split the inside of her lip against her teeth, and Ruth tasted blood and metal together and kept her jaw shut. The ring was worth almost nothing to the guard, which was the whole point of taking it.

The clippers took Ruth's hair down to the scalp. The women at the counter gave her a striped dress of grey and blue too thin to stop a breeze, a pair of wooden clogs, a red triangle for a political prisoner and a number on a cloth strip to sew over her heart.

---

She kept the tag under her tongue for a week.

She learned to sleep on her side with her jaw clamped. She learned to drink the turnip soup past it in small swallows, with her tongue pressed hard on the metal like a thumb on a loose shingle in the wind. At roll call she stood in the rank in the dark with the corners of the little rectangle against the floor of her mouth and the taste of it, sour and coppery, in every breath, and she was afraid every hour of the day of two things: that a guard would make her speak, and that she would choke in her sleep and swallow Sam whole.

In the second week she traded half a day's bread to a Czech woman from the tailor shop for the loan of a needle until evening. She worked a stitch open in the side seam of the striped dress, low on the left where the cloth rode against her leg, pulled a thread from her blanket, and sewed the tag into the seam with stitches so small that the Czech woman, taking her needle back, looked straight at the place and could not find them.

---

In those first weeks another of Baker Street's girls, a wireless operator named Eileen Nearne who had been taken in Paris in July, refused to do the camp's work and was told she would be shot if she refused again.

In the first days of September, Violette Szabo volunteered for a work party.

"Torgau," she said. "A factory. Denise and Lilian are coming, and the food is supposed to be better, and better than this would be anything." She was hollow under the cheekbones already and still grinning. "Come with us, Texas."

Ruth could not make the march to Torgau. The four burns on her left forearm had gone bad on the train, the neat row of dots swollen into one yellow wound that wept through the rag she had tied around it, and a red line was climbing the inside of her arm toward the armpit. A prisoner doctor looked at the line, sucked her teeth and sent her to the Revier, the camp infirmary.

"Next time," Violette said, and kissed her on both cheeks. Denise Bloch shook her hand formally. Lilian Rolfe, coughing, pressed a crust of bread into Ruth's good hand and would not let Ruth return it.

Ruth watched them march out the gate five abreast, Violette in the front rank swinging her arms like a girl going to the pictures. **[VERIFY: date the Torgau work party left Ravensbrück, early September 1944]**

In the Revier, three to a cot, she met the rabbits: four Polish girls from Lublin, students and teachers, whose legs SS doctors had cut open in 1942 and 1943 and packed with bacteria and slivers of wood and ground glass to make wounds like a soldier's, and then given some of them sulfa drugs and some of them nothing, to see which girls lived. The girls called themselves *króliki*, rabbits. One girl's calf had a trench in it from knee to ankle where the muscle had been cut away, and the skin had healed shiny and purple over the bone like a plum skin over the stone.

"One of the doctors was a woman," the girl said in good German, rewrapping the leg in paper bandage. "She was very correct."

The girl told her, too, that a letter home could carry two letters at once, one in pencil for the censor and one between the lines in the one ink no guard could take from a prisoner, her own water. A mother in Lublin had held such a page to a hot iron and watched the names of the cut girls come up brown. The Farm had taught Ruth the same trick with lemon juice and a candle, and Ruth said nothing about the Farm.

Ruth had watched Navy surgeons at Pearl Harbor stand twenty hours at a table cutting dead flesh off boys so the boys might live. She lay on the cot with her arm throbbing and thought about a doctor washing her hands after that work and going home to her supper.

A prisoner nurse lanced the wound with a razor blade and squeezed it out like a boil, and on the fourth day the red line stopped climbing.

---

Two Dutch sisters had come into the camp in September, from a prison in Holland, and in October the guards moved them, and Ruth with them, into Barracks 28. **[VERIFY: ten Boom sisters arrived at Ravensbrück from Vught in early September 1944]**

The barracks had been built for four hundred women, by the count the old prisoners gave, and held something like fourteen hundred. **[VERIFY: capacity and population of Barracks 28, as given in Corrie ten Boom's account]** The bunks went three high, board platforms with straw gone to dust, five and six women to a platform built for two. Among the French women on Ruth's tier were two nurses from the Vercors, from the cave at La Luire. Ruth knew the name of the cave before they said it, and she never told them how.

The first night, the fleas found her. She woke on the middle tier with her ankles and the backs of her knees on fire, and scratched until she bled, and the woman beside her said in French, "Welcome to the château."

The sisters slept across the aisle. The older one was frail and grey, with a soft face and a way of smiling at nothing. The younger was square-shouldered, past fifty, with a watchmaker's narrowed eyes. On the second night Ruth saw the younger sister draw a small brown book from under her dress, where it hung in a cloth bag on a string between her shoulder blades.

Ruth would have known a Bible in the dark by its shape.

Their names were Corrie and Betsie ten Boom, from a watch shop in Haarlem. Ruth learned the rest in pieces. The family had hidden Jews behind a false wall in their house, because their father, Casper, had told a Jewish woman at his door, *In this household, God's people are always welcome.* Somebody had betrayed them. Casper ten Boom was eighty-four, and he had died in prison nine days after the arrest.

That night Corrie read aloud in Dutch and put the words into German herself for the women crowding the platforms, and a Frenchwoman put the German into French, and a Polish woman into Polish, the words passing down the bunks like a hymn sung in a round. Ruth lay on her back on the middle tier with her arms folded and listened to the German and did not lift her head.

The reading that night came from First Thessalonians. *In every thing give thanks.*

Across the aisle Ruth heard the sisters argue about the verse. Corrie said nothing in that room deserved thanks. Betsie began to count on her fingers: the two of them together, the Bible not found, the women packed so close that more of them could hear the reading. Then Betsie added the fleas, and Corrie flatly refused, and Betsie thanked God for the fleas anyway, out loud, in Dutch and then in German so the women around her could hear it.

Ruth turned on her side, away from the sisters, toward the rag-stuffed window.

*Fool,* she thought. *You poor old fool.*

---

The guards never came into Barracks 28.

They came to the door, and they beat women in the camp street, but they did not come inside, and inside the sisters held their services every night, and the overseers kept their distance. Nobody understood the reason until Betsie heard it from a supervisor's own mouth. The guards would not come in because of the fleas.

Betsie laughed at the news, a small surprised sound like a girl finding a coin in an old coat pocket. Ruth lay on the middle tier scratching the bleeding bites at her ankle and felt something she refused to give a name.

She did not pray. She did not join the singing. Some nights she went and sat at the end of the sisters' platform anyway, because the bodies there were warm and nobody could make her sing.

"You are American," Betsie said one night in English.

"Texas."

"I have read about Texas. Cowboys." Betsie smiled. "You are angry with God."

"I'm not anything with God."

"Ah." Betsie patted her hand, the way Katharina Vellmer used to pat a round of bread dough. "Then you are very angry."

---

The cold came early, down off the Baltic with sleet in its teeth. The women stood at roll call on the parade ground in the black before dawn, in thin striped dresses, for hours, while the guards counted and recounted. Women who fainted were beaten where they fell. Women died standing and were held up by their neighbors so the count would come out right. In November Ruth traded two days' bread for a man's brown overcoat out of the clothing room, a broad white cross painted down its back, the paint still tacky. The coat was some dead woman's. Ruth did not ask whose. **[VERIFY: late-1944 issue of civilian coats marked with painted crosses]**

At night, under the coat, her fingers went to the side seam of the striped dress, and the small hard rectangle was still there.

Betsie began to fail at the end of November. First her legs went, and Corrie held her up at roll call with a Dutch woman on Betsie's other side, while Ruth stood in the row behind and watched Betsie's knees bend like wet paper. One morning the wind off the lake tore the thin grey scarf from Betsie's shoulders and dropped it in the frozen mud. Ruth stooped out of her row, snatched it up and wound it twice around Betsie's neck while the overseer had her back to the rows, and Betsie touched the knot with two fingers. "Dank je, Texas," Betsie said, eyes front. "There is no pit so deep that He is not deeper still." Then her hands began to shake. She coughed blood into the hem of her dress and folded the hem over so Corrie would not see it.

Ruth saw the blood.

Some nights Betsie talked about the days after the war, about a big house with gardens for people who had been hurt like this, where they could learn to live again, and she talked as if she had already walked through its rooms. Ruth lay on the next platform in the dark and listened, and did not say one word to spoil it.

In the middle of December, on a morning so cold the breath froze in Ruth's nostrils, Betsie could not stand at all. The orderlies came for her with a stretcher, and Corrie walked beside it down the camp street. Ruth watched from the barracks door until the stretcher turned the corner at the Revier.

On Saturday, the sixteenth of December, 1944, Betsie ten Boom died in the camp hospital.

A Dutch prisoner named Mien had found Corrie that morning and taken her by the hand to the washroom window behind the Revier. Corrie came back to the barracks that afternoon with a face Ruth carried the rest of her life. Ruth did not ask her one question. At dusk Ruth walked alone to the same washroom, where the dead lay on the concrete floor, naked, stacked like stovewood, waiting for the cart. One window gave onto the room, and she put her face to the glass.

Ruth knew how the dead looked in that camp, grey skin over bone, mouths open, eyes open, faces caught in the last pain of their lives.

Betsie's face was young. The lines were gone from it and the hollows had filled. She lay among the other bodies with her hands at her sides and looked like a woman of thirty who had fallen asleep after a long day in her garden.

Ruth walked fifty yards down the camp street, put both palms flat against a frozen barracks wall and stood with her eyes shut. God said nothing to her. She asked Him nothing.

---

On the twenty-first of December, the shortest day of the year, the SS men beyond the wire kept no Christmas. Himmler had given every SS man a clay lamp cut with runes, the Julleuchter, to light at the winter solstice in place of a candle for the Christ child, and that night the windows of the guards' quarters glowed with small low flames.

In Barracks 28 a farm girl from somewhere east of Warsaw, a quiet girl who had never spoken ten words of German, sat straight up on the bottom tier in the dark. The voice that came out of her was a man's voice, low and amused, in the German of Berlin. The voice called Ruth by the name the Drôme had given her, *Lucie*, a name nobody in that camp had ever heard. The voice told her that her husband with the red-tailed airplane would never come for her, and then it laughed, and the women on the tiers around the girl drew their knees up and crossed themselves.

Nobody in that camp knew Ruth Vellmer had a husband.

Ruth had read the fifth chapter of Mark at her mother's knee out of the German Bible, the man among the tombs who cut himself with stones, and the voice that answered Jesus, *My name is Legion: for we are many.* Ruth knew that laugh. That laugh was not madness. Ruth had heard madness on the burn ward at Pearl Harbor, and madness was afraid. The voice in the girl was not afraid of anything in the room.

Ruth had not spoken to God since the church in the Drôme, and she did not speak to Him now. She used His Name the way a courier uses a password. She climbed down from her tier, laid her palm on the girl's cold forehead and said the only words that came, in the German of her mother's Bible: "*Im Namen Jesu Christi, schweig.*" In the name of Jesus Christ, be silent.

The girl fell back on the boards and slept like a child.

For the space of one breath Ruth saw that room the way Elisha's servant saw the hills around Dothan when the prophet prayed, *LORD, I pray thee, open his eyes, that he may see.* The dark of Barracks 28 was not empty. Along the rafters and down the aisle between the tiers stood a host Ruth had no words for, tall and still as the guard at a king's door, and the thing that had laughed out of the girl was gone the way a rat goes when the lamp comes in. Then the breath went out of her, and there was only the dark, and the fleas, and a girl asleep.

In the morning the girl remembered nothing. The women on her tier would not sleep beside her again, and nobody in Barracks 28 said the word out loud. For thirty-two years Ruth told herself it had been typhus and fear, and for thirty-two years Ruth knew better.

---

In the last days of December a guard at roll call read out Corrie's number and ordered her to the front. Before she went, Corrie turned in the rank and took both of Ruth's hands in her watchmaker's grip.

"Your name. Your home. Quickly."

"Ruth Vellmer. Martinsburg. Pedernales County, Texas."

Corrie said it back twice, getting it right, the way a watchmaker counts the teeth on a gear. Then the guard pulled her away toward the administration building, and Ruth did not see her again in that place. Years later Ruth learned that a clerk had made a mistake with a paper, and that a week afterward the women of Corrie's age were sent to the gas.

On the last night of 1944, the platform across the aisle held five strangers. Ruth lay on the middle tier under the coat with the white cross on its back, scratching the fleas, and listened for the Dutch voices that did not come. Down the bunks somewhere in the dark, a Frenchwoman began to say the Twenty-third Psalm from memory, slowly, in German, the way she had heard it from the sisters all autumn.

Ruth did not join her. Her hand lay flat on the left side seam of the striped dress, over the place where the metal was, and stayed there until the whistle blew for roll call.
