# Prologue: Pallbearers

*St. Paul Lutheran Church, Martinsburg, Texas. Saturday, December 28, 2013.*

The wind came down out of the north that morning like a man with business in Martinsburg who meant to finish it by noon. The gusts rattled the storefront glass around the courthouse square, snapped the flag on its pole straight as a plank, and by ten o'clock had shoved every cloud in Pedernales County off toward the Gulf, so that the sky over St. Paul Lutheran was the hard, scrubbed blue that only comes behind a norther, the kind of blue that makes a limestone church look as if the masons knocked off yesterday.

The back pew on the left is the coldest seat in that church. The doors open right behind it, and every breath of December comes straight down the aisle and finds the back of your neck. The Mayfields sat there because the Mayfields came late. A cattle trailer jackknifed across Highway 87 below Comfort and held us forty minutes in the wind. A deputy finally waved the traffic off the highway and up through the old town, past a stone obelisk carved with German names and the words *Treue der Union*, and by the time the family climbed the church steps the whole county was inside, shoulder to shoulder, with men standing two deep along the side walls. Dixie Beck from the booster club took one look at us, said "y'all come here, baby," and moved her own husband and both her boys out of the back pew so a family she had never laid eyes on could have their seats.

Gloria sat on the aisle in her good black coat, her purse on her knees and both hands folded on the clasp. Our daughter, Ruthie, sat beside her with a two-year-old boy asleep on her shoulder, his cheek in her collar and one little shoe lost somewhere on the floor, and Ruthie's husband, Daniel, kept an arm along the pew behind them both. That boy is fifteen now and taller than his grandfather. He will tell you he does not remember that day, which is true, and that he was there, which is also true.

Up front, alone in the fourth pew on the left, the Vellmer pew since before the First World War, sat Kate Hartmann, seventy-one years old, a widow eight years, the girl Miss Ruth raised from the age of eight. Kate had telephoned our house on Christmas morning before daylight. Gloria answered. The sound Gloria made into that telephone is still in the walls of our kitchen.

Two families sat at opposite ends of St. Paul that morning, and a man waiting on a casket has time to count both of them.

Fritz and Katharina Vellmer raised six children on the homeplace on the Pedernales below Martinsburg, and Ruth, born in 1924, was the baby. Kate was her brother Walter's girl. By that December every Vellmer of the six lay in the ground except Ruth, and Ruth lay in the pine box. Kate was the last Vellmer in the Vellmer pew.

Our end of the church ran back to Joseph and Hattie Mayfield, who worked the Harwell place up the river from the Vellmers in the thirties. Joseph and Hattie had two boys. Sam was the older, and Sam's part of this story waits its turn. Eli, the younger, raised me in San Antonio. Gloria and I named our girl Ruthie, for the white lady who was good to Grandma Hattie, and the boy asleep on her shoulder was Marcus.

The doors opened behind us. The cold came down the aisle first, and Miss Ruth came behind it on the shoulders of her boys.

Twelve boys carried the casket. The box was plain pine, because Miss Ruth would sooner have built her own coffin with a handsaw than pay for brass, and old Hoffmann at the funeral home had known her too long to argue with the instructions she had left him. A pine box has six handles. Twelve boys meant two to a handle, shoulder jammed against shoulder, and the boys on the outside kept a free palm flat on the lid as if she might slide off and they meant to catch her.

From the back pew a man sees the casket first. The pine went by close enough to touch. The boys wore green-and-gold letter jackets over white shirts, with ties some mother had knotted for them in the church parking lot, and a few of them had outgrown those jackets since September, so that their wrists stuck out past the cuffs, red and raw from the wind. Tyler Kott had the front handle on the left, and he was crying the way a seventeen-year-old boy cries when he has decided nobody will see it, jaw shut, eyes fixed on the altar, the water running down both sides of his face into his collar. Caleb Waidner walked across from him. DeShawn Hollins had the middle handle on the right side, the only Black face among the twelve, and when he passed our pew his eyes came over and found the sleeping boy on Ruthie's shoulder and went back to the altar. Behind DeShawn a sophomore kicker named Luis Garza kept losing his step and finding it again.

The dog walked at the head of the casket on the left side, where Miss Ruth had always kept her.

Honey was a yellow Labrador four and a half years old, with a white blaze on her chest and one ear that never would lie flat, and four nights earlier she had been shot. The vet in Martinsburg had shaved her right shoulder down to the pink skin, and a seam of black stitches ran across the bare place like the lacing on a football. Lyle had tried to leave her on the bus with a horse blanket. Honey had squeezed out through a half-lowered window and come limping up the church steps behind the boys. She favored the leg on every step down that aisle, her toenails ticking on the wood, her nose working at the pine as she went, and her eyes never left the box.

Lyle himself brought up the rear in his Sunday suit, which was brown and a little shiny at the knees, his hat held against his stomach with both hands. He had driven the Mustangs to that funeral in Bus No. 7, the same 1999 Blue Bird that had carried them to every game that season, and the bus sat at the curb with its hot engine pinging in the cold. The heater on that bus only ever worked on the driver's side. Every boy had stepped off it with red ears.

The town filled the church, then the steps, then the sidewalk clear to the bus, coat collars turned up to their hat brims. Dixie Beck had worked the doors with a box of tissues, telling everybody "here you go, baby," grown men included. Coach Ray Winslow stood along the side wall with his hands jammed in his coat pockets, and for once in his life no sunflower seeds cracked in his jaw. Teddy Rausch, who taped the boys' ankles, had his cigarette behind his ear, the one he never lit, and nobody had the heart to tell him he had worn it into church.

An old Japanese man nobody in our family knew yet stood against the back wall beside our pew with his hat over his heart, and he never once took a seat. A Martinsburg lawyer three rows ahead of us kept his eyes on the floor through every hymn. Out front, a young sheriff's deputy named Kyle Brandt waited across the street beside his cruiser, bareheaded in the wind. He had been the first deputy through her gate on Christmas Eve, and he would carry what he found there a long time.

People said the things people always say at a funeral. They said she was eighty-nine, and that she had lived a good long life, as if somebody had checked the figures. They said they would miss seeing her on that bus with that dog, and on her porch on Elm Street with the nativity scene lit, and in a booth at the Mustang Grill on Main arguing with the pastor over pie. The old ranchers said she had carried their mail six days a week for thirty-nine years and never once put a letter in the wrong box. They said she never married and never had children of her own, then looked at Kate in the fourth pew and corrected themselves. They said she was sweet.

Miss Ruth Vellmer was a great many things in her eighty-nine years, and sweet was the least of them by a country mile.

The people in that church, every last one of them, believed they knew Miss Ruth. The Lindauer and Grieshaber families in the pews behind hers had sat at her back since before she could read a hymnal. The twelve boys had ridden a bus with her from August to November. The man in the back pew believed he knew more than any of them, and in one small way he was right. He knew a Mayfield family story about a schoolyard in the spring of 1937 and a white girl's broken nose. He knew that a money order had come to his Grandma Hattie in San Antonio every Christmas until the year she died, the envelope addressed in a tall slanted hand. He knew a navy-blue hat in the back row of a college graduation in 1968, and a woman who left before the reception.

He did not know the rest. Eighteen days later, in a lawyer's office on West Main in Martinsburg, he would be handed one sealed envelope and one small chain, and it would take him twelve years to say out loud what he found inside it.

The boys set the casket on its stand below the altar, and Tyler Kott said "easy, easy" under his breath, and Honey lay down beside it on the cold floor with her chin on her paws.

Those twelve boys thought they had carried an old lady from their bus. Not one of them knew who had ridden on their shoulders that morning.

On that day, I did not know her either.
