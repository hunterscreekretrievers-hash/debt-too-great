# 24. Jezebel's Paper

*Martinsburg and San Antonio, Texas, October 2012 through June 2013*

Clayton Rusk found her on the Elm Street porch swing on a Thursday afternoon in October 2012, a big, easy, silver-haired man with capped white teeth, in pressed jeans and ostrich boots that had never been near a cow.

"Miss Vellmer? Clay Rusk, Rusk Hill Country Properties, out of Austin. Could I have ten minutes?"

She gave him ten minutes. Honey lay across the top step in the afternoon sun and watched the ostrich boots come up the walk, her chin on her paws and the one ear that never lay flat standing up like a question. Rusk stepped over her and laid watercolor renderings along the porch rail. In the paintings the pecan bottom had become a clipped lawn, with a white pavilion strung in lights. The stone house was the *Vellmer Haus Bridal Suite*.

Honey got up before Rusk had squared the last sheet. The dog looked him over once, head to boots, the way she looked over every man who came up those steps. Then she turned her back on him, nosed the screen door open and walked into the house, and the door slapped shut on its spring behind her.

Ruth knew that grade.

Rusk talked about brides from Dallas who would pay forty thousand dollars a weekend. Then he took a cashier's check out of his shirt pocket and held it out between two fingers, and the number on it was larger than any sum Fritz Vellmer had seen in his life.

Ruth did not take the check. She picked up her truck keys.

"Follow me," she said.

When Ruth came down the steps, the screen door banged open behind her, and Honey came out of the house at a trot, passed the man from Austin as if he were a hat rack and climbed ahead of Ruth into the cab of the green Ford, the 1951 pickup she had kept running for fifty-seven years.

Rusk followed four miles out the river road, eating the Ford's caliche dust, and Ruth walked him to the foundation by the kitchen door. Honey stayed in the cab with her chin on the window glass and watched him the whole time. At knee height, in the bottom course, a block of grey limestone was cut with a mason's chisel in square, plain letters:

**F. V. 1908**

"My father quarried every stone in this house out of that hill across the river," Ruth said. "He cut them in this yard and laid them with his own hands at twenty-eight years old. Men from Austin stood him on that porch and called him a Hun in 1918, and he kept on laying stone." She put her palm flat on the cornerstone. "This is his house. I'm keeping it."

"Miss Ruth, with respect, at your age . . ."

"At my age," Ruth said, "I've heard better offers from worse men. Good afternoon, Mr. Rusk."

Rusk propped his card against the porch post, with ATTORNEY AT LAW in small capitals under the company name, and walked to his Cadillac. He sat a good while with the door open and the phone against his ear, looking up at the stone house, and once he laughed.

---

Rusk came again on the fourteenth of December, a cold grey Friday with a north wind blowing the hay out of her father's carved manger.

"Miss Ruth. Good to see you again."

She knew his face. She knew the teeth. The name was gone, as clean as if somebody had reached into her head and lifted it out with two fingers.

"You're . . ." she said. "You're the Austin man."

"Clay Rusk." His smile did not change at all. "We talked in October. About the property."

"Yes. I told you."

"You told me plainly. I only wanted to know whether you'd had further thoughts."

She opened her mouth to answer, and the sentence went out from under her in the middle. She stood with her hand on the post beside the wooden Joseph her father had carved in 1919 and could not find the end of it. The word she wanted was in German, and then it was not there in German either.

Honey came out from under the swing without a word from anybody. The dog set her shoulder against Ruth's left knee, planted all four feet on the porch boards and went stiff as a sawhorse, the way she braced for the low chair in the front room. Ruth let go of the post and put her hand flat on the warm yellow back, and she kept it there until the porch quit tipping her toward the rail. Honey never once looked at the man from Austin.

"I told you," Ruth said again.

"Of course," Rusk said gently. "Of course you told me. I won't keep you standing in this cold."

Rusk sat at the curb with the engine off and the phone against his ear, looking up at her through the windshield, and he talked a long time.

Ruth went inside and sat at the kitchen table in her coat, and Honey lay down across her feet. On the counter by the sink sat the pill box with its fourteen little doors, Friday evening still full.

She sat there until the window over the sink went dark.

---

The constable came on a Tuesday in the middle of March 2013, two years almost to the week after a dog man from Houston had caught Ruth falling off Eric Rogers's steps. She was eighty-nine that winter. Honey was four, and the pill box on the kitchen counter had grown so crowded that its little plastic doors no longer shut flat.

The man on the porch wore a tan uniform shirt and took off his straw hat when she opened the screen. His grandmother had worn one navy hat with cloth violets, two rows ahead of Ruth at St. Paul, every Easter for thirty years.

"Miss Ruth," he said. "Ma'am. I hate this."

"Then don't do it, Kevin."

"Got no choice." He held out a fold of papers stapled at the corner. "I'm supposed to put these in your hand and tell you you've been served. I didn't write a word of them."

She took them. He drove off slow, the way people pass a house with a funeral wreath on the door.

Ruth sat down on the porch swing to read, with the live oak ticking last year's leaves onto the porch roof, brown and curled as burnt paper.

The top page said CITATION. The second said IN THE MATTER OF THE GUARDIANSHIP OF RUTH MARIE VELLMER, AN ALLEGED INCAPACITATED PERSON. The applicant was a woman named Margo L. Strand, Certified Guardian, of Hill Country Guardianship Services, with an address on Bee Cave Road in Austin. The proposed ward, it said, suffered from progressive cognitive decline and owned real property of substantial value requiring protection.

At the bottom of the last page, under a signature with a looping C, the attorney for the applicant had typed his name.

Clayton J. Rusk.

Clayton Rusk had sat on this same swing in October. He came back in January and left the check on the swing. She mailed it back to Austin with one stamp too many, because she could not find her reading glasses.

Honey put her chin on Ruth's knee and looked at the papers the way she looked at a snake in the woodpile.

Ruth put on her coat and walked to Main Street.

---

Eric Rogers read the application standing, then sat down and read it again.

"In Texas just about anybody can file one of these," he said. "Before a judge can put a guardian over you, the other side has to hand him a certificate from a doctor who has examined you. They haven't filed one yet." He tapped one finger on the line about real property of substantial value. "A guardian can ask the court for permission to sell a ward's land to pay for the ward's care."

Under the desk Honey shifted her weight and laid her chin across the toe of Ruth's left shoe.

"If they put me in a home," Ruth said, "what becomes of her?"

Eric looked down at the dog before he answered, and he gave Ruth the truth the way she liked it, plain and all at once. "A guardian can sign her over to the county shelter the same afternoon, Miss Vellmer, the same as a couch or a car. No nursing home in this county takes a dog. In all these hills there's no place that keeps an old person's dog for the rest of its life once the old person is gone."

Ruth had driven the mail past the county shelter out by the sale barn every working day of her last thirty years on the route: the chain-link runs, the wet concrete, the hose coiled on its hook, and the dogs hurling themselves at the wire every time a car slowed. She reached down and found the white blaze with her fingertips. Honey's tail moved once on the floorboards, slow, the way a dog answers her name in her sleep.

"Then somebody ought to build one out here," Ruth said. "For the old people with no Kate."

She had not yet told Kate one word about the papers.

Ruth turned the application back to the line Eric's finger had marked, *real property of substantial value*, and read it three times.

"That man wants to put up a white arch where my mother hung the wash."

"I expect he has the arch on order already." Eric leaned across the desk. "Don't sign anything anybody brings to your door, and don't let a stranger in the house, no matter how sweet her smile."

---

By the middle of April the pill box held ten kinds of pills, and Ruth could name four of them.

She laid them out one Sunday night on a dish towel, as she had once set out instruments at the Naval Hospital in Honolulu. The little white one was for her blood pressure, the pink one for cholesterol, the tan capsule for her stomach, the white oval for her heart. The rest had come over the past four years from the new doctor, each one for a trouble the one before had started, the way a man in a hole keeps digging because the shovel is the only tool in his hand.

Every bottle came with a sheet in type so small she needed the magnifying glass. *May cause dizziness. May cause confusion. Use caution in elderly patients.* She kept the sheets in a shoebox on top of the refrigerator, and the shoebox was full.

---

The San Antonio medical group that had bought old Dr. Steinhagen's practice sent its letter the next week under a blue swoosh, inviting her to an Annual Wellness Visit with a screening for memory. She went at nine on Tuesday, the twenty-third of April.

In a room cold enough to keep milk, a nurse handed her a sheet of paper with a circle printed on it.

"Put the numbers on the clock for me, Miss Ruth. Then draw the hands at ten minutes past eleven."

Ruth put the twelve at the top. She put the one and the two and the three, and the numbers marched down the right side, and by the nine she had no room left, and the ten and the eleven crowded into the corner under the twelve like cattle bunched at a gate. She drew one hand to the eleven and one hand to the ten.

Dr. Neil Prescott, up from San Antonio for his Tuesday clinic, came in at nine-forty pushing a laptop on a rolling stand, a young man with very clean hands. He said good morning to the computer and began to type.

"Any trouble with your memory, Miss Vellmer?"

"Since about the time you started giving me pills."

Click. Click. "So your Mini-Cog score is a little low today. That's common at your age. I'm going to start you on something called donepezil, for memory."

"I'd like to come off some of them," Ruth said. "Not go on more."

"We can talk about that next time." He rolled the stand to the door, and there he looked at her for the first time, smiled, said, "You take care now," and was gone, and the computer went with him.

By the clock over the sink, he had stayed eleven minutes.

That night Ruth knelt by the bed on Elm Street and began the prayer. *Vater unser, der du bist im Himmel.* The next line was gone. For sixty-nine years the prayer had stopped at the same place, *Dein Wille geschehe*, because Ruth stopped it there, and tonight the words slipped away two lines early, the way the kitchen floor slid out from under her in the mornings. Ruth tried the English and got as far as heaven. One verse came whole, from a psalm she had never once liked, the eighty-eighth. "*Shall thy wonders be known in the dark? and thy righteousness in the land of forgetfulness?*" Ruth said the verse twice into the quilt, because the land of forgetfulness was where she lived now, and she wanted Him to have the address. Honey came and leaned her shoulder into Ruth's back until Ruth got up off her knees.

The donepezil gave her dreams so vivid she woke with the smell of them in her nose: coal smoke, wet wool, lavender crushed under boots, the inside of a cattle car. She told nobody.

Honey knew anyway.

On a night at the end of April Ruth stood again on the fifth floor of 84 Avenue Foch, under the mansard roof, with her nose two inches from the plaster and her arms straight out in front of her, and behind her a length of rubber hose ticked against Strelow's boot. Her arms were sinking toward the floor. She could not hold them. She could not make a sound.

Something heavy came up onto the bed.

Honey had never once been allowed on that bed. She climbed onto it now, all sixty-two pounds, walked up the quilt and lay down full length across Ruth's chest and stomach, heavy and warm as a sack of grain, and pinned her to the mattress.

Ruth broke out of that room the way a swimmer breaks the top of a stock tank, gasping, and the plaster was gone, and the dark was her own bedroom on Elm Street, smelling of cedar and dog. Her hand found the fur before her eyes found anything. Under her palm the dog's heart thumped slow and even, and Honey did not move until Ruth's own heart came down to match it.

In the morning a better psalm came to her, *I laid me down and slept; I awaked; for the LORD sustained me,* and Ruth said nothing to the dog about the bed. Honey slept on it every night after that.

---

On a Thursday in May the asphalt shimmered like a skillet left on the fire.

Ruth drove the Buick to the Dollar General for dish soap and dog biscuits. Forty cars swam in the heat like catfish in a muddy pond, and every one looked like hers. She pressed the button on her keys, and somewhere a horn chirped, and she could not tell which way.

Honey took the leash in her teeth.

The dog had never done that in her life. She turned and pulled, straight across two rows of trucks, until Ruth's hand lay on the hot roof of the Buick.

Across the lot a man in a tan sport coat sat in a white Silverado with the engine running. Dale Kunkel sold insurance on the square, sat across the aisle from her at St. Paul, and had worried about her since Easter. He lifted two fingers off his steering wheel. She lifted two in return.

A young woman from Rusk's Austin office telephoned Dale that week and told him his answers would help Miss Vellmer get the care she needed. Dale answered every question on her list. Three weeks later his account came back to Ruth typed, stapled and under a notary's seal.

*On or about May 16, 2013, at approximately 2:00 p.m., I observed Ruth Vellmer in the parking lot of the Dollar General store in Martinsburg, Texas. Miss Vellmer appeared confused and disoriented and was wandering between vehicles in the afternoon sun with no apparent awareness of the location of her automobile. Miss Vellmer then operated a motor vehicle.*

Every word was true. Nothing in it said the dog had found the car.

---

The stack held four more sworn statements. Harlan Moellering, who owned the two hundred acres on the east fence, swore that one night in March Miss Vellmer had telephoned him at eleven o'clock to ask whether a flier had come down in his pasture, and whether he would hide the boy in his barn before the soldiers came along the road. The statement a young man from Rusk's office typed for him left out that she had told Harlan she was sorry and had only been dreaming. The same typed page left out the option Clayton Rusk had bought on those two hundred acres in January, the month Harlan's wife went into the nursing home in Kerrville and the first bills came due. Eric Rogers found that option in the county clerk's records in June.

Inge Lochte and Doris Ahrens of the St. Paul quilting circle swore together that Miss Vellmer had twice in one afternoon called Doris by her dead sister's name. Over kolaches in Doris's front room in April, Margo Strand had told the two women, in a voice like warm milk, that their statements would get Ruth the care she needed and that talking with Ruth about the case would only upset her. So in May Inge crossed Main Street in front of the bank rather than pass Ruth on the sidewalk, and went home and cried.

Henry Vellmer's daughter, Joyce Lange, needed no kolaches. She telephoned Rusk's office on her own and signed a typed page that said her aunt "has a history of agitated outbursts at church, going back to 1979."

Margo L. Strand, who had never met Ruth Vellmer, swore to the rest. The medical evidence came last, filed with an amended application on the twentieth of May. **[VERIFY: 2013 Texas Probate Code rule on the physician's certificate in guardianship (the Estates Code guardianship title took effect Jan. 1, 2014), including the requirement that the exam fall within 120 days of filing]**

PHYSICIAN'S CERTIFICATE OF MEDICAL EXAMINATION. Dr. Neil Prescott had signed it on the sixth of May, thirteen days after an eleven-minute visit he had spent facing a computer. *Diagnosis: Dementia, probable Alzheimer's type. Is the proposed ward totally without capacity to care for herself and to manage her property? YES.* On page three someone in his office had typed all eleven medications in a column.

Eric Rogers spread all of it across her kitchen table under the hanging lamp on a Thursday evening in June.

"Ms. Strand has forty-one wards in four counties," he said. "Thirty-three of them see doctors in that same medical group." He squared the corners of the stack. "That's how the machine works. Nobody in this whole stack has to tell a lie. Everybody just has to leave out one true thing."

Ruth looked out the window at the crepe myrtle. A picture rose up out of the German Bible on her mother's table: a queen at a writing desk with her husband's ring on her finger, a seal pressed into hot wax, letters carried out the door to the elders of a town, and two men of Belial sitting down in the front row to swear against a man who would not sell his father's vineyard.

"They don't throw stones anymore," Eric said. "They have notaries."

Ruth took a yellow tablet out of the drawer under the telephone and uncapped her pen. "Doris Ahrens has quilted beside me at St. Paul for fifty years," she said. "I mean to tell her what I think of her kolaches, in ink."

Eric laid his hand on the tablet. "The time may come when we need to set the story straight and try our case, Miss Vellmer," he said, "but the only audience that counts will be the judge, not Ms. Strand and her counsel."

Ruth looked at his hand a long moment. Then she capped the pen and laid it across the tablet, square with the edge, as she had once laid a pencil across her sorting case at the end of a route.

"Who pays Ms. Strand, if she wins?"

"Your own money pays her," Eric said. "The statute calls five percent of everything that comes into a ward's estate, and five percent of everything that goes out, a reasonable fee, and the ward's own account covers it. Three years ago government auditors counted hundreds of complaints against guardians in forty-five states, went through twenty closed cases and found guardians had taken five point four million dollars from a hundred and fifty-eight wards."

"So the woman who takes my land sends me the bill for taking it," Ruth said. "That's a business, Eric, and old women are the stock on the shelf. One honest lawyer sits at my table. I have met the rest."

---

This part of the story needs no diary. Kate called me at a quarter to six on a Wednesday evening in June, and her voice sounded like a woman hollering down a well.

The court had appointed a young Austin lawyer named Whitney Hale to represent Miss Ruth, and Ms. Hale came out on one of Miss Ruth's worst afternoons with Dr. Prescott's certificate already in her briefcase, asked the year and the name of the president, and told the court that a guardian of the estate would serve the proposed ward's best interest. Then she telephoned the next of kin, and so Kate Vellmer Hartmann learned that somebody wanted Mama Ruth's land, and that Mama Ruth had known since March.

"Isaiah," Kate said, "you have to drive me. My hands won't hold a steering wheel."

Kate had run the night shift in the emergency room at the Robert B. Green for twenty years, and a Mayfield had driven the city ambulance that backed up to her doors. Gloria packed ham sandwiches and pushed me out the kitchen door.

On Highway 87, somewhere past Comfort, with the June sun going down red as a stove lid, Kate started counting on her fingers the pills she remembered from Christmas, and she ran out of fingers.

"Forty-nine years a nurse," she said, "and I never once opened that box."

We came up Elm Street a little after nine with the porch light burning and Miss Ruth on the swing in a housecoat. Kate went up those steps at a dead run, seventy-one years old, her purse banging her hip, and a yellow dog met her at the top step, stood square in front of her and would not move.

Kate dropped to one knee on the porch boards and put out the back of her hand, the way a horsewoman offers it to a strange horse. The dog smelled the hand slowly, knuckle by knuckle. Then she leaned into Kate's shoulder, and Kate put her face in the yellow fur and stayed there.

"Mama Ruth," she said into the dog. "Why didn't you call me?"

"Because you'd do this."

In the kitchen the shoebox came down off the refrigerator with a scrape, and then came Kate's voice, counting under her breath the way she counted a pulse.

"Eleven," Kate said. "Eleven, Mama. You were on seven at Christmas. This one says 'complex sleep-related behaviors,' and it names sleep-driving. Sleep-driving, Mama." Her voice broke and came back harder. "They gave you a pill for your memory after the other ten took it."

Miss Ruth looked past her at me on the walk with my hat in my hand, and the porch light caught her grey eyes.

"Isaiah Mayfield," she said. "You've got your Grandmother Hattie's forehead."

Later the two of them sat on the swing with the crickets going and the dog lying across both their feet at once.

"They're going to take Grandpa Fritz's house," Kate said. She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "Isaiah says we fight it. Mama, I don't know how to fight a piece of paper."

Miss Ruth put her long hand on Kate's knee.

"I know how to fight paper," she said.

She never said where she had learned it. Neither one of us on that porch thought to ask, and a question would have bought us only a lie.

I slept on the front-room couch that night, and I slept straight through what came next. Kate told me the rest at breakfast.

---

Ruth woke on the back step a little after two.

She stood in her nightgown at the edge of three limestone steps with the yard black below her and no memory of getting out of bed. Something warm and solid pressed hard against both her shins.

Honey stood across the top step like a gate. All four feet were planted, her body set like a corner brace, her shoulder jammed against Ruth's shins so the next step would have been into dog instead of air. The dog trembled the way a pointer trembles on a covey, and she did not move an inch.

"Brace," Ruth whispered, though the dog was already braced, and she put both hands on Honey's back and stood there until her heart slowed.

Kate stood in the kitchen doorway in her robe.

"I heard the screen," Kate said, very small. "You were going down those steps."

"Honey wasn't going anywhere."

Kate looked at the dog and said nothing more. Then she went back into the kitchen, opened the yellow bedtime door on every day of the pill box, took out all seven sleeping pills, and dropped them in the trash under the sink.

---

At breakfast Kate went hunting for a pen in the drawer under the telephone and came up with a letter from Houston, postmarked the end of January.

"Who's J.?" Kate said.

"Honey's man," Ruth said. "He sat on that swing in January, looked at my pill bottles on the windowsill and told me this to my face. I told him no."

Kate unfolded the page. The letter told about an old Japanese man out by Comfort who read eyes and mixed herbs, and who had brought sick people back to their feet after the doctors gave up on them. Near the bottom, in letters two inches tall, he had printed a name and a place:

*KENJI MORITA. COMFORT.*

He had signed it as he signed every letter: *In God's Grip of Grace, J.*

Ruth looked at the name. On the morning of December 7, 1941, a Japanese pilot had flown low over the hospital lawn at Pearl Harbor, close enough for her to see the leather of his helmet. She thought about that face, and then she looked at the eleven brown bottles Kate had lined up along the windowsill, and she weighed the one against the other, the way her father weighed a stone in both hands before he set it in a wall.

"Comfort," she said. "That's not far."
