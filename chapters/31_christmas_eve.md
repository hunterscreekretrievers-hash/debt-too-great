# 31. Christmas Eve

*Tuesday, December 24, 2013. Elm Street, Martinsburg.*

The late service at St. Paul let out at a quarter past eleven, and the people of Martinsburg came down the limestone steps into the cold with their candles pinched out and still smoking, calling *Frohe Weihnachten* and Merry Christmas across the parking lot. Ruth walked the two blocks home with Honey on her left and a stub of white candle in her coat pocket, still warm.

The night was clear and thirty-one degrees, with stars thick as spilled sugar over the water tower. Every porch on Elm Street had lights. Hers was the only one with a stable.

She had sung "Stille Nacht" in German with the old people in the back rows while the young ones sang it in English up front, the two languages running together under the candles like two creeks meeting below a bluff. At the door Pastor Albrecht had held her hand a second past the handshake. "You'll call me if you see that truck again."

"I'll call Dixie," Ruth had told him. "Dixie's faster."

At the gate she stopped and looked at the porch. The bulbs burned red and green and blue and orange on Fritz's cedar stable. Joseph stood with the glued seam in his neck, and Mary knelt at the back with her long face. In the manger the pale new Child lay with His fists up beside His face, the raw pecan white as bone under the colored lights.

Ruth did not go to bed. She went inside only long enough to take the afghan off the back of the sofa and the Colt out of the nightstand drawer. Then she came back out, sat down on the swing in her church coat, slipped off her shoes and spread the afghan over her knees with the pistol under it, her right hand resting on the grip the way another woman's hand rests on her knitting. Her Christmas cards had gone out in Monday's mail, the last of them to a dog man in Houston. Kate would drive up from San Antonio in the morning, the turkey was thawing in the sink, and the whole house smelled of yeast. Honey lay down on the top step with her chin on her paws.

In her coat pocket Ruth's left hand found the old Child's whole carved fist, the one piece the tire had missed, smooth as a creek pebble. She turned it over with her thumb and looked up at the stars.

Seventy Christmases before, she had stood fifty-two minutes under the departure board in Union Station in Washington with a boy from the Harwell place in an Army overcoat, the two of them barred by the custom of the capital and the law of half the country from sitting down together in the station restaurant, and she had thought the world could not be any colder. A year later, on a plank bunk in Barracks 28, she heard Polish women singing carols through chattering teeth and found out she had been wrong about the cold.

Her left thumb found the bare third finger of the same hand and rubbed it, back and forth, as she had rubbed it for sixty-nine years.

At eleven thirty-four a truck turned onto Elm Street from Third without its headlights.

The telephone hung on the kitchen wall twenty feet behind her, and she did not get up for it. Ruth had sat on this swing for that truck.

Honey heard it first. The dog's head came up off her paws. Her odd ear went straight, and a low sound started in her chest, the same sound Ruth had first heard from her in a stadium parking lot in Kerrville, when a man put his hand on his own son's arm hard enough to whiten the knuckles.

The white Dodge rolled to the curb in front of the house and stopped. The engine kept running. Under the streetlight Ruth could see the cracked tailgate and the one dark taillight, and three heads in the cab.

The passenger door opened, and the dome light flooded the cab. Tarek Haddad stepped out in his pressed black jacket. Karim Nasser climbed out behind him with a tire iron from the shop hanging from one fist like a man carrying a fish by the gills. Samir Darwish stayed behind the wheel with both hands on it. In the dome light Ruth saw him push his glasses up his nose with one forefinger, slowly, and look straight ahead through the windshield at nothing.

Tarek came through the gate. He did not hurry. He walked up the short brick path the way a man walks into his own store. He carried a pistol down along his right leg, a black semiautomatic, and a can of spray paint in his left hand. Karim Nasser came through the gate behind him and stopped on the bricks.

Ruth did not rise from the swing.

"Get off my walk," she said.

Tarek stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up at her on the swing and at the stable behind her. He shook the paint can. The little ball inside rattled.

"You wrote my plate on your hand, grandmother," he said. "So the police called my boss about his truck. My boss called me into his office. He says one more police call, and I'm finished at his shop." He smiled. "I came to say Merry Christmas."

"Samir," Ruth called past him, toward the truck. "Take them home. Drive away right now."

Samir Darwish did not turn his head.

Honey had come up off the step onto all four feet, the growl running in her like a motor. Ruth reached down with her left hand and took her by the collar. "Stay," she said, low, and the dog stayed, trembling under her hand like a fence wire in a wind.

Tarek came up the three steps onto the porch. He walked past the swing close enough for Ruth to smell his cologne and the cigarette under it, went to the stable and bent over the manger. He lifted out the pale new Child, turned it under the colored bulbs the way a man turns a coin he has found on a sidewalk, and put it in his jacket pocket. Then he raised the paint can toward Mary's long face.

Ruth let go of the collar.

Honey took him.

She crossed the porch without a sound, sixty-two pounds of yellow dog, and hit him square in the chest. The paint can flew over the rail into the lantana. Tarek went backward off the top step with the dog on top of him, and the two of them came down the steps onto the brick path, her teeth sunk in the sleeve of the black jacket, and he shouted something in Arabic, high and shocked. He hit her across the head with the pistol. She did not let go of the sleeve.

Tarek turned his face up to the dog's and spat in it. "*Kalb najis*," he said through his teeth, the Arabic for *unclean dog*.

He got the pistol around under her and fired.

The shot cracked off every housefront on Elm Street. Honey yelped once, let go of his sleeve and spun off him on three legs, her right shoulder dark and wet, and fell against the bottom step. She tried to rise. Her front leg folded under her. She tried again.

Tarek got to his knees on the bricks. His sleeve hung torn open from wrist to elbow, and blood ran down over the gold watch. He looked at the blood with surprise, as if no one had ever told him he could bleed.

Ruth had not moved from the swing. She was eighty-nine years old, sixteen days short of ninety, in her stocking feet and her church coat with a candle stub in the pocket and her mother's afghan across her knees. Below her, at the foot of the steps, Honey was trying to stand and making a high thin noise through her teeth.

"Look at me," Ruth said to Tarek Haddad.

He looked up at her.

"I have seen your face on better-dressed men," she said.

Karim Nasser came past him at a run. He took the steps two at a time with the tire iron raised over his shoulder, and his chipped tooth showed in the porch light.

Ruth's hand came out from under the afghan with the Colt in it, and she fired once.

The sound was small, smaller than the shot that had hit the dog. Karim stopped on the top step as though he had walked into a wire stretched across it. The tire iron rang on the boards. He went over backward down the steps, past the dog, and lay on his back on the bricks with his arms flung wide, and he did not move again.

Tarek fired three times from the walk.

The first bullet hit her in the chest, under the left collarbone, and pushed her back into the swing so hard the chains creaked. The second hit her lower, in the stomach. The third went past her shoulder into the clapboards. She put her left hand flat against her coat and looked at it and saw the blood come up between her fingers, black under the colored lights.

Tarek came up the steps to finish her.

He came slowly, with the pistol out in front of him. The afghan had slid off her knees onto the porch boards. Ruth raised the Colt the last few inches off her lap with both hands, as the range sergeant in the West End had taught a girl from Texas in the spring of 1944, one round, center of the paper man, and fired once more.

Tarek Haddad sat down on the second step. He looked surprised again. Then he folded over sideways and slid until he lay across the bottom two steps with his face toward the street, and the black pistol dropped out of his hand onto the bricks.

At the curb the engine of the white Dodge roared. Samir Darwish floored it with the passenger door still hanging open, and the truck went up Elm Street with no lights, the door slamming itself shut on the turn at Third, and was gone.

Then Elm Street went so quiet that Ruth could hear the hot bulbs tick on the stable.

Across the street a light came on in Emil Klier's bedroom window. Emil's dog started barking. Another dog answered it down the block, and another past that, until half the dogs on Elm Street were barking at once.

Ruth laid the Colt on the swing beside her and got to her feet. Standing took her two tries. She made it as far as the head of the steps, where the dog was dragging herself up toward her, and there her legs gave way, and she sat down, and then lay down slowly on her side across the top step with her head on the porch boards, under the string of lights. The bulbs were warm near her cheek. She could smell the hay from the homeplace barn. She could smell the dog, and the gunpowder, and the yeast from the kitchen. Her breath came short and wet, and she knew the sound of it, because she had heard that sound on a burn ward at Pearl Harbor in December of 1941 and on a plank bunk in Ravensbrück in the winter of 1945, and she knew how long a body had once it began to make that sound.

Honey crawled up the last step on her one good front leg and laid her head on Ruth's shoulder.

Ruth got her hand into the dog's fur. The fur was wet and warm. Under her wrist she could feel Honey's heart going fast and hard, like a fist knocking on a door.

Above her, at the back of the stable, Mary still knelt on the porch boards, untouched, her long face lit red and then green by the bulb beside her. The manger in front of Mary was empty again.

Ruth looked at the empty manger.

Her lips moved. Emil Klier, who reached her four minutes later in his pajamas and a barn coat with a cordless telephone in his hand, said afterward that he thought she was praying. He could not make out the words. He said they might have been German. He said she sounded angry at somebody, and the somebody was not him. The words came hard and fast, like a woman talking to somebody who has kept her waiting too long.

Then her voice went quiet, and Emil, who had grown up on that prayer in German, knew it by the sound. Emil said afterward that she began it the old way, *der du bist im Himmel*, the way only the old people still prayed it. He heard *Dein Wille geschehe*, and a stop, the way an old horse balks at the same gate year after year. Then Ruth Vellmer went on through the gate. *Wie im Himmel, also auch auf Erden.*

He said her left thumb was moving on her bare ring finger the whole time she talked, and then the thumb was still. Emil told one more thing, and only to his wife. Before the thumb went still, Ruth turned her head on the porch boards and looked down the brick walk to the gate, the way a woman looks when the person she has waited on half a century finally lifts the latch.

By the time the first sheriff's cruiser turned onto Elm Street with its lights going, eleven minutes after the shots, Ruth Vellmer was dead. The first deputy through the gate was Kyle Brandt. He had a flashlight in one hand and his pistol in the other, and he stopped on the brick path when his light found the man on the bricks, and the man across the steps, and the empty manger, and the old woman at the head of the steps, and the yellow dog lying across her with her teeth bared at him. Honey would not let him touch her. She would not let anybody touch the old woman until Emil got a hand on her collar and talked to her, and talked to her, and at last she put her head down on Ruth's arm.

On the swing lay a small old pistol with a worn checkered grip. Brandt found the brass by flashlight on the porch boards and counted it. Two.

Then he stood on the bricks with his light on the yellow copy of a report, tucked into the frame of Ruth's front window where she had wedged it so she could find the case number in the dark. He did not touch it, and for a long while he read it without a word.

Somewhere west of Martinsburg, on a farm road with no lights, Samir Darwish drove alone with both hands on the wheel, at ten and two, exactly at the speed limit, the way a man drives who has been pulled over once and means never to be pulled over again. Nobody in Pedernales County ever saw him again.

*Wednesday, December 25, 2013. Christmas morning.*

The sheriff telephoned the Texas Rangers at a quarter to one, the way a small Texas county calls the State when it has three dead and four deputies, and Ranger Boyd Teague drove over from Kerrville in the dark. He was fifty-four, long and spare as a fence stay, with a grey mustache trimmed square and a silver star cut from a Mexican five-peso coin over his heart. By first light he had dug one slug out of the clapboards behind the swing. Before the justice of the peace let the funeral home carry Tarek Haddad off the steps, Teague went through the dead man's pockets himself. In the pocket of the black jacket lay the pale new Child, both fists raised beside His face.

At eight he walked the house with Brandt and Kate Hartmann, a pocket notebook open in his hand.

The bedroom held a cracked King James Bible and no clock anywhere.

"Mama Ruth woke at four-thirty every morning of her life," Kate said. "No alarm. Never once."

On the closet floor, beside the door, sat a packed canvas bag: a change of clothes, a flashlight and a second grey wool coat folded square. Teague wrote *go-bag* in his notebook. With Kate's nod he cut six stitches in the coat lining. Ten twenty-dollar bills lay sewn flat against the wool. Behind them, in waxed paper gone the color of tobacco, lay a grey card printed in French. *Carte d'identité. Brenner, Lucie. Née à Mulhouse.* The card bore a 1944 prefecture stamp and the photograph of a girl of twenty with Ruth Vellmer's long jaw.

"Ma'am, who was Lucie Brenner?"

Kate held the card in both hands. "Mama Ruth never said one word to me about France."

In the back bedroom sixty-seven shoeboxes lined pine shelves, one to a year since 1947, each holding carbon copies of that year's letters. Here and there among the carbons lay an onionskin signed with a name that was not hers: *Lucie, Pedernales County.* Teague read the top one twice and could make nothing of it. In his notebook he wrote that name under the name off the French card, drew a line between the two, and put a question mark at the end of the line.

The grey Royal portable sat on the kitchen table with a sheet still in the roller, a carbon and an onionskin behind it. The page was dated December 23 and stopped two-thirds of the way down, unsigned.

> *To the Editor:*
>
> *Six nights ago somebody threw raw hamburger soaked in antifreeze over a fence on Elm Street for an old woman's dog. The dog would not touch it. The deputy laid it in the trunk of his car, and there it rides.*
>
> *Three men in this county stand named in three offices, in San Antonio, in Austin and in Blanco. Every office holds the paper. Not one office has sent a man to read it.*
>
> *I learned what paper is worth the summer I was twenty, in a country where the Germans kept the best records in Europe.*

Teague read it standing, with his hat against his chest.

Beside the telephone lay two spiral notebooks in the tall slanted hand: date, hour, plate, make, color. The first page of the first one was dated November 21, 2013, and logged the propane man's truck. Whatever had set Ruth Vellmer to writing down cars had come to her on a Thursday, one month before she died. He turned to December.

*Dec. 3. 1:50 a.m. White Dodge pickup, cracked tailgate, left taillight dark. Three men. North on Third. BH7, rest unread.*

*Dec. 10. 3:40 p.m. Exxon, U.S. 290. Same truck. BH7-4419. The one with the watch.*

*Dec. 17. 2:04 a.m. My curb. No headlights, motor running. BH7-4419.*

"She reported every one of those," Brandt said.

"She wrote the hour down too, son, before anybody had a reason to lie about the hour." Teague bagged the notebooks. "No jury will see a better witness."

On the kitchen sill stood two pill bottles, a Mason jar of red seaweed and store-brand aspirin. The vegetable bin held potatoes and onions.

"You won't find a turnip," Kate said. "She never let one in a house."

A green ledger in the drawer by the stove ran eleven lines of initials and towns, *S.M.B., Luling* and *M.Z.B., San Antonio* among them, with a column for every December since 1952. The 2013 column stood ruled and empty. Teague copied three lines into his notebook and left a blank beside each one.

Under the dish towels lay a dime-store frame with its picture turned to the wood. He turned it over: a young Black pilot grinning beside a fighter plane with a red tail. On the back of the print, in a square hand: *Ramitelli, Italy, 1944. Red tail, red-hot. S.* Kate shook her head. The Ranger laid the frame back face down, exactly as he had found it.

In a cedar box on the hall shelf lay statements from a brokerage house in San Antonio. Teague read one figure on the top sheet, put it back and closed the lid. For a while he said nothing at all.

Four kitchen chairs stood along the far side of the table with their backs to the wall, every one facing the door.

"Son," Teague said to Brandt, flat, "this is how people live who have been hunted. Saw it once before, as a young trooper in Hondo. An old man who never would name what he came through overseas. Packed bag. Chairs to the door."

At nine o'clock he put both slugs, the one from the clapboards and the one the vet, Matt Ahrens, had lifted out of the dog, into a trooper's car with the black pistol, bound for the state crime laboratory in Austin.

At five minutes before noon a plain Ford sedan with government plates came up Elm Street and parked behind the Ranger's truck. Two men in dark windbreakers climbed from the front seat. The older one carried a file folder under his arm, an inch thick already, with a red tab on its edge. He showed Teague a badge and a single typed sheet: Federal Bureau of Investigation, San Antonio Division, Joint Terrorism Task Force.

"Ranger, as of eleven-thirty this morning this is a federal matter. We'll take it from here."

Teague read the sheet twice. "Since when is a dead widow on Elm Street federal?"

"Since before she died, sir."

The younger agent carried out the canvas bag, the French card in a plastic sleeve, the spiral notebooks, the Colt and its two brass casings, sixty-seven shoeboxes, and the grey Royal with the unfinished page still in the roller. They boxed the stable and every figure in it. When the older agent asked for the slugs, Teague told him where they had gone at nine, and the agent wrote that down without a word. He never asked for the Ranger's pocket notebook, and Teague never offered it.

By one o'clock the sedan was gone. Ranger Boyd Teague stood alone in the driveway on Elm Street with his hat in his hand and a case taken off him by one sheet of paper. He stood there long enough for Kate to bring him a cup of coffee, and he thanked her and held it until it went cold, and never drank a swallow.

That inventory sat in a sheriff's drawer and a sealed federal file for thirteen years, children, before anybody laid the pieces side by side. Nobody in that kitchen could say who Lucie Brenner was, what the initials meant, why a pilot lay face down under the dish towels, or who Ruth Vellmer had been before she ever carried the mail. A carbon of the Ranger's handwritten list had gone into the sheriff's file at ten that morning, and somewhere on the road between Martinsburg and Austin a copy found its way to a newsroom. Three phrases made the trip whole: a go-bag, a fake French ID, and a notebook of license plates on Middle Eastern workers.

In his truck, before he turned the key, Teague opened his pocket notebook to the page with the two names on it, *Lucie Brenner* and *Lucie, Pedernales County*, the line between them and the question mark at the end. He looked at that page a long time. Then he buttoned the notebook into his shirt pocket, behind the silver star, and kept it there.
