# 22. The Office on Main Street

*Martinsburg, Texas, March to June 2011*

The letter from the appraisal district came on a Tuesday in the middle of March, and a little before ten in the morning Ruth stood at her kitchen counter with it in one hand, trying to decide whether she had taken her pills.

The pill box lay open on the Formica in front of her, a long plastic tray with two rows of little doors, the days of the week raised in white letters like the names on a church roll. The blue door marked T hung open, and the compartment under it held nothing. She could not say whether she had tipped those pills into her palm twenty minutes ago with her coffee or had never filled that door on Sunday night. She stood with her long fingers spread flat on the counter and waited for the answer to come the way answers used to come to her, quick and plain as a dinner bell across a pasture. The kitchen clock ticked, and no answer came.

The letter was the one the county sent every few years about the homeplace, a form asking whether the land still ran cattle, so the two hundred and forty acres could keep their agricultural valuation. Ruth had filled in that form each time it came, in blue ink, in the same tall slanted hand. This morning the boxes slid under her eye the way the floor slid some mornings, a small sideways give, as if the house sat on a raft on the Pedernales in high water. She read the first question three times. She made herself go slowly, her finger under the type, as her mother had taught her to read the German Bible at the kitchen table in 1930. By the fourth question she could not have told a soul what the first one wanted from her.

She tucked the form into a manila envelope, put on her coat, and walked to Main Street.

---

Eric Rogers had moved his practice off the square that winter into an old craftsman bungalow on West Main, a few blocks past the courthouse. The gable was rust red over grey-blue clapboard, trimmed in green, with a front porch on square columns and a flagstone walk running up from the sidewalk between clay pots and a bed of agave. An iron lamppost painted the same red as the gable stood at the foot of the walk. The gold letters on the front window had not seen a summer yet: ERIC ROGERS, ATTORNEY AT LAW. The rooms inside still smelled of fresh paint. Ruth's will sat in a folder on a shelf behind his desk. She had signed it at the old office on the square, in the same chair where she sat now, because Eric had brought the chairs with him.

Eric read the form standing, then sat down and went through it line by line.

"Miss Vellmer, you've filled this out yourself every time the county sent it."

"Every time."

"What happened this time?"

"The boxes moved." She laid her hand flat on his desk. "The boxes stayed put, Eric. I moved."

He turned the form around and filled in the answers himself while she gave them to him, the lessee, the cows and the two bulls, the hay meadow by the river, and then he turned it back for her signature. She signed *Ruth M. Vellmer* in the tall slanted hand, and the hand did not shake.

"Anybody been out to the homeplace asking about that land?" he said.

"Nobody asks an old woman about anything, Eric."

"You'd call me if anybody came out asking questions."

"I'd call you."

She stood. The room tipped, a slow lean to the left, the bookcase and the window and the gold letters on the glass all sliding the same direction together like freight in the hold of a ship in a cross sea. She held the back of the chair until the floor came level.

"Miss Vellmer?"

"I took my pills," she said, and found she did not know whether that was true.

---

The porch had three limestone steps, relaid new when Eric fixed up the house, square-cut and sharp along the nose, with no wear in them yet to show a foot the middle of the tread. Ruth got the first one right. On the second her right foot came down on the edge instead of the middle, her ankle turned, and the flagstone walk came up toward her face.

A hand took her elbow, a big hand, hard in the palm, and another went flat between her shoulder blades, and the walk stopped coming.

"Whoa, now," a man said. "Got you, ma'am."

He was a big man in a feed-store cap and a canvas chore coat with yellow dog hair worked thick into every seam. He smelled of cedar shavings and coffee and the inside of a kennel, which is a clean smell when the man keeping the kennel knows his business. He set her down on the iron bench beside the door the way a man sets down a carton of eggs.

"I'm fine," Ruth said.

"Yes ma'am. Sit a minute anyway."

A yellow dog sat on the flagstones at his left heel, where one word from him had dropped her when he reached for Ruth. She wore a red vest with white letters stitched across the side: SERVICE DOG IN TRAINING. One ear stood up at the fold and would not lie flat. The dog did not bark and did not break her sit. She watched Ruth with brown eyes, steady as the bubble in a carpenter's level.

Eric leaned out the door. "You're early."

"Always am," the man said.

"Give me thirty minutes." Eric looked at Ruth on the bench. "Miss Vellmer, are you all right?"

"She's all right," the man said. "She's going to let me buy her a cup of coffee while you finish making me poor. Aren't you, ma'am?"

The last man to offer Ruth Vellmer a cup of coffee had been a soldier on leave at Christmas of 1943, in Union Station in Washington, and the restaurant there would not seat the two of them at one table. She heard herself say yes.

---

The Mustang Grill sat directly across Main from Eric Rogers's office, in a narrow limestone storefront under a green-and-gold awning, and from the front booth a person could read the gold letters on his window. Last fall's football schedule was still taped inside the glass by the door, ten games and ten scores in black marker, all but two of them losses: 7–42. 0–35. 14–13. 3–49. Inside stood chrome stools with green vinyl tops, a jar of pickled eggs the color of old pearls, and a pie case with a hand-lettered card propped against every pie.

"Morning, Miss Ruth," the waitress said. Bonnie Gold had poured coffee at the Mustang Grill since the Carter administration. She looked at the man and lifted one eyebrow. Then she looked at the vest on the yellow dog folding herself under the booth and lifted the other one.

"He caught me falling off Eric Rogers's steps," Ruth said. "He gets coffee."

"Then he gets pie." Bonnie set down two cups and two slices of buttermilk pie without asking anybody.

At the end of the counter Miss Tipton, who owned the storage units on the east end of town and had taken her mail from Ruth's hand six days a week for thirty years, turned on her stool and lifted her coffee cup to the man.

"Morning, Houston," she said, and turned back to her eggs.

The man was from Houston, he said. He bred Labrador retrievers and had trained families by the thousands in their own living rooms. He had lived in Martinsburg since the winter, in a rented place out on the Kerrville road with a yard big enough for the dogs, and Eric Rogers had taken his case.

"What kind of case?"

"A lawsuit over a movie, with a director named Peter. I'd rather tell you about the movie."

Ruth waited.

"Holland, under the Nazis." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the saucer. "A watchmaker's family in Haarlem, the old man and his two grown daughters. They built a room behind a false wall upstairs and hid Jews in it, and young fellas from the Dutch underground. Boys, mostly. The picture's about the boys. One of them, a fella named Hans Poley, wrote a book when he was an old man, and the picture's called *Return to the Hiding Place*. Poley tells how they'd drill, how fast they could all get into that little room the second the alarm sounded. Then the day the Gestapo came."

Ruth's cup had stopped halfway to her mouth.

She set it on its saucer. The coffee shivered in a ring and went still. Her left hand moved across the oilcloth by itself until her thumb found the bare place at the base of her ring finger and rested there, the way a tongue goes to the gap of a pulled tooth.

Haarlem.

Ruth saw, plain as the pie case, a woman's hand, thin as a stick of kindling, holding out a crust of black bread in the dark. She saw a stack of letters with Dutch stamps, tied with a faded blue ribbon, lying in a footlocker under her iron bed in the stone house on the homeplace. She said none of it out loud in the Mustang Grill on a Tuesday morning.

"The family," she said. "Did they live?"

"Some of them. The old man died in prison about ten days after they were taken. One of the daughters died in a camp in Germany. The other one came home and went all over the world telling about it." He looked at her. "You went white as that napkin."

"The pills," Ruth said. "Some mornings the floor moves."

He looked at her a while longer, the way he had looked at the dog under the booth, the way she would learn he looked at anything with a heartbeat. Then he let the matter drop. For the rest of her life she counted that in his favor.

"How many pills?"

"A box with fourteen doors. The doctor gives me a new pill every time I tell him about the last one."

"Who's at the house with you?"

"God, when He's speaking to me." She picked up her fork. "Mostly He keeps His own counsel."

The man laughed, one short bark, and Bonnie looked over from the pie case.

"You need a dog," he said.

"I'm eighty-seven years old."

"Then you need a good one." He pointed his fork at the floor. Under the booth the yellow dog lay with her chin on his boot and her eyes on Ruth. "That's Honey. She's two. I named her for a dog we had when I was a boy. My family got that dog in 1974, the year the Super Bowl came to Houston. My sisters were dating, and every boy who came to the door, Honey met him in the front hall. She sat down and looked him over, head to boots. If she lay down at his feet, he was all right. If she got up and walked out of the room, that boy might as well have gone on home." He shook his head. "She graded every one of them. She was never wrong. Not once."

Ruth laughed. The sound surprised her. The laugh came the way water comes from a pipe that has sat dry all summer, rusty first and then clear.

He had trained Honey for a client, he said. In January the family had called to say they would not need her after all, and they wanted to donate her to the right person, and their preference was a veteran or a Gold Star family.

He looked at Ruth over his coffee. "Any chance you're a veteran?"

"I don't tell many people," Ruth said, "but yes, I am, as a matter of fact." She did not say which service, or where, and he asked nothing more. "My brother Walter went down with the *Oklahoma* at Pearl Harbor. He lies in Hawaii under a stone marked Unknown."

The man set his cup down and did not say anything for a while. Under the booth Honey thumped her tail once against the floor.

---

He walked her home on the street side, the way men did when she was a girl. He kept his hand near her elbow, never on it, and the yellow dog walked at his other heel in her vest, all the way down Elm Street, past the redbuds blooming purple in a neighbor's yard and the first bluebonnets coming up blue-grey in the bar ditch, to the white frame house with the deep porch. Halfway there she came within one breath of telling him about the letters with the Dutch stamps. He did not ask what had turned her white in the café, and she let the question lie where he had left it. Ruth and the stranger stood on her front walk a moment, two people who had each just carried something heavy past the other without either one setting the load on the ground.

"I'll come see you," he said.

Eleven years before, at the gate of the homeplace, Ruth had looked down every road in the county for the Lord and could not find Him on one of them. That Tuesday He had walked a man up to Eric Rogers's door as she came down the steps, with a yellow dog at his heel. *Thou compassest my path and my lying down, and art acquainted with all my ways.* From her front walk Ruth watched the man from Houston go back up Elm Street, and she did not tell him one word of it.

---

He came the second Saturday in April, with Honey on the seat beside him.

Honey came down off the front seat on his word, in her red vest, and stood on the walk with her nose working. She was the color of cornbread crust, built square through the chest, with a white blaze on her breastbone shaped like a thumbprint and that one ear standing at the fold, as if she were listening to a different conversation from everybody else. She went up the porch steps ahead of them both. She smelled the swing, the welcome mat, and the cedar box in the corner where Ruth kept the nativity figures packed in newspaper eleven months of the year. She smelled Ruth's shoes. Then she sat down on the porch boards and looked straight up into Ruth's face.

"Well," Ruth said.

"She's looking you over," the man said. "Give her a minute."

Honey lay down across Ruth's feet. She put her chin on the toe of Ruth's left shoe and let out a long sigh through her nose, the sigh of a man who has finished a fence.

"That's a passing grade," he said.

Ruth looked down at the yellow head on her shoe. No dog had lain across her feet in thirty-one years, not since Bess went under the lavender, and before Bess there had been Liesl across the foot of the iron bed. Ruth had never let herself count how much she missed the weight of one.

The man stayed three afternoons. He taught Ruth the words, and he taught Honey the house. The first words came before all the rest, the same ones he taught first to every dog he ever trained: COME, SIT, HEEL and FETCH IT. Ruth said each one in the voice she used on cattle, and Honey answered each one before the word was out of Ruth's mouth. "Fetch it" sent Honey after a pill bottle under the stove, the reading glasses on the bedroom floor, the flip-up cell phone wherever Ruth had last left it. After those came the service words. "Brace" made the dog plant all four feet and go stiff as a sawhorse, so Ruth could put both hands on her back and push herself out of the low chair without the room tipping her onto the rug. "Stay with her" kept the dog at Ruth's knee from one room to the next.

In the kitchen Honey put her nose to the long pill box on the counter, snorted once, and took two steps backward. He watched that and said nothing.

On the back step he showed Ruth how the dog would stop at the top and lean her shoulder into Ruth's knee if Ruth started down too fast. "She'll put herself between you and the drop," he said. "Every time. You'll fuss at her for it. She won't care."

"I fuss at everybody."

"Noticed that."

On the third evening he stood at the open door of the truck, with no dog on the seat beside him, with the sun dropping behind the courthouse and the grackles settling into the pecans on the square, loud as a schoolyard at recess. He told her he would be in the county until June, and then he was going back toward Houston. He wrote his cell phone number on the side of the dog-food sack in black marker, in numbers two inches tall.

"I can read," Ruth said.

"I know you can. That's for Honey."

She stood on the porch until his taillights turned onto Main. Then she went inside and locked the door. The dog already lay across the threshold of the bedroom, chin on her paws, the one ear up, keeping watch on the hall.

Ruth sat on the edge of the bed in the dark. Since she had moved to town, the only sounds in the house at night had been the refrigerator, the clock and her own breathing. Now another creature breathed in the doorway, slow and even as the sea against the seawall at Hospital Point on a calm night in 1941. She listened a long time. Somewhere past midnight she lay down, and for the first time since Christmas she slept straight through until the light came.

---

He came back the last Saturday in June with the truck loaded to the rails under a blue tarp roped down tight. That summer the whole state lay under the worst one-year drought on its record, and the bar ditch along Elm Street had gone the color of a paper sack. Ruth met him at the gate with a pint of peach preserves in each hand.

"Last of the Stonewall peaches," she said. "The drought got the rest."

He set the jars on the dashboard and came up the walk. Honey met him at the bottom step with her whole back end going, then remembered her manners, came back to Ruth's left knee and sat. He knelt on the bricks and ran his hands over the dog the way a man checks his own work: ears, ribs, the pads of her feet, the white blaze on her chest.

"She's yours now," he said. "She knows it. Don't let anybody tell you different."

"Who would tell me different?"

He stood and dusted his knees and looked down Elm Street toward the courthouse a moment before he answered.

"Somebody always tries," he said.

"You'll write." Ruth did not say it as a question.

"Yes ma'am. You'll answer?"

"I carried the mail in this county for thirty-nine years," Ruth said. "I answer every letter that comes to this house."

He laughed the one short bark she had heard in the Mustang Grill, shook her long hand, and held it a second, and then he climbed into the truck. She stood at the gate until the tarp went out of sight past the square, headed west out of town on the Kerrville road.

Then she went inside, sat down at the kitchen table with Honey across her feet, and wrote him a letter before he had been gone an hour, so that her words would be waiting in his box when he got home.
