# 17. The Widow at Twenty-One

*Malmö, Sweden, May 1945, to the Pedernales River, Pedernales County, Texas, December 1947*

The Swedes burned every rag the women from Ravensbrück carried out of Germany.

A nurse in a gauze mask held a canvas sack open at the door of a requisitioned schoolhouse, and the women off the white buses dropped their rags into it, the striped dresses gone dishwater grey, the wooden clogs, the strips of blanket they had wound around their feet since October. A man with an iron hook dragged the sacks to a fire in an oil drum, and smoke the color of wet wool rolled over the schoolyard wall.

Ruth kept her right fist shut.

The nurse said something in Swedish, then tried German. "Alles. Bitte."

Ruth said in German that she had nothing left. The nurse looked at the fist and went to the next woman.

In the shower room Ruth opened her hand for the first time in two days. The tag lay in her palm inside the strip of grey-and-blue camp cloth she had torn from her dress seam on the ferry, a small notched rectangle of Army metal stamped with a name she had not spoken aloud since July. She closed her fingers over it and let the hot water run down ribs she could count with her eyes. She never stood under a shower again.

They weighed her on a doctor's scale in the school gymnasium. Forty-one kilos, a whisker over ninety pounds. The doctor looked at the ulcers on her shins, at the four round white burns in a row on the inside of her left forearm, at her scalp where the hair was coming back in a grey-brown fuzz like a pasture after a fire, and he wrote it all on a card without asking one question.

On the fourth morning a young man from the American legation in Stockholm sat down beside her cot with a folder on his knee, told her London had carried her as missing since August, and asked for the name of her next of kin.

"My parents," Ruth said. "Fritz and Katharina Vellmer. Martinsburg, Texas."

"Anyone else?"

Ruth looked at the gull on the windowsill, its feathers blown backward by the Øresund wind.

"No," she said. "Nobody else."

When he had gone she borrowed a pencil and three sheets of hospital paper from the Polish girl in the next cot, and wrote the letter she had composed every night for six months on a plank bunk in Barracks 28. She addressed it to Mrs. Hattie Mayfield, at the frame house on the East Side of San Antonio whose number she had read on the backs of forty-one envelopes. She wrote it plain: the Red Cross, a hospital in Sweden, a question about Sam, and a line to say she thought of all of them every day. Anybody in Texas who cared to look could open a white girl's letter to a colored mother.

On the last sheet, for nobody, she wrote the date.

> *Friday, May 4, 1945*
>
> *Malmö. They burned the dress. I have the cloth and what was in it. There is a gull on the window every morning. I wrote to Mrs. Mayfield. Now I wait, which is the one thing Ravensbrück taught me to do well.*

---

In the first week of June a second American came, an older man in a tweed coat who said he worked for the commercial section of the legation, the way the OSS men in Stockholm always said it. He sat with her three mornings in a spare office above the harbor and wrote down every name she could give him, every barracks number, every guard she could describe down to the boots. On the third morning he capped his pen, shut the door and told her what the papers had printed while she lay behind the wire, and one thing kept out of every paper.

The Red Army had reached Ravensbrück on the thirtieth of April, five days after her bus pulled out through the gate. The women still standing on the Lagerstrasse that morning belonged to the Russians now.

One of his own colleagues, he said, had feigned a bad back through the winter of 1943 so he could lie on a table in Stockholm under the hands of Himmler's masseur. The same masseur had flown into Berlin that April with a Swedish Jew named Norbert Masur. At half past two in the morning after Hitler's birthday, Himmler sat down with Masur at the masseur's country place north of the city and agreed to let Jewish women out of Ravensbrück.

"The Polish girls," Ruth said. "The ones from Lublin. Where do they go now?"

The man in the tweed coat turned his pen over in his fingers a long moment before he answered her.

In February, at the old tsar's Livadia Palace above Yalta on the Black Sea, Roosevelt and Churchill had sat down with Stalin and settled the map of Europe. Poland went to a government Stalin chose, with a promise of free elections, and the man in the tweed coat did not pretend to believe a word of it. The rabbits were going home to Lublin, and Lublin already answered to Stalin's committee. A second paper signed there, he said, had never reached any newspaper: every Soviet citizen in Allied hands would go home to Stalin, willing or not. Ruth saw again the two Russian girls who had set their soup kettle down in the snow the evening of the shots. On the first of June, near Lienz in Austria, the British had driven the Cossacks who fought for the Germans onto trucks with rifle butts, bound for the Russian lines, and some of their women threw their children into the Drava rather than hand them to the Soviets.

In the first week of May the Czechs had risen in the streets of Prague and begged on the radio, in English, for the American tanks. General Patton's Third Army stood at Pilsen, a morning's drive west. Eisenhower ordered him to halt. The Red Army took Prague on the ninth of May.

"Why did they stop him?" Ruth asked.

The man put his pen in his pocket. "Because it was in the agreement," he said.

Ruth looked out the window at the harbor. She thought of the Polish girls in Barracks 28 who had prayed every night for American tanks, and of the Czech women who had sung on the Lagerstrasse in the dark so the guards would not hear their crying. She had carried messages through the Drôme for men who promised her that Europe would be free. A courier learns to hear the silence in a man's report. This man had not said whose agreement it was, and nobody had asked the women in the barracks.

She was home in Texas before she understood what he had told her: the iron door had swung shut five days behind her bus, and her own side had hung the hinges.

---

The doctors kept her in Sweden through June because her lungs rattled at night and a fever climbed in her every afternoon like the mercury in a thermometer left on a truck dash. By the middle of the month she could walk to a bench above the harbor, where she sat in a borrowed coat and watched the ferries run to Copenhagen as if the war had never happened.

Hattie's answer came on the twenty-first of June.

The envelope held a letter in pencil on tablet paper, a second sheet in the same hand, and a photograph.

The photograph fell out first, face up on the bench beside her.

A young Black man in a leather flying helmet with the goggles pushed up on his forehead stood beside the nose of a fighter plane, one hand flat on the metal the way a cowboy lays his palm on a trusted horse. The pilot was grinning at whoever held the camera, a big, crooked grin she had first seen start, barely, on top of Enchanted Rock in the spring of 1937, above her own splinted nose.

On the back of the photograph, in his square hand: *Ramitelli, Italy, 1944. Red tail, red-hot. S.* **[VERIFY: 332nd Fighter Group base at Ramitelli, Italy, in 1944]**

She read Hattie's letter with the photograph face down in her lap.

*Dear Miss Ruth,*

*I am sorry to be the one. The War Department sent a telegram in April saying Sam was missing in action over Germany on the 24th of March. Then a letter came from a Lieutenant in his squadron who said he saw Sam's plane go down burning a long way inside Germany on a mission to Berlin, and he did not see a parachute. Last week the Army wrote that Sam is dead. They say he died that day.*

*I have copied out for you the end of the last letter he sent me. I could not part with his own paper. He sent me two of the picture, so this one is yours.*

*You were always good to my boy. God keep you.*

*Hattie Mayfield*

Hattie had copied it with a mother's care, every word spelled the way her son had spelled it, the crossings-out crossed out again in her own pencil.

*Mama, I am flying long ones now, all the way up into Germany and back, and the boys are good and the airplane is better. If anything happens to me, I want you to find a Texas girl named Ruth Vellmer, from down the river by the Harwell place. You remember her. Tell her I got back to the group like I promised her. Tell her I am sorry about the train station in Washington. Tell her I kept my word. She will know what I mean by it. Don't ask her about it, Mama. Just tell her.*

The twenty-fourth of March. Ruth counted back by roll calls and bread, as the camp had taught her to count, and found the sleet on the Lagerstrasse that morning, and knew she had not felt one thing go out of the world, not one.

She did not cry. She sat on the bench above the harbor with the photograph face down in her lap and her right hand closed around the tag in her coat pocket until the edges of the notch bit into her palm, and the ferry blew its horn for Copenhagen, and she was twenty-one years old.

> *Thursday, June 21, 1945*
>
> *Sam is dead since the 24th of March. Mrs. Mayfield copied his letter out for me because she could not let go of his. I would not have let it go either. He says he kept his word. He always kept it.*
>
> *I will not write the rest. Some things a book cannot hold and a girl cannot say, and in Texas the law would hang a name on them.*

---

Henry met the train at the Southern Pacific depot in San Antonio on a Sunday afternoon in July, still in his co-op khakis, and walked right past her on the platform.

She said his name. He looked at the Red Cross dress hanging on her like a feed sack on a fence post, and his face came apart the way men's faces had come apart on the burn ward in Honolulu when the nurses lifted the dressings.

"Ruthie," he said, and picked her up the way he used to swing her onto the mule. She weighed nothing, and brother and sister both felt it.

At the gate of the homeplace her mother stood in the yard in her apron with a dish towel twisted in both hands, and her father on the porch behind her, bareheaded, his hat against his leg. Ruth saw for the first time that Fritz Vellmer was an old man. Katharina's hands found her daughter's ribs through the Red Cross dress, and the dish towel went down in the dirt, as a clean sheet had gone down there in 1929, when a girl of five climbed too high in the live oak.

Katharina learned her daughter's war the way a mother learns most things, by picking up after it. Every morning at half past four, with no clock in the room, Ruth came awake in the dark and stood beside her bed with her arms straight at her sides, the way the women had stood on the Lagerstrasse to be counted, until the shapes of the chifforobe and the window gave her back the room on the Pedernales. In October a neighbor woman from down the river brought a sack of turnips from her garden, and Ruth thanked her at the gate, carried the sack to the lot and fed every turnip to the cows. The next spring Katharina left the turnip row out of the garden.

The first Sunday home, her mother put on her hat and gloves and waited at the screen door while the bells of St. Paul came across the river bottom on a south wind.

"Kommst du?"

"No, Mama."

Katharina went out to the truck alone.

Ruth sat on the step and watched her mother's dust hang over the river road. A verse came up in her without her leave, the way the Lord's Prayer had come up on the fifth floor of Avenue Foch, and this time the line was not Ruth's but Naomi's, five verses past the one Sam had finished for her in the temple. *I went out full, and the LORD hath brought me home again empty.* Ruth had gone out with a brother on the *Oklahoma* and a boy in a flying school, and the Lord had brought her home on a Southern Pacific train with a dog tag in her fist. Ruth said one word to the river bottom, and the word was *Mara*.

After a while Fritz came out in his work pants with a bucket of last year's pecans and two nutcrackers and sat down on the step beside his daughter. He did not say why he was not in his pew.

They cracked pecans until the bells rang the end of the service, and the only sound on the porch was the shells breaking.

Ruth knew why her father stayed home from church. In 1918 four men from the Council of Defense in Austin had stood on this porch and called him a Hun to his face. In 1940 he had found his sixteen-year-old daughter on a limestone ledge beside Hattie Mayfield's oldest boy, talking about airplanes, and inside a month the Mayfields were gone from the Harwell place after two generations. He had done to Hattie Mayfield's family what those men from Austin had done to his. Ruth never said one word to him about 1940, and he never asked her one word about France.

---

On the sixth of October the shoebox on her closet shelf, where the photograph lay turned to the cardboard, got its first clipping: a picture from the San Antonio paper of President Truman hanging the Medal of Honor on the boy from the laundry truck in Hawaii, a Marine of seventeen who had thrown himself on two Japanese grenades on Iwo Jima and lived, with more than two hundred pieces of shrapnel still in him.

On the twenty-second of December the same paper reported that General George S. Patton had died in an Army hospital in Heidelberg, twelve days after an Army truck turned across the path of his staff car on a road outside Mannheim. For a week the paper had printed that he was mending. He had come through the whole war at the front of his tanks, and now the paper wanted Texas to believe a crumpled fender on a Sunday drive to hunt pheasants had killed him. Ruth read the paragraph twice, the way she had read a report in the Drôme, then cut that one out too and laid it on top of the Marine.

---

Ernie came home from Camp Hearne in January 1946, after two and a half years of guarding German prisoners and talking with them through the wire in their own language.

On his second Saturday home he drove Ruth to the feed store in Martinsburg. Otto Lindauer stood at the counter with a sack of cottonseed cake on his shoulder while old Roy Hagen, whose grandson and namesake still runs that store, wrote the ticket. Otto was twenty-three now, thick through the neck, and nine years before he had pinned Sam Mayfield against the sheet-iron wall of the old cotton gin.

"Heard you had yourself a fine war, Ernie," Otto said. "Sittin' up nights jawin' Kraut with the Nazis. Bet they felt right at home."

Roy laid down his pencil. "Otto, take your cake and go home," he said, and Otto went. Out in the truck Ernie sat with both hands on the wheel and did not start the engine.

"Most of them were farm boys, Ruthie," he said at last. "A few were the real thing, and I could name every one. In December of '43 the real thing beat a corporal named Hugo Krauss to death for being too friendly with us. Walter's in a grave in Hawaii marked Unknown, and that man in there thinks I'm a Nazi because I can talk German to one."

Ruth thought about Scharführer Strelow at 84 Avenue Foch, who had pressed four burning cigarettes, one after another, against the inside of her left arm, neat as buttons on a shirt. She could have told her brother a great deal about the real thing.

"Start the truck," she said.

He started the truck.

---

On the fifth of March, 1946, Ruth stood at the kitchen counter with her hands in the dishwater while the radio carried Winston Churchill's voice from a college gymnasium in Fulton, Missouri, with President Truman seated on the platform behind him. *From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent.* Katharina said it was a fine phrase. Ruth dried her hands and went out to the lot. She had watched that curtain come down five days behind her bus, and the man with the fine phrase had sat at Stalin's elbow at Yalta.

---

The parcel from London came on the twenty-fifth of April, 1946, a year to the day after the white buses, with a return address that was only a box number. Katharina carried it out to the calf pen, and Ruth, on her knees with a scouring calf's head locked under her arm while Fritz poured the drench, did not let go until the calf had swallowed the last of the dose.

Inside was a cardboard box Ruth knew, with a card on the lid in a stores girl's round hand: VELLMER, and under it, LUCIE. The diary lay in its waxed canvas cover beside the bundle of forty-one letters in Lena's fiddle string, the V-mail from Italy still under the knot where she had slid it on the twenty-fifth of June, 1944. A note on plain paper said, *These were left in our keeping. I am glad to send them home to the one who left them. V. M. Atkins.* The woman who signed it was in the ruins of Germany herself that spring, asking camp guards for her girls by name.

That night Ruth folded the pages she had written on hospital paper in Malmö into the back of the diary and wrote one more entry.

> *Thursday, April 25, 1946*
>
> *The book came home today, with his letters still in Lena's knot. I laid the Sweden pages in the back of it. A girl wrote in this book who thought she would read it aloud to somebody someday and hear his laugh. I will let her keep it. I am done writing in it.*

---

In the spring Ruth had written one page to the pastor's wife at Valfrède about a borrowed ring she had no way to return. The answer came in August with a Dieulefit postmark, and Ruth read it on the tailgate of the hay truck with a thunderhead over the river and the first drops spotting the ink. Madeleine wrote that a ring was only silver and asked for nothing in return. Odile's Henri had come home from Germany the spring before, thin as a hoe handle and alive. Gaston had not come home. The Germans shot him in Valence in the first week of August 1944, and Marthe still kept the goat and the lavender and sent her love. Folded inside her letter lay a second envelope from Pasteur Jean Chabrol, who wrote that he had kept the enclosed under a flagstone beneath the communion table until the Germans were gone, and had waited for word of anyone left to receive it.

*Ceci vous appartient,* he wrote. This belongs to you.

Ruth did not unfold the paper in the rain. The envelope rode buttoned inside her shirt until the loft was full, and that night Ruth carried it upstairs to the olive-drab footlocker under her iron bed, stenciled R. M. VELLMER, wrapped the paper in a square of oilcloth and laid it flat against the bottom boards, under the nurse's cap and everything else, where no hand but hers would find it.

---

In March of 1947 the carrier on the rural route out of the Martinsburg post office retired after twenty-six years. Ruth took the civil-service examination for the route at a table under the WANTED posters in the lobby, beside eleven men, most of them home from the war, and scored highest of the twelve. She bought a war-surplus Willys Jeep in San Antonio with the white Army star still ghosting through the paint on the hood, because rural carriers furnished their own vehicles. On the first Monday in April she was at the post office at six-thirty casing mail into the pigeonholes in route order, and by eight the Willys was throwing caliche dust over the first cattle guard.

Every family on the route learned her Jeep, her wave and the way she squared the flag on a box before she drove to the next one. She carried seed catalogs and pension checks and the letters a family waited all week to read, and she never once lost a piece. Ruth Vellmer had found the one job in Pedernales County where a woman could be seen by everybody every day and known by nobody, eight hours alone in an open Jeep with the wind in her face and no one behind her.

---

The letter from Holland came in December 1947 with a Dutch stamp, a Bloemendaal postmark and Corrie ten Boom's round, upright hand on the envelope. Ruth read it on the porch in her coat with a norther rattling the bare pecans.

Corrie wrote from a big house with gardens, and she wrote about the guard from the shower-room door at Ravensbrück who found her in a Munich church basement, and what it had cost her to forgive him.

Ruth sat with the letter in her lap and looked at her left hand, at the bare finger where a borrowed ring had sat three days in the Drôme, before it went onto a string at her throat and a guard with yellow braids at Ravensbrück snapped the string and buttoned the ring into her own tunic pocket.

The guard in Munich had come to Corrie. He had named his sin. He had asked to be forgiven.

Nobody had ever come to Ruth Vellmer. Nobody had ever named one thing that had been done to her or asked her for anything, and the men who had done the worst of it were in the ground or in the mountains or back home with their wives, drinking beer under linden trees. She sat on the porch until the cold got into her fingers, and she could not tell whether what she felt was envy of Corrie or envy of the guard.

On Saturday, the twentieth of December, she ran the half-day route, then drove the Willys over the hills to Kerrville, where no clerk at the window knew the mail lady from Martinsburg, and stood in line behind a rancher mailing a ham. She bought a postal money order for twenty dollars, payable to Mrs. Hattie Mayfield, San Antonio, Texas, then crossed the street to the dime store for a Christmas card with a picture of the Magi on camels crossing a blue desert under one great star.

Inside the card she wrote only *Merry Christmas*, and under it two letters.

*R. V.*

She sealed the envelope, licked the stamp, and pressed it flat in the corner with her bare finger.
