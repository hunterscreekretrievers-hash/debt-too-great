# Epilogue: Christmond

*Christmas Day, 2026. Martinsburg, Pedernales County, Texas.*

The power came back a little after nine. The refrigerator in the cabin kitchen shuddered and took up its humming, every light Daniel had left switched on blazed at once, and Ben ran through the rooms turning them all off again, because the house looked wrong with lights burning in the daytime.

Ben had found the package before any of that, while the window was still grey: a small square wrapped in spiral-notebook paper under the leaning cedar, beside the bucket of creek rocks, where nothing had been at midnight. He stared at it, then at the dead bulb over the table, then at the package again. "Santa found us with no power," he said.

Lily did not look at her grandfather. "He has a satellite," she said.

Ruthie made biscuits in Ruth's black skillet, and the children ate them standing at the window, watching the ice go to water on every fence wire and every live-oak limb, dripping and shining and letting go its hold.

At noon Marcus came and stood in front of Fritz's cowhide chair with his coat already zipped.

"Can we go see her?"

Daniel drove. The county road ran wet and black between white ditches, and the cattle guards rang under the tires. At the low-water crossing the Pedernales stood high and brown against the slab, and Daniel stopped the truck and studied the water a long time before he trusted it. Deer stood in the open pastures and watched the truck go by like landlords checking on a tenant. Rural mailboxes went past on their cedar posts, a dented one every quarter mile. From 1947 to 1986 Miss Ruth carried that route out of the Martinsburg post office, first in a war-surplus Willys and later in a right-hand-drive Jeep Dispatcher, and every box on this road held mail from her hand at one time or another. The whole county knew her wave. Nobody knew her story.

The grey cardigan stayed open the whole way, and the tag rode outside it on its chain where anybody could see it. Ruthie noticed and said nothing this time. She reached across the seat and turned the tag so the P showed.

Martinsburg sits on its square the way it has since the Germans laid out the town. Limestone storefronts wear their dates cut in the cornices. The Mustang Grill was closed, with a paper wreath on the door and a hand-lettered sign: *Merry Christmas, Go Mustangs, Back Tuesday.* The courthouse clock ran four minutes slow, the way it has run in every living memory in town. White lights hung along the second-floor rail of the hardware store. Lyle's pickup sat at the curb in front of the feed store with the keys in the ignition and the doors unlocked, the way Lyle has left that truck every day of his life. Somebody had shoveled the slush off the sidewalk on the south side of the square, the side that stays in shadow, and spread rock salt in a careful line.

The statue sits on the south lawn under a pecan tree, facing Main.

The children were out of the truck before Daniel had it in park.

She sits on a bus bench, life size, in bronze gone a little green at the folds of her coat. Her pocketbook rides on her knees. She has her good shoes on, her ankles crossed the way a lady was taught to cross them in 1930. Honey sits beside her with her chin in Ruth's lap and her one ear standing straight, and Ruth's left hand rests on the dog's head.

Ben, like every child before him, went straight to the dog. The bronze of Honey's head has worn bright gold where a thousand small hands have rubbed it, and he rubbed it too, with his mitten off, then put his face down close to hers and looked into her eyes.

"She's cold," he said. "Where's the real Honey?"

"Lyle kept her. She slept under his porch swing eight more years and rode No. 7 to every away game. Somewhere past ten her muzzle went white and her hips went stiff on cold mornings, so Lyle lifted her up the bus steps in his arms and set her on Miss Ruth's old seat. Every Friday of the season she still went to Lyle's front window at twenty minutes to three, long after she could no longer make the climb by herself. She was thirteen when she died. Lyle buried her in the pecan bottom below the cabin, under the big pecan that gave Fritz Vellmer the limb for his manger, wrapped in the grey wool coat that came off the Elm Street porch with her."

Ben chewed on that. "So she's at your house, Grandpa."

"She's at our house."

Lily stood in front of the bench and read the plaque out loud. RUTH MARIE VELLMER. 1924–2013. HONEY. Under that, in smaller letters, ran the names of twelve boys, and under the names, the line Dixie Beck had settled: *She rode with us.*

Lily read it twice. Then she put one mittened finger on the brass just after VELLMER, where the metal ran smooth and empty for an inch before the dates began.

"There's room," she said.

"So there is room."

---

*What filled that inch came later.*

On the Monday after Christmas, Marcus and I carried the marriage paper, the tag and the folder marked LUCIE in a shoebox up the stairs to the Martinsburg paper on Main Street. The editor read the French with a dictionary open on her desk and photographed the tag on a sheet of white typing paper, and the story ran that Thursday under a headline with no adjectives in it: MARTINSBURG WIDOW WAS WIFE OF TUSKEGEE AIRMAN. Under the fold ran a second headline, LUCIE WAS MISS RUTH, and beneath it the paper reprinted, word for word, the letter it had first printed on Thursday, the seventeenth of January, 1980: the two TIME covers, the Grand Mufti in Berlin, the crowd in Tehran, the townspeople of Weimar, and *Lucie, Pedernales County* at the bottom. For thirty-four years Martinsburg had read Lucie over coffee at the Mustang Grill, and for the first six of those years the woman behind Lucie had put that paper in the county's mailboxes with her own hand. By the weekend the story had gone around the country, and the line everybody passed along was the plain fact. The racist grandmother of The Daily Beat had married a Black fighter pilot in a French church in 1944, when the law of her own state made that marriage a felony, two to five years in the penitentiary. The Daily Beat left its old headline standing. CNM never said a word. Shari Whitcomb, who had talked to every camera in 2014, did not take one call.

---

*February 2027.*

The room filled in February. On a raw Tuesday the booster club sent an engraver from Kerrville, paid out of Dixie Beck's coffee cans, to kneel on a folded feed sack in front of the bench and cut MAYFIELD into that smooth inch after VELLMER and ahead of the dates, in letters made to match, and under the name one more line: WIFE OF LT. SAMUEL J. MAYFIELD, 332ND FIGHTER GROUP. For the rest of that winter the new letters shone raw gold against the old brown brass, the way a new nail head shines in a weathered board, until the March rains dulled them down to the same brown. Lily went back to the square the first Saturday after the cutting and laid one finger in the M, and the edges of the new cut were still sharp under her fingertip.

---

That was February. On Christmas Day, with the ice still dripping off the pecan, Marcus did not read the plaque. He walked all the way around the bench once, slowly, the way he had circled that trunk the night before, a full turn before he would put a hand on it. Then he stood in front of her and studied her face. The sculptor had worked from photographs and got her right: the long jaw, the mouth set to tell you something you were not going to enjoy hearing, the eyes.

"Were they really grey?"

"Grey as a norther."

Marcus put his hands in his coat pockets. "Grandpa. What happened to all that money?"

"Eric Rogers opened that second envelope alone, in his office, with the door shut." A few blocks down West Main, the same sun lay on the gold letters in the lawyer's front window. "He has never said one word about what was in it. The county quit asking him somewhere around 2016."

"Somebody knows the answer."

"Somebody always knows, son."

Lily had her mitten on Honey's bronze head. "Grandpa, what happened to the dog man, and to all those lawsuits?"

"The people who sued him settled and paid him, because their lawsuits were groundless, lawfare meant to intimidate him, though they never paid the quarter of a million they owed him. They served him with one of those lawsuits on the night his mother died. They had one of his own friends secretly tape his conversations for a year and a half, more than ninety of them, the kind of talk a man has only with a friend, and the tapes went to Amanda."

Lily took her mitten off Honey's head. "His own friend taped him?"

"His own friend. A man who ate at his table." Ben was listening with his whole face, so I said the rest to him. "King David put it in the forty-first Psalm: *Yea, mine own familiar friend, in whom I trusted, which did eat of my bread, hath lifted up his heel against me.* He wrote it again in the fifty-fifth Psalm: *For it was not an enemy that reproached me; then I could have borne it: neither was it he that hated me that did magnify himself against me; then I would have hid myself from him: But it was thou, a man mine equal, my guide, and mine acquaintance. We took sweet counsel together, and walked unto the house of God in company.* Pick your friends the way the dog man picks a pup, son. Watch what a man does when he thinks nobody is looking, and watch who is paying him.

"Miss Ruth knew that kind of friend before any of you were born. In the Drôme an old waiter who called her *ma fille* and poured her lemonade let the Milice wait for her in his back room, and he said *pardon* over and over while they split her face open on his doorframe. In Haarlem a man knocked on the ten Booms' door asking for help, then sold the whole family to the Gestapo. Some friends sell you for money, and some sell you because a man with a pistol gave the order. The price changes. The friend is the same."

Marcus shook his head slowly. "That's lower than the lawsuits."

"Then, right before the movie came out, the people who had sued the dog man dragged him back into court, in violation of the settlement they had signed, and asked a judge to seal every page of the record. Amanda's lawyer told the court the record was 'of a very legitimate and high concern' to her family's 'physical and financial security,' and that she had 'every right to protect the identity and the whereabouts of her daughters.' The same daughters' pictures were on Facebook, in the movie's trailer and in its marketing. The judge refused to seal the record, and the dog man sued them for breaking the settlement."

Marcus stared at the bronze dog. "Wow. The irony. A Christian movie about Nazi oppression, and they buried the man who made the filming possible under fifteen years of groundless litigation."

Lily turned from the bench to face me. "What were they hiding?"

"Plenty! But that's for another night around the fire. Ask the dog man, who has been raising and training Labs for thirty-five years." I looked at the bronze dog he had brought to this town in a red vest.

Ben rubbed the dog's gold head. "Honey would have bitten that lawyer."

"Honey had better manners than that lawyer, son."

Somebody had come before us. On the bench beside her, where a person would sit if a person sat down next to Miss Ruth on a bus, lay a green-and-gold letter jacket folded square, a pecan pie in a foil tin on top, and a note tucked under the tin against the wind. The melting ice had soaked the jacket dark. Ruthie lifted the corner of the tin, read the note, and set the tin back in its place.

"Every Christmas," she said. "Somebody."

The somebody has a name. The name stays on the bench with the pie.

At Ruth's bronze feet sat a coffee can wrapped in brown paper, and in it, rolled like a scroll, last year's Watch Night bulletin from Shiloh Missionary Baptist below Luling. On the back, under the offering, somebody had printed one line in capitals: IN MEMORY OF R. V.

The St. Paul bell started for the one o'clock service, in the tower Fritz Vellmer topped out with his own two hands, and the sound came over the roofs flat and heavy in the clean air. On the far side of the square the lady across Elm Street climbed the steps of St. Paul in a dark wool coat, and at the top she turned and lifted one gloved hand to me before she stepped through the doors. Her name could wait for another Sunday. Two blocks off, on Elm Street, an empty cedar stable stood on a white porch with its colored bulbs burning in the daylight, and Kyle Brandt's cruiser had sat in front of it from eleven until midnight the night before, the way it does every year.

Lily took the strip of camp cloth out of her coat pocket. She had brought it without asking anybody. She held it up against the bronze of Ruth's coat at the hem, the way a woman holds a swatch against a dress at the fabric store, then folded it again, slid it into her pocket and buttoned the flap. When the flap was buttoned, Lily looked up at me, and I knew the question from the cabin was still riding in that pocket with the cloth, the question I left without an answer. Lily has Gloria's patience. When Lily is grown she will ask it again, and I mean to answer it then. I know what it costs a girl to carry a question that long, and I know a man of eighty may not be here when she asks it.

Marcus had his phone in his hand. He did not take a picture. He held the phone a while, then switched it dark and pocketed it.

He reached out and laid his hand on Ruth's left hand, on the bronze, where it lies on the dog's head. His thumb found the bare wedding finger and stayed there.

Ben came around from the dog and took his grandfather's hand. Ben's hand was cold and sticky with biscuit honey.

"Grandpa. Uncle Sam gave the tag to Miss Ruth, and Miss Ruth gave it to you."

"Every word of it."

"Who gets it after you?"

Ruthie turned her head. Daniel studied the courthouse clock very hard.

"You'll know when."

Marcus still had his thumb on the bare finger. "How do you pay somebody back for something like that?" He was not asking his grandfather. He was asking her.

The wind came across the square and stirred the jacket on the bench. The bell stopped.

My hand went to the open collar and closed around Uncle Sam's tag, warm, the way it has been warm against a Mayfield chest every day for twelve years, the way it was warm in the hem of a girl's coat on the fifth floor of 84 Avenue Foch, and under her tongue at Ravensbrück, and in her fist on the ferry to Malmö.

"You don't pay a debt like that, son."

The tag stayed in my hand.

"You carry it."
