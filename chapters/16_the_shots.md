# 16. The Shots

*Ravensbrück, January to April 1945; the sky over Berlin, March 24, 1945*

The work party came back through the gate on a grey afternoon in the last week of January, and Ruth did not know them.

She was on a labor detail hauling a cart of frozen sand when the column came in from the station, thirty or forty women in men's jackets and rags, their feet wrapped in paper and wire, shuffling through the gate the way cattle come off a truck after a long haul, heads down, each woman holding up the next. **[VERIFY: Szabo, Bloch and Rolfe returned to Ravensbrück from the Königsberg-an-der-Oder subcamp in late January 1945]**

One woman at the back walked by herself. Her face had gone to bone and her dark hair was cropped and filthy, and she walked with her chin up and her shoulders back, the way she had run up the range stairs in London.

Ruth dropped the shaft of the cart.

"Violette."

Violette turned her head. For a second nothing moved in her face. Then the corner of her mouth lifted.

"Texas," she said, in a croak. "You're still here. I told them Americans were stubborn."

Behind her two women carried a third in a blanket slung like a hammock, and Ruth saw a thin freckled face, eyes shut, lips bloodless and cracked open on each breath. Lilian Rolfe weighed nothing. Denise Bloch came after them on two sticks with her feet wrapped in rags gone black and stiff, and Ruth smelled the feet from six steps away, sweet and rotten, like meat left out in August. The smell was gangrene. The flesh of Denise's toes was dying on her while she walked.

A guard shoved Ruth back toward the cart with a rifle butt. The column turned down the camp street toward the punishment block, where nobody went by choice. **[VERIFY: where the three were held on their return, January 1945]**

Of the three, only Violette Szabo walked in on her own two feet.

---

For a week Ruth tried to get to them. She traded her bread three days running to a Czech woman who carried soup to the punishment block, and the Czech woman came back each evening and shook her head. No visitors were allowed. The soup carrier had seen Lilian on a straw sack against the wall and Denise unwrapping her feet, and Violette sitting with her back straight and her eyes on the door.

"She asked about you," the carrier said on the third evening. "She said the factory at Torgau wanted shells for the German guns, and the women told the foreman no. She said tell Texas the soup is better at the Ritz."

Ruth laughed so suddenly that a woman nearby swore at her in Polish.

That night on the middle platform in Barracks 28 she thought about Violette at the pistol range in London, talking about her little girl, Tania, who would be not quite three now. Kate in Texas would turn three in February, and Ruth had not seen her since the fall of 1943. She did not pray for the three women. She lay with her hands in her armpits for warmth and said their names in the dark, one at a time, slowly, the way she had heard Betsie say the names of the sick. *Violette. Denise. Lilian.* She told herself the names were a list.

---

The shots came in the evening after the soup, in the first days of February. **[VERIFY: date of the executions, between about January 25 and February 5, 1945]**

Ruth was standing in the barracks doorway with a tin cup of hot water. The sky over the pines had turned the grey-yellow of old pewter, and smoke came from the crematorium chimney behind the wall, steady and low, flattening in the cold air over the camp.

The first shot sounded small, a flat crack like a man splitting a stick of kindling.

Up and down the camp street women stopped where they stood. Two Russian girls carrying a soup kettle set it down in the snow.

The second shot came. Ruth had counted the seconds between them the way Fritz had taught her to count between lightning and thunder on the porch. The count was eleven.

Then the third shot came.

Then the shots stopped, and the smoke went on rising from the chimney.

Ruth stood in the doorway with the cup in her hand until the water in it was as cold as the tin. Nobody said a word. The Russian girls picked up the kettle again.

Afterward, through the women who worked near the crematorium, the camp pieced together the rest. The three had been brought out into the yard at dusk. Lilian had to be carried. Denise could not stand. Violette walked. The commandant, Fritz Suhren, pronounced sentence on them, and they were made to kneel, and each was shot once in the back of the neck. **[VERIFY: account of the executions from postwar war-crimes testimony]** One woman swore she had seen them holding hands through a crack in a fence. Ruth never knew whether the woman told the truth or told what everybody needed, and she kept the detail anyway.

> *(Written on a loose sheet in Sweden, May 1945.)*
>
> I heard it. I heard all three. Eleven seconds between the first two, by my count. Between the second and the third I lost the count, and I cannot stand that I lost it.
>
> She asked about me. In that block, with them going to kill her, she asked the soup woman about me. I had a bad arm in September and she had a good one, and so she went to Torgau and I stayed. The whole difference between us was an infected arm.
>
> Violette was 23, Denise 29, Lilian 30. **[VERIFY: ages of Szabo, Bloch and Rolfe at death]** I am 21.

---

The camp ate its own after that.

In February the trucks began going out to the Uckermark, a little way off through the pines, a camp for girls once and now a pen for the old and the sick, and from the Uckermark the trucks returned by night to the gas chamber by the crematorium wall. Women were picked at roll call: any woman with grey hair, any woman with swollen legs, any woman who could not run the length of the camp street while a doctor watched. At first the guards said the picked women were going to a rest camp. Then their clothes came back without them and were dumped in a heap in the clothing room, every dress with its number still sewn to the breast. **[VERIFY: Uckermark selections and the gas chamber near the crematorium, early 1945]**

Ruth pinched her cheeks before every roll call to put color in them. When they made the women run, she ran the camp street with her clogs in her hands and her bare feet bleeding on the frozen sand, and the doctor waved her back into the rank with two fingers. Once a Norwegian woman stood in front of her at the moment the doctor's eyes went down the row, and the Norwegian woman was taken instead. Ruth never learned her name.

---

On Saturday, the twenty-fourth of March, Ruth stood at roll call from four in the morning until sunup, with sleet blowing sideways across the parade ground and the woman beside her coughing blood into her sleeve. Afterward she sat on the bottom tier of Barracks 28 with a Dutch girl's head in her lap and picked lice from the girl's hair one by one and cracked them between her thumbnails, all through the grey middle of the day.

She did not know anything had happened. Nobody in that camp could have told her.

*This next part, Miss Ruth never knew while she lived it, and she never saw the paper that carried it. The letter is in the Red Goose shoe box on Hackberry Street, in the hand of a lieutenant named Emmett Pierce of Detroit, who flew in the 100th Fighter Squadron with Uncle Sam and wrote to Grandma Hattie in April 1945, because Sam had asked it of him long before, if it came to that.*

*The Red Tails took off from Ramitelli on the Adriatic that morning, fifty-some P-51s with drop tanks under their wings, to fly the longest escort mission the Fifteenth Air Force ever flew: up over the Alps and across Germany to Berlin and home again, sixteen hundred miles round trip, with B-17s of the 5th Bomb Wing bound for the Daimler-Benz tank works. Colonel Benjamin O. Davis Jr., who had gone four years at West Point without a classmate speaking one word to him off duty, led the group until his engine went bad and he had to turn back, and Captain Armour McDaniel took it the rest of the way. They met the bombers near Berlin at a quarter to noon. The sun was out up there, Pierce wrote, and the ground was gone under a floor of grey.*

*Then the jets came.*

*Messerschmitt 262s, two dozen or more of them, the first jet fighters on earth, coming through the bomber stream so fast the Mustangs looked like they were parked. Pierce wrote that he had never seen anything move like that and never wanted to see it again. Three men of the 100th each got a jet that day, Roscoe Brown and Charles Brantley and Earl Lane, and the group won a Distinguished Unit Citation for the mission, and Captain McDaniel was shot down and spent the rest of the war in a German prison camp.*

*Sam turned into a jet that was diving on a Fortress, Pierce wrote, and got between them, and the jet's cannon caught his engine. The Mustang rolled over on its back with fire pouring from the cowling and went down through the haze a long way inside Germany. Pierce watched it until the haze took it. He did not see a parachute.*

*Sam was twenty-one years old. North of Berlin, beyond the pines, the girl from the Pedernales was cracking lice between her thumbnails in a barracks full of fleas, and she did not feel one thing go out of the world.*

---

On Good Friday the two nurses from La Luire brought word that Mère Élisabeth, a nun from Lyon, had gone to the gas chamber in place of a woman with children at home.

In April the guns began.

The rumble came from the east, so low that Ruth felt it in the bunk boards before she heard it. The Russians were coming. The guards got meaner and then got scarce. Prisoners in the clothing room fed records into the stove by the armful.

In the third week of April the white buses came up the sand road through the pines, white from bumper to roof, with big red crosses on their sides and tops so the airplanes would not shoot them. Men in grey-green uniforms with Red Cross armbands climbed down and stood beside the buses, tall, clean, pink-faced, staring at the camp like men looking down a well at something drowned.

The Swedes had come for the Scandinavian women first. Then the lists grew. A British prisoner named Mary Lindell, who had run an escape line in France and walked the camp with a nurse's air of total command, gave Schwarzhuber, the camp leader under Suhren, a list of forty-seven women of British and American birth, and Schwarzhuber let them go. Allied fighters shot up one column in spite of the crosses on the road to Denmark that week, and about twenty-five of the people aboard never reached the border.

On the twenty-fifth of April a Swedish officer with a clipboard read the short list at roll call in an accent that turned every name into something new.

"Vellmer, Ruth."

Ruth stepped out of the rank, and nobody struck her for it.

At the gate a guard ordered the women to drop the camp coats in a pile. Ruth dropped the brown coat with the white cross without looking at it. She kept the striped dress, and her hand stayed flat on the left side seam all the way out the gate.

The bus smelled of diesel and of Swedish soap. A woman in a starched apron gave each prisoner white bread, cheese and a bar of chocolate. Ruth held the bread in both hands and did not eat it. The Scottish girl beside her ate hers in three bites and was sick in the aisle ten minutes later. Ruth turned in her seat and saw the wall, the wire, the green barracks and the crematorium chimney, still smoking.

The buses went north and west across Germany: towns knocked down to brick and chimney stumps, a horse dead in a ditch with its legs stuck in the air, barefoot children who watched the white buses pass without waving. At the Danish border at Padborg a crowd stood along the road and began to throw things at the buses, and Ruth flinched, and then she saw the things were flowers, tulips and lilac from their own gardens, and bread, and apples, and the women on the bus began to cry. **[VERIFY: Danish crowds greeting the white buses at Padborg]**

Ruth did not cry. She sat with her hands in her lap and her thumb on the bare place at the base of her ring finger.

---

On the ferry from Copenhagen to Malmö she went up on deck alone. The wind off the Øresund smelled of salt and coal smoke. Sweden lay ahead, low and green, with a church steeple at the water's edge.

Ruth reached down to the side seam of the striped dress and worked at the blanket thread with her fingernails until it gave, and then she tore the seam out whole, a strip of grey and blue cloth as long as her forearm, and the tag slid out of it into her palm. She had not seen it in daylight since the barn in the Drôme. The metal had gone dull, and the rolled edges had worn smooth against the cloth, and the letters were all still there.

SAMUEL J MAYFIELD.

She stood at the rail with the tag in her fist and saw the debt for the first time whole. Violette had gone to Torgau because Violette had a good arm, and Ruth had stayed because Ruth had a bad one. A Norwegian woman had stood in front of her at a selection. A Polish woman at a counter had seen her shut mouth and looked away on purpose. A clerk's mistake had let Corrie out, and Betsie had died thanking God for fleas. Ruth could find no reason in any of it that pointed to her. Women had died in her place, women whose names she would never learn, and there was nobody left to pay. A verse of the Psalms came up in her without her leave: *None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him.*

She was twenty-one years old. If she lived to be a hundred, she could not work off a debt that size.

The ferry's horn sounded for Malmö. Ruth wrapped the tag in the strip of camp cloth, closed her right fist on both and went below, and she did not open that hand again for two days.
