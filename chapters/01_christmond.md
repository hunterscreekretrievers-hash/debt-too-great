# 1. Christmond

*The Vellmer homeplace on the Pedernales River, below Martinsburg, Texas. Thursday, December 24, 2026.*

The attic window in the old hired hands' cabin is two panes wide and one pane tall, and on Christmas Eve my two youngest grandchildren had their noses flat against it, watching the eastern sky for a sleigh.

Ben was seven and a believer. Lily was twelve and had quit believing two Christmases back, but she had decided on her own, without telling a soul, that her little brother would get one more Christmas of it, and she had climbed into that attic to see that he got it. A quilt hung around both their shoulders like a tent. From the foot of the attic stairs a man could see the soles of their socks, the backs of their two heads, and past their heads, filling the whole window, the moon.

Eighty years in Texas, a wife and both parents buried in its ground, and never a moon like that one. The moon came up over the cedar breaks east of the river as big as a wagon wheel and the color of new butter, and as it climbed it went white. The almanac people call it a supermoon, meaning it had swung as close to the earth as it would come all year, and it rode in as the biggest full moon of 2026. The old German settlers in these hills had a better name for the December moon. They called it *Christmond*, the Christmas Moon.

That word came to the Mayfield house in San Antonio in a Christmas card in 1996, the last time the moon was full on Christmas Eve. The card was plain, the kind sold in a box of twenty at the drugstore, and inside, in a tall slanted hand pressed hard into the paper, it said: *Go outside tonight and look at the moon. The old people here called it Christmond. R. V.* Gloria and I stood in our backyard on Hackberry Street in our bathrobes and looked, because when Miss Ruth Vellmer told a Mayfield to do a thing, the Mayfields had a long habit of doing it. This year the Naval Observatory had the moon full on December 23 at 7:28 Central time, so it stood one night past full on Christmas Eve, and no eye on the Pedernales could have told the difference. By a Texas clock, the next full moon on Christmas Eve comes in 2102. I would be a hundred and fifty-six that Christmas, and the Lord has not consulted me on the matter.

An ice storm had blown in out of the Panhandle on Monday night and glazed every living thing in Pedernales County. On Tuesday afternoon a limb off the big live oak by the cattle guard gave way under the weight and took the power line with it, and the co-op had trucks out on every road between Johnson City and Stonewall, and by Christmas Eve none of those trucks had found us. The big stone house Fritz Vellmer built in 1908 stood dark across the yard with its electric heat dead in the walls. So the six of us had moved into the cabin, the cedar-and-limestone place Fritz built for his hired hands, with a stone fireplace that could warm a room, a kerosene lamp on the mantel, a cedar tree cut out of the break and stood up in a bucket of creek rocks, and enough split oak stacked on the porch to see us through to New Year's.

The tree had no lights, because every wire on the place was dead. Lily and Ben had made paper chains for it out of a spiral notebook and a jar of paste, and the chains drooped off one side so that the whole tree leaned east, as if it were watching for the sleigh too. Marcus said it looked like a Charlie Brown tree. He meant it as an insult. Ben took the Charlie Brown name as the highest compliment ever paid a tree.

By nightfall on Christmas Eve the last of the cloud had cleared off, and behind it lay a sky scoured clean and cold enough to crack. From the attic window you could count the fence posts for a quarter mile. Every strand of wire on every fence line wore a skin of ice, and under that moon the strands looked like lines drawn across the pasture with a silver pen. The round bales in the north pasture lay dark on the white ground like a herd of buffalo bedded down for the night. The tin roof of the cedar-post barn shone like a skillet. Down in the bottom, the pecans along the Pedernales stood black and still, every limb cased in ice, and below them the river ran dark and smoked in the cold. The cedar breaks on the far ridge had bowed under the glaze the way old men bow their heads in church, and here and there a tree had split, showing white wood in the moonlight like a bone through skin.

"You could read a newspaper by it," Lily said.

"Nobody reads newspapers," said Marcus, from the floor downstairs.

Marcus was fifteen. He lay on his back by the front window with his phone held over his face, hunting one bar of signal like a man trying to catch rain in a teacup, and a battery pack the size of a brick rode on his chest. At supper he had told us he was at nineteen percent and the battery pack at forty, and he had called out the figures every hour since, the way a leadsman on a riverboat calls out the depth.

My daughter Ruthie stood in the kitchen doorway in her socks, holding a cup of coffee she had boiled on the fire in an old enamel pot. Ruthie is forty-six and named for the woman who owned that pot. Her husband, Daniel, had gone to sleep in the recliner at a quarter past eight with his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and his mouth open, and nothing short of Gabriel's horn was going to move him before morning.

The top button of my grey cardigan was fastened, as it had been all evening, and two fingers went to it, and to the chain under it, out of a twelve-year habit.

"Grandpa," Ben said, without turning his head. "Does Santa need the moon to see?"

"He's managed on cloudy nights."

"But it helps."

"Most likely it helps."

Ben chewed on that answer. His breath fogged a circle on the glass, and he wiped it with his sleeve and fogged it again. Then he froze all over, the way a bird dog locks up on point in tall grass.

"There," he said. "Lily, there."

A light was crossing the sky above the barn, small and sure, moving south to north, slower and steadier than any airplane.

"That's a plane, Ben," Lily said.

"Planes blink. That one isn't blinking."

"That's a satellite, son."

Ben watched the light cross the whole width of the window and did not argue. He was working a problem through behind his eyes. "Santa could have a satellite," he said.

Lily looked down through the hatch. She had her mother's eyes, which were her grandmother's eyes, and she was asking for something with them. "He could have one," she said. "Couldn't he, Grandpa?"

"The man's been in business a long time. I expect he's upgraded."

Ben nodded, satisfied, and shifted his knees on the attic boards for a better angle on the satellite, and his heel came down hard on something in the dark corner behind him.

"Ow."

"What'd you kick?" Lily said.

"A box."

The attic roof in that cabin comes down low on the north side, and the moonlight through the little window fell across the floor in a long white rectangle that ran clear to the corner. At the far end of the light, where the rafters met the floor, sat a wooden footlocker.

That footlocker had been carried up those stairs in January of 2014, eighteen days after the funeral, on the shoulder of a sixty-seven-year-old man who had just come from a lawyer's office on West Main in Martinsburg with one sealed envelope in his coat pocket and one small chain in his fist. The man set the footlocker down in that corner, laid a feed sack over it, and went back down the stairs. Twelve years he had owned it. Twelve years the lid had stayed shut. A man can be brave about a great many things in eighty years and still be a coward about a box.

Lily pulled the quilt off her shoulders and crawled across the boards. Ben came behind her on his hands and knees. The mice had chewed the feed sack to lace, and Lily lifted it off in one piece and laid it aside, and the two of them knelt on either side of the footlocker the way the shepherds kneel in a nativity scene, the way the shepherds knelt in the one Miss Ruth kept on her porch on Elm Street. That porch comes later, and it gets its due before daylight.

The footlocker stood as high as a man's knee and a little over three feet long. The box had tin corners, a rope handle at each end and two brass hasps furred with verdigris, and somebody had painted it olive drab a long time ago, and the paint had faded to the color of a dry creek bed. Across the front, in white letters stenciled through a cut card and still sharp after eighty-five years, ran a name:

R. M. VELLMER

Ben read it the way he reads cereal boxes, one letter at a time. "R. M. Vel... Vell-mer."

"Vellmer," Lily said. "That's Miss Ruth. Like on her statue, on the bench." She looked down through the hatch again, and her voice went careful. "Grandpa, is this hers?"

"Yes, ma'am."

Down on the floor by the front window, Marcus quit hunting for signal. He typed the name into his phone with both thumbs, R-U-T-H V-E-L-L-M-E-R, and held the phone up to the glass to wait on the one bar.

"Why is Miss Ruth's box in our attic?"

"Because Miss Ruth left it to me."

"What's in it?"

The honest answer to that question was a short one, and an old man owes children the honest answer. "Twelve years, and I never once lifted the lid."

Lily stared. A twelve-year-old girl who has opened every present under every tree since she could crawl cannot imagine a box that stays shut for twelve days, much less twelve years. Ben had his hand on the lid already.

A grandfather could have told those children to put the feed sack back and come down and go to bed. The moon filled that little window, one night past full and as close as it would come in that grandfather's lifetime, and Ben's face, turned up to the glass, was round as the moon itself and silvered by its light, and the words came out on their own. "You can open it. Careful."

Fritz Vellmer built those attic stairs around 1910 for hired hands who were young and limber, and they are steep enough to be a ladder with ambitions. My knees held a meeting on every step. Halfway up, the knees adjourned, and from there, head and shoulders through the hatch and elbows on the attic floor, a man could see everything.

The attic smelled of cedar rafters, dust and mice. The cold bled straight through the window glass and stung the face like creek water. The moonlight lay across the footlocker like a hand.

The hasps were stiff. Lily worked at the first with her thumbnail until I slid a closed pocketknife across the boards to her and told her to use the back of it. The first hasp gave, then the second, and she put both hands under the lip and lifted, and the lid came up with a crack like an old man's knuckle.

The smell rose first: cedar, camphor from mothballs gone to powder, old paper, and under all of it something familiar that took a second to name. Beneath the camphor lay the inside of a hymnal at St. Paul Lutheran on a cold Sunday morning, the same breath of paper and cedar that had come off Miss Ruth's navy dress the day she sat in the back row at a college graduation in San Antonio in 1968.

A white card was pinned inside the lid with two brass thumbtacks. Lily turned the lid toward the window so the moon fell on it. The handwriting on the card was the same tall slanted hand as the Christmas card from 1996, the letters pressed hard by somebody with a strong grip and no time for foolishness.

"For Isaiah," Lily read, and stopped. "Grandpa. It's got your name."

"Your name and nobody else's."

"Why does it have your name?"

"Get the things out first. Then we'll talk. Mind how you handle them."

Ben went first, because Ben always goes first. He reached in and came up with a folded square of white cloth and shook it open, and it became a cap, a small white cotton cap with a band, the kind that pins to the hair. He put it on his head. The cap came down over his ears.

"I'm a chef," he said.

"You're a nurse," said Lily.

"I'm a chef nurse."

Lily went in next, slower. She came up with a bundle of letters tied with a piece of grey string, the envelopes yellow and soft at the corners. She tilted the top envelope into the moonlight and frowned.

"These aren't in English."

"Can you make out the writing?"

"Like German. Some of it." She squinted. "This one's got a little hat on the e. Is that French?"

"Most likely."

She set them on the floor beside her, squared up, the way she squares her schoolbooks. Then she reached in again and drew out a photograph mounted on grey cardboard, bent at one corner, and held it up to the window. From the hatch a man could just make it out: six children lined up on the porch of a stone house, squinting into the sun, the tallest boy holding a baby on his hip.

Lily looked at the photograph. Then she looked out the window, across the frozen yard, at the dark stone house Fritz Vellmer built, with its long porch and its four square posts and the moon on its tin roof. Then her eyes came back to the photograph.

"Grandpa," she said, very quietly. "That's the big house. That's the porch, right over there."

"That's the very porch."

She said nothing more. She laid the photograph face up on top of the letters.

The next thing took both hands to lift: a black Bible with the spine split and mended with yellowed tape, swollen to half again its proper size with clippings and folded papers pushed between the pages. Lily carried it against her chest the way a farm wife carries a basket of eggs across an icy yard.

Under the Bible lay more: a dog-eared paperback, bundles tied with string, and something folded in tissue paper, square and green and gold. Nobody touched any of it yet.

"That's enough for the attic," I said from the hatch. "Bring it down to the fire. All of it. Two trips, and nobody runs on these stairs."

"Can I wear the hat?" Ben said.

The answer should have been no. "Yes," I told him anyway.

I backed down those stairs one step at a time, and the children followed with their arms full, Lily first, with the Bible against her chest and the letters and the photograph balanced on top. Marcus had rolled up off the floor to watch. A page had loaded on his phone while the children talked in the attic, and whatever it said had put a crease between his eyes, and for the first time that evening the phone rode in his back pocket instead of in front of his face. He stood at the foot of the stairs looking up at his sister with his mouth a little open.

"What is all that?"

"Go up and bring the footlocker. Bring it whole. That box weighs more than you'd guess from the size of it."

He went up two steps at a time, the way a boy can at fifteen, and grunted at the lift, and came back down sideways with the footlocker on his shoulder, one hand on the rope handle and one flat on the lid, his face red, careful of every step. He carried that box the way twelve boys in green-and-gold jackets once carried Ruth Vellmer down the aisle of St. Paul Lutheran, though he did not know that yet, and the stairs were no place to tell him.

In the kitchen doorway, Ruthie had set her coffee cup on the counter.

The quilt went down on the floor in front of the fire, a Lone Star quilt that came in the mail from Martinsburg the week Ruthie was born in 1980, with nothing in the box but the quilt and a card that said *For the namesake*. Marcus set the footlocker at the edge of it. Lily laid the Bible and the letters and the photograph in the middle, side by side, as you would set a table for company. Fritz Vellmer's cowhide chair by the fire took my weight, and the hair on the hide was cold against the backs of my hands. Across the back of the left one runs a puckered white seam, put there by a mortar fragment on a rice-paddy dike outside Cu Chi in 1969, and a cold night still finds that scar first.

Daniel snored. The fire popped and threw a spark onto the hearthstone, and the spark died there.

Ben came down last, holding the rail with both hands. He walked into the firelight wearing a cap that had not been on a human head since the war.
