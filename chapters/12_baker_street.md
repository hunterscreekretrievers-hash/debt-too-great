# 12. Baker Street

*London, April to June 1944*

The guns in Hyde Park started at half past ten.

Ruth had been in London four nights, in a requisitioned flat off Marylebone High Street with a gas ring, a cold-water tap and blackout curtains so old the light came through the moth holes in pinpricks. Tonight she stood at the window in her nightgown with the curtain pulled back an inch and watched the searchlights swing across the clouds over the park, long white fingers feeling for something in the dark, and the anti-aircraft shells bursting beneath them in little red stars, and she counted the seconds between the flash and the sound the way she had counted thunder on the Pedernales.

A bomb came down somewhere to the southeast, toward the river. She felt it in the floorboards before she heard it, a shudder like a horse kicking the side of a stall, and the window glass buzzed in its frame. Down in the street a woman was laughing, and somebody else was shouting at her to get to the shelter, for God's sake, and the woman went on laughing as if she had heard the best joke of the war.

The Londoners called it the Baby Blitz. Ruth had seen one bomb site already, on her walk to Baker Street the morning before, a row of brick houses with the front sheared off one of them so that she could look straight into a bedroom on the second floor, where a brass bedstead stood made up with a pink chenille spread and a wardrobe door hung open on one hinge with a man's suit still on its hanger. A warden picked through the rubble below with a long pole. A dog sat on the curb beside him and watched the bricks, waiting for a voice from under them.

She let the curtain fall. The guns went on until two.

---

The office of F Section had no sign on the door. Ruth walked past the building twice before she found the right entrance, on Baker Street, a short walk from the address where the British claimed Sherlock Holmes had lived, a joke that amused Londoners more than it amused her. **[VERIFY: F Section at Norgeby House, 83 Baker Street, and its distance from the Holmes address]** Inside, the corridors smelled of floor wax and cigarette smoke and wet wool, girls in FANY uniform carried folders past her without a glance, and behind every closed door a typewriter was clattering. Two of them on the stairs were laughing over the Lysander that had gone to fetch a Miss Nearne home from France that month with *Jacqueline MUST come* chalked along its fuselage.

Vera Atkins smoked through the whole interview.

She was a tall woman in a dark tailored suit with her hair pulled back plainly, perhaps thirty-five, with a long, fine-boned face and eyes that rested on a person a fraction past the point of good manners. She did not offer her hand. She read Ruth's file while Ruth stood, and then she closed it and lit a fresh cigarette from the end of the last one.

"Sit down, Miss Vellmer."

Ruth sat.

"Your Americans have lent you to us," Vera said. Her English was precise and clipped, with something underneath it that was not English at all. "We share offices these days, your people and ours, and we share agents, and we share the losses. Your German is very good. Your French?"

"School French, ma'am. Enough to buy bread."

"We shall see about the bread." Vera tapped ash into a saucer. "You are going to the south. The Drôme, east of the Rhône, between the river and the mountains. Lavender, walnuts, goats and Protestants, Italians from November of 1942, and since last September a good many Germans."

She said it the way a doctor names the part of the body that will be cut.

"Why a German speaker, ma'am? In France?"

"Because the Germans in the Drôme talk to one another in cafés and on railway platforms and in the backs of lorries, and they believe no French girl understands a word. You will understand every word. Your cover is the other reason." Vera turned a page. "Forget Anna Weber. The Americans' practice girls are always from Colmar. Your papers will say Lucie Brenner, born in Mulhouse. When the Germans annexed Alsace in 1940, they put thousands of Alsatians out on the roads with a suitcase each, and a great many of those people drifted south. **[VERIFY: Germans' expulsion of Alsatians to the unoccupied zone, 1940]** A girl from Mulhouse living in the Drôme speaks French with German in it. So do you. Her accent is her alibi. Your French will be the weak thing; I won't pretend otherwise. Someone in the field will mend it."

"Yes, ma'am."

Vera opened the file again and ran one finger down a page. "Born January 1924. That makes you twenty. The Navy, I see, has you a year older." She did not smile. "Unmarried. Parents living, Texas. Is there a man?"

Ruth looked at the saucer full of ash.

"A boy from home. He flies."

"For whom?"

"The Army Air Forces, ma'am. He'll be in Italy by summer."

"Does he know your whereabouts?"

"No."

"Will anyone tell him?"

"Not from me."

Vera Atkins looked at her through the smoke and then wrote something in the margin in small, upright letters.

"The circuit you will join is called STONECUTTER," she said. **[VERIFY: confirm no real SOE F Section circuit used the name STONECUTTER]** "It works the hills south of the Vercors, round the Protestant villages, and it has been short a courier since March. Southern France is run from Algiers now, so you will train here, fly to Algiers in June and go in from there by parachute. Your field name will be Lucie as well. Couriers carry enough without two names."

"Lucie," Ruth said.

"Saint Lucy. The old pictures show her carrying her own eyes on a dish." Vera drew on her cigarette. "I find that appropriate for a courier. You will see nothing, Lucie, and know nothing, and carry everything."

---

Baker Street took her clothes away on the second Monday.

A tailor in a back room down the corridor, a refugee from Prague with pins in his mouth and a tape measure around his neck, measured her for a skirt, two blouses, a cardigan and a grey wool coat cut the way the tailors of the south of France cut them, with the buttonholes worked by hand and the label of a shop in Valence sewn in the collar. The tailor chalked the pattern for the coat on a London paper spread across his table, and while he worked, Ruth read the story under his chalk upside down: General Patton at the opening of a club for American soldiers in a Cheshire town, telling the crowd it was the destiny of the British and the Americans to rule the world, with the Russians left out of the sentence. Around his pins the man from Prague said the general had a whole army waiting in Kent and an oil dock going up at Dover, though nobody he knew had laid eyes on one of its tanks. He handed her shoes resoled with French nails, a handbag, a comb, a powder compact and a pencil stub, all French, all used.

"Everything American," he said, holding out a cardboard box. "In this box."

She put in her wristwatch from Honolulu, her Navy hospital badge, a lipstick bought on Hotel Street, and last, the bundle of letters tied in Lena's fiddle string.

Down the corridor a girl from the Dutch section was telling the stores desk that the Germans in Holland had wired London on the first of April, in plain English. The wire complained that Baker Street had lately tried to do business in Holland without them after such long and successful cooperation, and it promised the next agents London sent the same care as every agent before them. One Dutch wireless man in German hands had left out his security checks two years before and worked the letters of CAUGHT into his messages, the girl said, and London had paid it no mind. Ruth waited for the laugh at the end of the joke, and the laugh never came from either girl.

That night Ruth asked for the bundle back until morning, and the girl at the stores desk gave her a long look and handed it over without a word.

In the flat off Marylebone High Street, with the blackout drawn, Ruth untied the string on the bed under the bare bulb and laid the letters out across the blanket in order, from the first one in pencil from the East Side of San Antonio in the fall of 1940 to the last one, from Michigan, in February, in a hand that had learned to write in a moving airplane. Forty-one letters lay on the blanket. She read the first and the last and the one from Moton Field, *I flew one of those*, and then she tied them up again in Lena's own knot, and in the morning at eight she gave them back to the stores girl, who wrote VELLMER on a card and LUCIE under it and dropped the bundle in the box.

---

Ruth met Violette Szabo at the pistol range.

The range lay in a basement in the West End, a long low room with whitewashed brick walls and a bank of sandbags at the far end and the smell of cordite soaked so deep in the brick that no scrubbing would ever lift it. **[VERIFY: SOE pistol practice in a central London basement, or move the scene to a range outside the city]** Ruth came down the stairs on a wet afternoon in May and found one other woman already there, a small dark-haired girl in a pullover and slacks with her hair tied back in a scarf, firing a pocket automatic at a paper target with fierce delight.

The girl fired six rounds, fast. The range sergeant walked down and brought the target back, and the six holes sat in a group Ruth could have covered with the palm of her hand, every one of them in the paper man's chest.

"Not bad, Mrs. Szabo," the sergeant said.

"Not bad? Sergeant, that is very good, and you know it." She had a London voice, south London, with something French at the edges. She turned and saw Ruth on the stairs and grinned. "Are you next? Don't let him rattle you. He's a pussycat."

They shot side by side for an hour. Violette was the better shot, and she knew it, and she was not the least bit modest about it, and Ruth liked her for that more than for anything else. Afterward they sat on the bottom stair with their ears ringing and shared a cigarette Violette produced from somewhere, and she talked.

She had just come back from France. She did not say where, and Ruth did not ask, but Violette had been in Paris on the way home for an afternoon, and she had bought dresses. She described them one by one with her hands: a black one, very simple, with a cut here; a blue one for her little girl.

"You have a little girl?"

"Tania. She's almost two." Violette's face changed when she said it, and then went still again. "She thinks her mummy works in an office and goes away on business, which is true, isn't it?"

"Her father?"

Violette looked at the cigarette.

"Étienne was a Legionnaire. Free French. He was killed in the desert, at El Alamein, October of '42. Étienne never once laid eyes on Tania." She said the facts in order, the way Vera Atkins said facts. "I married him after a few weeks, if you can believe it. Everyone said I was mad."

"Everyone would say I was mad too," Ruth said, "if I ever told anyone."

Violette turned her head and studied her with frank interest, the way a woman studies a hat in a shop window.

"There's a man, then."

"A boy from home. He flies fighters."

"Alive?"

"In Michigan, by his last letter. On a boat to Italy by now, or there already."

"Keep him alive, then." Violette stood and brushed off the seat of her slacks. "Write him the most frightfully boring letters. Weather. Food. Tell him about the ration queues. Men like that, they want to know you're in a queue somewhere being bored." She held out her hand and pulled Ruth up off the stair. "They take me back in two or three weeks, by my guess. Perhaps we'll pass each other in some doorway."

She went up the stairs two at a time, small and quick, and at the top she turned and waved, and the scarf came loose from her hair, and she caught it in the air and laughed.

Ruth saw her one more time, at the end of May, at Orchard Court in Portman Square, where F Section briefed its agents in a borrowed flat with a doorman who knew every agent by field name and a bathroom tiled entirely in black. **[VERIFY: Orchard Court flat, the doorman Park, and the black-tiled bathroom used as a waiting room]** Violette sat on the edge of the black bathtub with her coat on, waiting her turn to go in to Vera, reading a letter. She looked up and saw Ruth in the doorway and lifted two fingers, the way a girl lifts a hand across a dance floor. Then the doorman said her name, and she folded the letter into her pocket and went through the door.

---

At four in the morning on the sixth of June, Ruth woke to a sound she had never heard: a low, steady roar overhead that went on and on and would not stop, wave after wave of airplanes, more than anyone could count, passing toward the Channel in the dark. She went to the window. The sky above the rooftops was full of the noise and of nothing in sight. Down in the street the people of Marylebone stood on their front steps in their nightclothes with their faces turned to the sky.

Nobody spoke. An old man two doors down had taken off his hat.

---

Sam's letter came on the twentieth of June, a V-mail in the small photographed hand the Army used to save shipping space, forwarded from the box in Washington in an OSS pouch.

She read it sitting on the bed with her back against the wall and her knees drawn to her chin.

> *Italy*
> *June 9, 1944*
>
> *Ruth,*
>
> *The ship put us off at Naples in the middle of a city knocked flat, and the mountain behind it had blown its top in March, so the ash was still grey on the grapevines like flour on a baker's arm. A crew chief on the dock told us the same ash wrecked better than seventy of our B-25s on a field at the foot of the mountain. A truck took us over the mountains to the other sea. The field is a farm with steel matting laid on it for a runway, tents in an olive grove, a farmhouse for the colonel, and the Adriatic so blue at the end of the strip it looks painted.*
>
> *I am in the 100th Fighter Squadron. Lucius is in the 301st, across the field, and comes over to lose at cards.*
>
> *We fly the P-47 Thunderbolt, which is seven tons of airplane and climbs like a cow but dives like the wrath of God. We take the bombers up north and bring them home. The bomber boys are white, and most of them don't know who's flying beside them, and some of them don't care to learn the truth. So the crew chiefs are painting our tails red, the whole tail, bright as a fire truck, so every bomber crew in Italy will know the 332nd brought them home. They say Mustangs come next month.*
>
> *When I get one of my own I'm painting a name on her nose. I already know the name. It's WINDMILL GIRL, and every man in this squadron can guess till the war ends, and none of them will get it.*
>
> *I have four missions now. On the second one a German came up under a bomber and I put him off it, and when we landed the colonel said, "Good work, Mayfield," and walked on, and I stood on the wing like a fool for a quarter hour.*
>
> *Every night I think about the board, the hostess, the sergeant, the cap rolling down the steps. Mostly I think about the stairwell.*
>
> *Wherever you are, Ruth, watch the sky. I'll be the red tail.*
>
> *S.*

**[VERIFY: V-mail from an APO in Italy to a Washington box, then by pouch to London, in about eleven days]**

She read it three times. Then she took out a sheet of the cheap French notepaper the tailor had given her for her cover and put it back, and took out a sheet of English paper she was supposed to have surrendered, and wrote Sam the most frightfully boring letter of her life.

*Sam, it rained on the slates all night. The eggs here come as a yellow powder that has never met a hen. In the queue on Marylebone High Street a woman ahead of me bought the last two oranges in London and gave one to a boy.*

She wrote nothing about Baker Street, nothing about Algiers, nothing about France. At the bottom, after a long time, she wrote one line and left it standing.

*I am watching the sky.*

At first light she posted it to the APO number at the corner box.

---

> *Saturday, June 24, 1944. London.*
>
> *Tomorrow they take this book. Nothing in English goes where I am going, and nothing from Texas.*
>
> *Sam's letters are in a box at Baker Street with my name and my other name on a card. His last one goes in with them in the morning. I have read it twelve times. He is painting a name on an airplane, and nobody in Italy will guess it, and the one person who would is going where no letter can follow her.*
>
> *Violette went in after D-Day. Nobody says where.*
>
> *I have not prayed since January. I notice it the way a person notices a clock has stopped, only when the room goes quiet.*
>
> *The rest of this book I will write when I come back for it.*

---

On the morning of the twenty-fifth Ruth carried the diary in its waxed canvas cover and the V-mail from Italy up the stairs at Baker Street to the stores desk.

The girl untied the bundle of letters for her without being asked. Ruth set the V-mail on top of the stack, tied the fiddle string over it herself in Lena's knot, and laid the diary beside the bundle. The girl wrote the date on the card under VELLMER and LUCIE, and carried the box back through a door into a room with a locked steel cabinet against the wall, and Ruth heard the cabinet drawer close.

That night a car took her to an airfield in the country, and an airplane took her south, over the Bay of Biscay and down the coast of Portugal in the dark, toward Africa. She sat on a canvas seat in the cold with a bag of French clothes between her feet and no word of English on her anywhere, and she kept her eyes on the little round window. Once, over the sea, the cloud broke under a thin crescent moon, and she pressed her forehead to the glass and watched the sky, the way he had asked, a sky with nothing in it but stars.
