# 25. Kenji

*Comfort, Texas, June and July 2013*

The man at the feed store in Comfort did not look up from his ticket pad.

"Mr. Morita?" he said. "Out the Sisterdale road four miles, left at the cattle guard with the boot on the post, and keep going till you think you've gone too far. Then go a little farther. If he's not at the house, he's down at the creek. Honk twice. He don't like a man to walk up on him."

"I'm not a man," Ruth said.

He looked up then. "No ma'am," he said. "You sure aren't one."

The boot was a cowboy boot with the toe worn through, nailed upside down to a cedar post. Past the cattle guard the road dwindled to two caliche ruts with grass whisking the bottom of Kate's Camry, and twice Ruth had to ask Kate the name of the town.

The house sat in a pocket of live oaks above Cypress Creek, a little limestone farmhouse with a tin roof gone the color of a dove's back. Racks of window screen stood on sawhorses in the yard with leaves, roots and flower heads drying in rows, and one long rack of something dark and wrinkled that Ruth took for prunes until she smelled salt on it: red seaweed, two hundred miles from any sea. A brown hound too old to get up lay under the porch and thumped his tail at Honey, twice, the way an old man lifts two fingers off the wheel.

Kate honked twice.

A small old man came up from the creek carrying a galvanized bucket, bent a little at the shoulders, in khaki work pants, a white undershirt and a sweat-stained straw hat. His face was brown and creased like a pecan shell, and his eyes were black and very bright behind steel-rimmed glasses.

He looked longest at Honey.

"A good dog," he said. "She watches the right person." He wiped his hand on his pants and offered it. "Kenji Morita."

Ruth took the hand, dry and strong, and held it a second too long.

"Ruth Vellmer," she said.

"Come in out of the sun, Miss Vellmer."

---

Kenji sat Ruth on a kitchen chair by the screen door, where the light came in flat and white, and pulled another chair so close their knees almost touched. Kate stood against the counter with her arms folded.

He did not ask her what was wrong. He took a brass-handled magnifying glass and a penlight from his shirt pocket and said, "Look at the corner of the ceiling, please. There. Don't move your eyes."

She looked at a water stain shaped like Australia. He leaned close, smelling of soap and fresh ginger. The penlight came across her right eye from the side, and she felt him looking past her face into the grey of the eye itself, the way her father used to study a split face of limestone to find the rift for the next break.

"You have good eyes," he said. "Grey. A northern eye." He moved the light. "Other eye, please."

He bent over the left eye longer still. "Here," he said, and pointed with his little finger, low in the eye, near seven o'clock. "A dark ring, all the way around the pupil, like rust inside a pipe. Your digestion. The bowel. Very toxic. Here also, and here, little dark marks, like specks of pepper, in the stomach zone. You have parasites, Miss Vellmer. A long time. Many years."

"What kind of parasites?" Ruth said.

"A threadworm." He said the word the way another man might say *dandelions.* "Very small. It goes into the body through bare feet in dirty ground, and it never leaves, because its eggs hatch inside you and the young worms go back into you, year after year. A person can carry it sixty years. English soldiers who were prisoners of the Japanese came home with it, and the doctors in Liverpool found it still in them thirty and forty years later." He did not smile at the word *Japanese*, and neither did she. "The worm makes poisons, and the poisons go in the blood, and the blood goes to the brain. You feel this as a fog. You cannot find your car. Yes?"

Ruth said nothing. Then, "Yes."

"Where were you in a war?" he said.

The question came so quietly, and in the same voice as the worms, that she nearly answered it.

He nodded as if she had answered him. "A mark sits at the outer edge of the iris also," he said. "Very old. The body was starved once. Badly, and barefoot, in bad ground. Did a doctor give you a shot of cortisone? For the hip?"

"Twice," Kate said. "This spring."

"Cortisone puts the body's guard to sleep, and this worm waits for exactly that. The worm multiplies, and it can kill an old woman." He held out his hand. "Now show me the pills."

---

Kate had brought the pills in a grocery sack. He lined the eleven bottles up on his table like dominoes and read every label through the bottom half of his bifocals.

"This one is for the hip," he said. "This one is for the dizziness that comes from the hip pill. This one is for the bladder. This one, the bladder pill, makes the mind slow in an old person. The doctors know this. The fact is printed in their own books." He picked up the donepezil. "This one is for the mind the other pill makes slow." He looked at Ruth. "You do not stop these all at one time. Stop some of them at once, and your heart races, or you have a seizure." He touched the white oval. "This one you keep. Your heart needs it. This one, the blood pressure, you keep for now. The rest we take away one at a time, slowly, like taking stones off a wall from the top down, so the wall does not fall."

At the bottom of the sack lay the red-and-white aspirin bottle from Ruth's pocketbook, and she set it at the end of the row herself without a word.

He tore a sheet of butcher paper off the roll and drew a seven-week calendar on it with a carpenter's pencil. The bladder pill went first, the dizziness pill the second week, the donepezil the third, and the low-mood pill came down by halves across four weeks, because a sudden stop would make her cry for no reason and think she was going crazy.

"You should tell your doctor," he said.

"My doctor doesn't look at me."

"Then tell him anyway, on paper, and keep a copy of everything."

"I always keep copies," Ruth said. "A long time ago I knew an office on Baker Street that lost seven files in every eight after the war, to a fire and to clerks."

She folded the butcher paper in quarters and put it in her pocketbook. The Bayer bottle stayed on his table. From that day on she bought the store brand.

---

The cure came in a quart Mason jar, a paper sack and a list.

Kenji made it at the counter the way her mother had made lye soap in an iron pot, with no measuring cups and no hurry. Into the jar went the red seaweed off the drying rack, broken small. "Makuri," he said. "In the days of the shoguns every Japanese mother gave it to her new baby, and after the war the schools in Japan still gave it to the children for worms. In 1953 two scientists in Japan found the medicine inside it and named it kainic acid, after kaininsō, the seaweed's other name." Into the sack went a fist of grey-green leaves from the ditch by his gate. "In Lima my mother called this paico. Here the Mexican ladies call it epazote. In the mountains of Peru the mothers give it to children for worms. It grows wild in bar ditches all over Texas, and nobody looks at it."

"The seaweed, one spoon boiled in a cup of water, every morning before breakfast," he said. "The paico, a tea after supper. Roasted pumpkin seeds, a handful a day. Every morning one umeboshi, the sour plum. At noon, raw ginger, grated, and a green leaf of shiso." He pointed at the ruffled purple-green shiso along his porch. "Why does a Japanese man eat wasabi and pink ginger and a shiso leaf with his raw fish? For the worms in the fish. In a laboratory in Japan they put the fish worm in a dish with the juice of each one, and the worm quit moving. The grandmothers knew it first." He held up a finger. "No sugar. Sugar feeds them. Two weeks taking it. One week of rest. Two weeks again. The eggs hatch in the rest week, and the second round kills the new worms."

He looked at Kate. "You are a nurse."

"Forty-nine years."

"The worm has a medicine also. Ivermectin. A Japanese scientist named Ōmura found it in a spoonful of dirt beside a golf course. They give it to horses and cattle by the tube, and to people in Africa by the hundreds of millions for the river blindness, and the government in Washington prints this very worm on its label. No doctor gave it to her, because no doctor looked for the worm. A blood test will find it. Ask for the test by name. Strongyloides." He wrote the word on the back of a feed-store receipt in capital letters, in case Kate's pride would not let her ask him to spell it.

Kate folded the receipt into her blouse pocket without a thank-you. In early August, Dr. Miriam Castellanos, who had worked beside Kate at the Robert B. Green for twenty years, ran the test, looked at the number that came back, and wrote the prescription before Kate could ask for it.

"Where did you learn all this?" Kate said.

He wiped the counter with a rag. "My mother knew it," he said. "Her mother knew it in Japan, and the paico she learned in Peru. Behind a fence in South Texas my two sisters had the bloody flux one summer, and the camp doctor had nothing left in his bag, and my mother made this from things growing along the wire. Both my sisters lived. I was eight years old. I learned from watching her hands."

"What camp?" Kate said.

"Crystal City," Ruth said.

They both looked at her.

"Down below Uvalde," she said. "In the spinach country. Where they have the statue of Popeye." She looked at the old man. "They had German families there too, in the war."

"Yes," he said. "The Germans on one side. We on the other. The fence went all the way around everybody. I was born in Lima, Peru, in 1935. My father had a little store that sold bicycles and sewing machines. In 1943 the Peruvian police came for him, because the Americans gave them a list. They put him on a ship. Then they put my mother and my sisters and me on a ship to be with him, because they said a family should be together. In New Orleans they took our passports. Then they told us we were in the United States illegally, because we had no passports." He smiled with one corner of his mouth. "The paper first. Then the fence."

Ruth thought of a stapled stack of paper on her kitchen table with a notary's seal on every page.

"How long were you there?" she said.

"Until 1948, three years after the war. The Americans traded some of the families to Japan for American civilians. Peru would not take us back, and Japan we had never seen, so the Americans kept us. My father went to Seabrook Farms in New Jersey and stood on a line packing frozen vegetables until he died. Nobody ever said they were sorry. In 1988 the Congress voted the Japanese Americans twenty thousand dollars each. We were not Japanese Americans. We were from Peru. Ten years later they sent us five thousand and a letter." He shook his head. "My father was already dead."

Honey came over from the doorway and lay down across Ruth's feet.

"I was behind a fence too," Ruth said.

She did not say where. He did not ask again. He put the Mason jar and the paico in the grocery sack with the pill bottles and refused Kate's money. He took a pound cake from Ruth the next week instead, and a jar of her peach preserves, which he said were too sweet, and ate half the jar standing at the counter.

---

The first week Ruth was sick.

She sat in the bathroom on Elm Street with Honey across the threshold while her stomach cramped and turned like a wet rope being wrung, and Kate stood in the hall with her hands over her mouth.

"Mama, I'm calling somebody."

"He said this would happen."

"He's a man with a garden."

"He's a man whose mother kept her children alive in a camp." Ruth leaned her forehead on her hand. "Go make me some oatmeal and put those pumpkin seeds in it."

Kate went and made the oatmeal.

On the fifth day the cramps stopped. On the eighth Ruth woke at half past four, the carrier's hour, with no clock to call her, and lay listening until a cardinal started up in the pecan by the window, and she knew what time it was, and what day, and that her glasses lay on her Bible on the nightstand, because she remembered putting them there.

She lay still, the way a person lies still when a long hurt finally stops, for fear of starting it again.

That night Ruth knelt by the bed and said the prayer in her mother's German, every word in its place, as far as *Dein Wille geschehe*, and there she stopped. The stop was her own again, and Ruth nearly laughed on her knees. *This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope.* The pills had taken the words, and an old man who had been a boy behind the wire at Crystal City had given them back, seventy-one years after a Japanese pilot flew low enough over the hospital lawn for her to see his helmet. "You sent him," Ruth said to the Lord. "You sent the one I would have turned away at the door."

Pastor Thalmann had preached the Samaritan the Sunday after her nose was broken, and Ruth had heard only His silence. Seventy-six years later the Samaritan had come up from a creek outside Comfort with a galvanized bucket in his hand.

---

By the first of July the kitchen floor had stopped sliding. On a Saturday morning she walked heel to toe across the linoleum and back, the way a deputy makes a drunk walk the white line, and the floor did not move under her once. The blue door of the pill box marked SAT held three pills, and she knew the name of every one.

On the Fourth of July she drove herself to Comfort with Honey in the passenger seat and found the boot on the post the first time. Kenji came up from the creek and looked into her eyes on the porch for a long time.

"The ring is lighter," he said. He held a hand mirror up for her and moved the light, and she could see nothing in her own grey eye but her own grey eye. "Not gone. Next month, lighter again."

"I can't see it."

"You don't have to see it. You can find your car."

She laughed out loud on his porch, and the old hound under the steps thumped his tail.

Inside, the red-and-white Bayer bottle still stood at the end of his kitchen table where she had set it down in June. Ruth picked it up and turned it in her long fingers.

"Bayer," she said. "My mother gave us Bayer for fever in 1930. In 1925 Bayer went into a cartel with BASF and Hoechst and called it I.G. Farben. In the war Farben built a rubber plant at Auschwitz and worked it with men from the camp until they dropped, and a company in which Farben held a big share sold the SS its gas."

Kenji laid the hand mirror face down on the table.

"One of the Farben directors, Fritz ter Meer, got seven years at Nuremberg and was out in two. In 1956 Bayer put him at the head of its supervisory board. Standard Oil of New Jersey went partners with Farben in 1929, and when our Justice Department made Standard give up the German patents in 1942, a senator from Missouri named Truman said the word out loud. Treason."

Kenji looked at the bottle and did not tell her she was wrong. In June, in the passenger seat of Kate's Camry, she had twice asked Kate the name of the town.

"You said the doctors print the facts in their own books," Ruth said. "The first sulfa drug came out of the Bayer laboratory, and Hitler's government made the man who found it refuse his Nobel Prize. When the war ended, our own government brought sixteen hundred German scientists over here and gave them offices. So when a young man in San Antonio adds a new pill every time I mention the last one, I know who wrote the first chapters of his book."

In the fall of 1944 she had watched the girls the camp called rabbits limp across the yard on legs SS doctors had cut open and packed with glass and dirt to try their sulfa on the wounds. Nobody ever had to tell Ruth Vellmer that the men who made medicine had also made the gas.

---

The strength came back behind the mind, slower, the way water comes back into a dry creek after a long drought, first in the low places and then, one rain at a time, up the banks.

In the second week of July she drove out to the homeplace alone for the first time since Christmas, walked down the pasture to the pecan bottom with Honey ranging ahead through the bitterweed, and walked back up without once stopping for breath.

Then she climbed into the loft of her father's barn.

The rungs were cedar, hand-split, worn smooth as a saddle horn in the middle where seventy years of boots had stepped, and she went up slowly with Honey whining at the bottom. At the top she sat in the open hay door with her legs hanging over the drop, eighty-nine years old, and looked out over the pecan bottom to the Pedernales shining brown and low between the trees, and the long white scar of the new caliche road Clayton Rusk's surveyors had cut along her east fence, on the Moellering side of the wire.

She looked at the road a long time. A pair of red-tailed hawks wheeled over the pecans on the hot air, around and around without once moving a wing, the way a fighter plane circles a field before the wheels touch the grass.

Then she climbed down, every rung, and drove to Eric Rogers's office on Main Street.

"Miss Vellmer." He looked at her twice. "You look like somebody else."

"I climbed into my daddy's hayloft this morning," she said. "When is that hearing?"

"Tuesday, the twentieth of August."

"Good," Ruth said. "I want to testify."
