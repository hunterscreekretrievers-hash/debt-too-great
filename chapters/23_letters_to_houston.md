# 23. Letters to Houston

*Martinsburg and Houston, April 2011 to December 2013*

The shoebox came to Hackberry Street in the spring of 2014. A man from Houston with a yellow Lab riding the front seat of his pickup set it on Gloria's kitchen table and said Miss Ruth would want her letters kept with the rest of her. Inside lay twenty-six envelopes in a tall slanted hand, every one postmarked Martinsburg.

His side of the correspondence was already upstairs in her trunk, held with a black binder clip. No e-mail ever left Miss Ruth's house. The dog man wrote to her at two and three in the morning and printed the pages and mailed them, because she had told him on her porch that a letter a woman cannot hold is a rumor.

Marcus turned the clip over in his fingers and asked nothing.

---

The oldest pages under the binder clip were written before the dog man ever laid eyes on her. On February 27, 2011, he had poured himself out in a letter to a man who had mentored him since he was sixteen. The second time he sat with Miss Ruth at the Mustang Grill, he printed that letter and slid it across the table, because she had asked him what a Christian does when a preacher sues him. She read it through twice without touching her cup.

> *Now with the legal battle with this "Pastor/Director" of the movie I find myself having these debates with God about the validity of Christianity that must sound similar to so many people's arguments right before they throw in the towel altogether on the faith.*
>
> *A few weeks ago I saw an interesting biography about Sam Kinison who was known for his profanity-laced bashing of the church... For years secretly he had been giving to little churches across the country that he felt were authentic.*
>
> *I know the TRUTH that the thief comes to steal, kill and destroy... GOD IS A GOOD GOD, AND HE'S GOOD ALL THE TIME!*
>
> *Maybe that's the whole point, until we're dead and in heaven with our Lord there should be some nagging sense of lonely?*

The "Pastor" in that letter was the actor from the old dinner theater on Loop 1604. In 1988 the actor turned the closed theater into a church and filled it with three thousand people. Kate Hartmann sat in its pews for several years in the 1990s, sang its songs, dropped her check in its plate, and never knew a thing. In the spring of 2003 the pastor told his congregation he was taking a sabbatical for his health. That summer the man who had taken his pulpit preached a sermon on truth and hypocrisy and told the congregation the church had ended his pastorate over an unspecified sin, and the tape of that one sermon never went out on the table with the others. The San Antonio paper carried the story.

The dog man knew none of it when he shook the pastor's hand, any more than Kate knew it in her pew, and he learned it only after he asked Eric Rogers to subpoena every news outlet in San Antonio. Back from KSAT, the San Antonio news channel, came a report its Defenders team had aired years earlier. The station had received a letter signed *Lied to and Betrayed*, and with it a copy of the severance agreement between the church and its founder.

On San Antonio's KSAT news channel, the agreement said he had committed adultery twice and was no longer qualified to be a pastor. The agreement listed more than six hundred thousand dollars in questionable transactions that went to him and his company: money market funds, cameras, computers, a Ford Expedition. The pastor could keep every dollar if he left the church, got counseling, started no church nearby and kept quiet.

On camera the pastor said, "I'm ashamed of my sin." The senior pastor who followed him sent the station a fax saying the embezzlement accusations were false and there was no story. Then the pastor and his wife told the station the agreement was misleading, that independent audits showed his "total financial credibility," and that power-hungry church leaders had forged the agreement, which the couple signed to end the turmoil.

The copy in her trunk carries one pencil line, pressed hard, under the sentence about the little churches. Nothing is written in the margin. Across a booth at the Mustang Grill, when he told Miss Ruth what came back from San Antonio, she set her cup on its saucer and said, "My Katie sat under that man for years." Then Miss Ruth asked him one question. "Forging a contract is a felony in Texas. If his elders forged that agreement, why did he put his own name on it, and where are the charges?" Before he left the booth she wrote a book, a chapter and a verse on a paper napkin, *Matthew 7:15*, and slid it across the table. He kept the napkin folded in his Bible.

---

Ruth gave him her first counsel face to face, on her porch swing, on the evening of April 11, 2011. The dog man had a deposition in San Antonio the next morning, in the lawsuit over *Return to the Hiding Place*. A director named Peter had sued him for three and a quarter million dollars, and his former partner, Amanda, would sue him herself that summer. He had written to his sister that week that he was "like kindling and easily provoked." He told Miss Ruth the same thing in fewer words. She heard him out with Honey's head on her shoe.

"Answer the question they ask," she said, "and not one word more. A silence is a hook with no bait. Let the lawyer be the one to bite. Look at his hands, never his face. If you need a minute, ask for a glass of water, and drink it slow."

He asked her where she had learned that.

"A long time ago, men better at questions than anybody in San Antonio had me in a chair." She stood up, which meant the visit had ended. "They wanted me afraid of the quiet in that room. The quiet was the only thing in that room that belonged to me."

Two days later a note came under her door on motel stationery:

> *Wasn't as bad as I expected. I remained much more calm than mediation! Attorney said no major hits so I came home at peace for most part.*

Miss Ruth kept it. The fold lines have gone furry from a hundred openings.

---

Easter fell on April 24 that year. Two days later he sent her four printed pages titled WHAT IS FORGIVENESS? He had been sitting with the seventeenth chapter of Luke.

> *Yet stuck right there in the middle of the verse is "IF HE REPENTS."*
>
> *Does God forgive without repentance?*
>
> *Forgiveness is offered but not given by God to the unrepentant, is it?*
>
> *Can God really be a God of Grace if he's not a God of justice? If there is no justice for evil then surely there can be no grace or mercy for the repentant heart?*

He asked her about Naboth, the Bible story his preachers seldom touched: *"he did exactly what God commanded of him and he was put to death by his enemy. 1 Kings 21."* He asked about a widow in a newspaper advice column, betrayed by a husband already in the ground: *"Do the two emotions live side by side like roommates who don't speak or is there some kind of uneasy truce offered every day between forgiveness and the spear of anger for the utter pain of betrayal that lingers?"*

He signed it *In God's Grip of Grace, J.*, and under that, *Confessions of a Confused Christian.*

Her answer took a week.

> *You have read Luke right. "If thy brother trespass against thee, rebuke him; and if he repent, forgive him." Our Lord put the if there Himself. Preachers who leave it out are kinder than Jesus, and nobody gave them that job.*
>
> *Forgiveness is for the man who comes down the aisle. The rest belongs to the Judge.*
>
> *Naboth kept his vineyard and lost his life, and God did not explain Himself to Naboth either. Keep reading. Chapter 22, then Second Kings 9. The dogs licked Ahab's blood and the dogs ate Jezebel. God keeps His books longer than we keep ours.*
>
> *Your widow asks whether anger and forgiveness can live in one house. Mine has held both since 1944, one in each room, and the two have never spoken. Once a year, at Christmas, anger and forgiveness eat at the same table.*
>
> *R. V.*

He read the line about 1944 a dozen times, he told me in my kitchen, and never once asked her about that year.

---

The night before he left, he took her to supper at the steak house in the old stone building down Main Street. The steaks cost about what a calf brought at the sale barn in 1960. She ate every bite. When the check came she had it out of his hand before he saw the total and paid it in cash from her pocketbook. "A woman who carried the mail thirty-nine years can buy a man a steak," she said. After the funeral he learned that the same woman could have bought the steak house, the sale barn and the bank across the street.

He left the county on Saturday, June 25, 2011. Her first letter reached his box in Houston ahead of his truck.

On June 26 he wrote from the road:

> *I had a Divine Encounter in Utopia, Texas, last night... Truly God has directed my path.*

Her answer was three lines on a postcard of the Sabinal River: *The golf picture Robert Duvall made at Utopia opens in September. My sister Anna lies in the cemetery there. Next time you pass, tell her Ruth said the peaches made a short crop.*

After he left, the letters from Houston came up the Elm Street walk in a dog's mouth. Every day but Sunday, at a quarter past eleven, the carrier's little right-hand-drive Jeep stopped at the box by the gate, and Honey was already sitting on the bricks beside the post with her tail sweeping the walk. By the second week the carrier quit putting the bundle in the box and handed it down through her window instead. Honey carried it to the porch in her soft mouth, with the light grip a good retriever saves for a bird, and laid it on the swing beside Ruth's hip. Ruth turned over every envelope the way she had once checked her own sorting case before daylight: the corners, the flap, the stamp. Not one envelope ever came up with a bent corner, though every stamp came up a little damp, the way a dove comes back in a good dog's mouth.

"I ran a route out of this post office before your mama was born," Ruth told the carrier through the Jeep window, "and I never once had help that good."

In July he slept a week at Lyle's place while he moved the last of his things out of town, and Lyle and his wife fed him their homemade smoked sausage, the best in the county, at every meal. He wrote Lyle a thank-you letter, and Lyle, who did not know what a man does with such a thing, showed it to Miss Ruth at the Mustang Grill:

> *For those of faith, this life is merely proving ground for character as God tests our willingness to trust him through the trials... it was refreshing to find a Christian that actually lives it out in life and it's not just a "fish symbol" on the back of their SUV.*

"He's right about you," Ruth said, and Lyle went red to the ears and paid for her pie.

---

That September he was fighting a stack of city tickets in Houston on top of three groundless lawfare lawsuits, strategically filed to harass him and to quiet him from blowing the whistle on all the issues with the movie. The tickets came to fifty-five thousand dollars in fines. The city wrote him for illegal storage of an appliance, and the appliance was his barbecue pit. The city wrote him for no screen on a window. The city wrote him for stagnant water on a day it was raining. The city wrote him for failing to keep a cooling system able to hold the inside of his house twenty degrees cooler than the air outside, on a day when the high in Houston was sixty degrees. The city wanted forty degrees in his living room. One batch of those tickets came with a name written in the margin, the name of the woman financing all the lawsuits, and the police had sent that batch to the wrong address. A lawyer explained it to him: "This is standard practice. They write the tickets, put them in an envelope to an address that doesn't exist, and when you don't show up for court, they can arrest you for failure to appear."

He wrote her the angriest letter in the binder clip:

> *The reason tyranny and injustice exist is because NO ONE wants to fight...the question I keep asking myself is how much do I believe what I say I believe regarding "right" and "wrong"...*
>
> *It's much more a mental, moral and spiritual battle for me as opposed to just a financial battle of them taking everything I own.*

She wrote back on a single sheet:

> *The city wanted forty degrees in a man's living room on a sixty-degree day.*
>
> *Now tell me what that woman's name was doing in the margin of a police ticket.*
>
> *I learned in Germany the purpose of that kind of paper. In November 1938, after the mobs smashed the windows of thousands of Jewish shops in one night, Hermann Göring signed a decree that fined the Jews one billion Reichsmarks as 'atonement,' made them pay to repair the damage done to them, and handed the state the insurance money owed for their own broken glass. The law made the victim the criminal, and the victim's property paid the fine. That same year the Labor Front gave the Jewish clothing merchants of one German district two weeks to sell. The merchant who balked was threatened with the Gestapo, the Labor Front man meant to buy one shop himself, and the price was a song. When the police take their orders from the rich, a ticket book is a crowbar.*
>
> *I have seen what happens in a place where everybody waits for somebody else to fight. I have also seen one village where nobody waited. The second kind of place is rarer, and it is the only kind worth a life.*
>
> *Fight it. Fight it in daylight, keep every receipt, and never let them make you lie, because the day you lie, they walk off with the only thing they ever wanted from you.*
>
> *A courtroom is a room, and its honesty walks in the door with the people. In 1942 a Berlin court sentenced a Hamburg boy of seventeen, Helmuth Hübener, to death for typing the BBC news on a church typewriter. He told the judges he had committed no crime, and they went by the book, because Hitler's men had written the book first. Until 1967 the law of this state made a marriage across the color line a felony, and our judges read it with a straight face too.*

---

In the middle of October an envelope came from Martinsburg with no letter in it, only a clipping from the paper. Newt Gingrich had spoken in Washington that month, and Miss Ruth had underlined two of his sentences in pencil:

> *"The Founding Fathers deeply distrusted judges and thought that the lawyer class was dangerous and that you could not give them unbridled power or they would undermine and destroy free society."*
>
> *"Read the Declaration of Independence. A very large number of its specific charges against Great Britain involve dictatorial judges."*

Across the top margin of the clipping she had copied one line of the Declaration in her own hand, in the same place the city had written a woman's name on his ticket:

> *He has made Judges dependent on his Will alone, for the tenure of their offices, and the amount and payment of their salaries. R. V.*

He taped the clipping inside the lid of the box where he kept his tickets.

---

On October 26, 2011, his forty-ninth birthday, a card came with a sprig of dried lavender tucked inside the fold.

> *Happy birthday. Honey is well. She brings me my reading glasses before I know I have lost them. She sleeps across my bedroom door and will not be moved. I have not slept so well since Roosevelt.*
>
> *R. V.*

He kept the lavender in his truck visor until it went to dust.

---

On Wednesday, December 7, 2011, the city dismissed the tickets, and that same night a verdict came in the mail in a dog case from Galveston. He wrote to her before bed:

> *Wednesday all was resolved on the tickets with the city! There were 103 dismissed, 7 dismissed with the community service.*
>
> *All in all, it was a week of complete celebration!!! So much weight off my shoulders it's hard to comprehend.*

Her Christmas card came a week later.

> *December 7 has been a bad day in my life for seventy years. You have given it back to me with a ribbon on it. I did not know a man could do that.*
>
> *Christmond blessings. R. V.*

---

His furniture, his files and twenty years of field-trial ribbons stayed behind in Unit 14 at Miss Tipton's storage place on the east end of town.

In March 2012, when he called to pay the year's rent, Miss Tipton told him not to bother. The unit was paid a year ahead, through the following March. She would not say by whom.

He never asked Miss Ruth. She never told him. The receipt turned up in her trunk, folded small inside her checkbook register: one year's rent on Unit 14, paid in cash, March 2012.

---

In April 2012 the other side's paper grew teeth. Adam had eight witness affidavits drawn up, as alike as eight stamps off one sheet, and every one of them notarized by Peter's own assistant. The dog man read them, cited the witness-tampering statute in the Texas Penal Code, and took the eight affidavits before a judge. On the night of April 19, before he sat down with Eric to get ready for the hearing, the dog man wrote to Miss Ruth:

> *Just pray I get good advice from Eric tomorrow. I'm planning on looking the lion in the face and attacking... I've already started making notes for my hearing with him, so if you'll pray for the judge the hearing with [Adam], if the judge grants my subpoenas could be so huge!!!*

Her answer came on a postcard of Pedernales Falls, in a hand pressed hard enough to dent the card.

> *"My God hath sent his angel, and hath shut the lions' mouths, that they have not hurt me." Daniel 6:22. Daniel never once attacked a lion. He kept his window open toward Jerusalem and prayed three times a day, as he always had, and he let the king's own law do the rest. Pray first, then look the lion in the face. R. V.*

When the hearing came, the king's own law did the rest. The judge tore into Adam from the bench.

---

For his fiftieth birthday, October 26, 2012, she mailed a card with no picture on it, only a verse copied out in her own hand.

> *"But they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint." Isaiah 40:31.*
>
> *A boy I knew once loved this verse. He wanted to fly more than he wanted anything under God's sky. Fifty is young. Go fly.*
>
> *R. V.*

Ten weeks later, worn down by a long winter of hearings, he wrote to his sister and asked her to pray *"that I might mount up with wings like Eagles, run and not grow weary."* He never knew the verse had belonged to a boy from the Pedernales. The only woman alive who knew sat on a porch in Martinsburg with a yellow dog, and she kept the secret.

---

He came by the ten Booms honestly. He grew up on Corrie's story. His big sister went to Wheaton, became a Wycliffe Bible translator and married a Dutchman, and the two of them knew Hans Poley in Holland. The family lived around the corner from the After Dinner Players in Houston, the theater company of Jeannette Clift George, and as a boy he walked with his parents down the street and around the corner to their productions. Jeannette Clift George played Corrie in Billy Graham's 1975 picture *The Hiding Place*, and when it came out, the family went to see it in the theater. More than eleven million people saw that picture in its first three years, and the role brought her a Golden Globe nomination. When he was a young boy, Corrie ten Boom herself came to speak at his church, and he met her.

That history is why he rolled his finder's fee back into the production of *Return to the Hiding Place*, almost a quarter of a million dollars that could have done more good in his own life than in Peter's hands.

The dog man went to that set in Michigan in the summer of 2010 because Amanda asked him to go as her agent. Amanda never paid him for his time or bought him a ticket home to Houston, and the director's daughter kept switching off the key to his hotel room. On that set the dog man saw more than a padded budget: a script far too long for any theater, cameras rolling before the money was raised, and a family drawing salaries off other people's money. The line producer was not yet twenty and already had a handful of pictures behind her. Peter had none. When the dog man asked her how Peter had treated her, the line producer said, "I will *never* work with another Christian director, and I'm a Christian." The husband and wife who kept the production's books had their own line for Peter: "You stop lying about me, I'll stop telling the truth about you!" The dog man wrote down everything he saw, the padded budget first, and sent it to Amanda before the first lawsuit was ever filed. Amanda cast her lot with Peter anyway, and Peter's San Antonio lawyer, Adam, sued the dog man for three and a quarter million dollars over one email. His reward was three groundless lawsuits, a host of false accusations, and fifteen years in court.

Eric Rogers took the movie case on contingency. The dog man fought the other two pro se, because he could not afford a lawyer. He had kept his end of every bargain. The "Christians" he had his agreements with buried him in litigation and never paid him the almost quarter of a million dollars they owed him, and he ended up losing his house in Houston. Losing that house was one of the reasons he came to Martinsburg, and Martinsburg brought him to Eric Rogers and to the front steps where Miss Ruth fell. Paul put it in Romans 8:28: *And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose.*

Years after the house was gone, in December of 2017, Adam put his clients' best offer in writing. Peter and Amanda were prepared, Adam wrote, to make "one, and one only, attempt to settle this case": three thousand five hundred dollars for any and all claims against all of them, with no admission of liability. Adam called it "a very generous and one time offer." Peter and Amanda still owed the dog man almost a quarter of a million dollars.

Eric Rogers read every page the other side ever filed against him. "I've never seen anything like this," Eric told him. "They want you dead or in jail."

The other side had put its plan in writing long before any judge saw it. In the late summer of 2010, two weeks after the dog man reported the padded budget, Peter wrote that if the dog man was busy defending himself, he would "not have time or money to move on any kind of offense." That email stayed out of the dog man's hands for two years. On March 14, 2017, the director's own lawyer put one thing in writing: Amanda and Peter had no "connection to one another other than the failed movie project that was completed over 7 years ago." Their own lawyer said it last.

An old man sums up those fifteen years the way Joseph summed up the pit and the prison and the brothers who sold him: *Ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good.*

Back in December 2012, the dog man had written to her about lying. That year he was trying to buy the book rights from the children of Hans Poley.

> *Or even like Hans Poley, had the question about lying...still would like to know what passages he was referring to in the Bible. A friend reminded me of Rahab.*
>
> *It's more bearing false witness that God finds as an abomination, that's the betrayal that wounds so deeply, maybe because at that point people actually do step into the role of Satan, the accuser.*

At that moment thirty-odd years of Corrie ten Boom's letters lay in Miss Ruth's trunk, airmail onionskin under Dutch stamps, and she did not tell him so. She sent him a list.

> *Rahab, Joshua 2. Hebrews 11:31 puts her in the same chapter with Abraham. The Hebrew midwives, Exodus 1:19 and 20. They lied to Pharaoh's face, and the Bible says plainly that God dealt well with them.*
>
> *Read the commandment slow. "Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour." The word against carries the whole commandment. That house in Haarlem was hiding people from men who meant to kill them. The man at the door with a gun asking where the Jews are has stepped out of the word neighbour by his own choosing.*
>
> *I have lied to men with guns. I would do it again tomorrow, and I have never once asked God to forgive me for it, and He has never once raised the subject.*

---

In January 2013 the lawsuits came to a mediation in the Hill Country, and he drove back up Highway 290 and slept in Lyle's bunkhouse. On the twentieth he wrote to his lawyer that he wanted *"an ending I can live with without regret."*

On the Saturday afternoon before the mediation he went to Elm Street. Miss Ruth met him at the door in her coat in a warm house. She called him by the wrong name twice. Eleven pill bottles stood in a row on the kitchen windowsill like soldiers at roll call, and Honey would not leave her side for a minute.

He sat on the porch swing beside her and told her about a Japanese man out the Sisterdale road near Comfort, a man who read eyes and mixed herbs and had brought sick people back to their feet after the doctors gave up on them. Miss Ruth said she had doctors.

The case settled on Monday, the twenty-first. The next day he wrote her:

> *At this point my perspective is God delivered me from the lions' den; but we know what happened to the accusers of Daniel who wished him dead.*

Her answer is the only letter in the shoebox where the hand wanders. The lines slope off toward the corner of the page. One word is written twice.

> *Daniel 6:24. ". . . the lions had the mastery of them, and brake all their bones in pieces or ever they came at the bottom of the den."*
>
> *I am glad you are out of the den. I am tired today. The doctor in San Antonio says that is my age. I have been this age before, and it did not feel like this.*

---

The next letter in the box is postmarked August 2013, and the hand is the old hand again, tall and straight as the name on a mailbox.

> *You were right about the Japanese man. Kate drove me to Comfort in June. He looked in my eyes for a long time and then he took away nine of my eleven bottles and gave me a jar of Japanese seaweed and a sack of ditch weed, and Kate's doctor found the very worm the old man had named at his kitchen table. I can read the newspaper again. I can read the stock pages again, which is more important.*
>
> *I owe you a debt. Do not argue with me about it.*

On his birthday that October she wrote:

> *I ride a school bus on Friday nights with thirty-four boys who smell like a feedlot. Honey has graded every one of them. She has not been wrong once.*
>
> *Happy birthday. You gave an old woman a season she never expected in this life.*
>
> *R. V.*

---

The last envelope in the shoebox is a Christmas card postmarked Martinsburg, Monday, December 23, 2013. The card reached Houston on Friday the twenty-seventh, the day after the dog man heard what had happened on her porch.

The picture on the front is a manger. Inside, in the hand every family on her old route would have known:

> *Somebody broke my manger this month. I carved a new Child out of pecan. He came out a little crooked, which seems right to me.*
>
> *I have owed a debt since 1945 that I will never pay, and I have spent my whole life paying on it. This year I have begun to think the Lord was never sending me a bill.*
>
> *Kiss the new puppies for me. Merry Christmas.*
>
> *Ruth*

She signed her whole first name only once, on the last card.

He drove up Highway 290 the next morning and climbed the steps of the Mustang bus at half past nine to see about her dog.

"Did he ever answer her?" Lily said.

"There was nobody left at the address," the old man said. "So he came instead."
