# 19. Hammer and Chisel

*Martinsburg and San Antonio, January 1961 to June 1967*

Ruth found the clipping in the hymnal rack of the last pew at St. Paul on a Sunday in September 1961.

Somebody had left a thin religious magazine folded behind the hymnals, the kind a visiting preacher hands out by the door to people who never open it. The congregation was standing for the Creed, which Ruth had not said once in five years of sitting in that pew, and she pulled the magazine out to have something to do with her hands.

The column was on page nine, under the name A. W. Tozer. She read it standing up while the people around her said *I believe in the Holy Ghost*.

The man wrote that God was seldom as gentle as the popular teachers painted Him. He wrote that "a sculptor does not use a manicure set" to turn rough marble into a thing of beauty. He wrote that the saw and the hammer and the chisel were cruel tools, and that the stone stayed shapeless without them. At the foot of the page the editors had boxed another line of his: *It is doubtful whether God can bless a man greatly until He has hurt him deeply.*

Ruth read the paragraph twice more. She stayed on her feet after the congregation sat, until Kate, nineteen and home from nursing school, tugged at her skirt. During the sermon she tore the column out along the fold a little at a time, so the sound would not carry.

That night she sat at the kitchen table with the clipping flat under the lamp and a fountain pen in her hand. She underlined the line about the manicure set. She underlined the hammer and the chisel. In the margin, in small square capitals, she wrote one word.

RAVENSBRÜCK.

She knew what the preachers would want her to take from the clipping: that the camp had been God's chisel, and that the Ruth Vellmer who came out was finer stone than the girl the Germans took in the Drôme. She had heard that sermon on the radio out of Dallas for five years, preached by men who had spent the war selling insurance.

She thought about Betsie ten Boom's hands. By December 1944 Betsie's fingers had gone so thin that the knuckles stood out like knots in a rope, and she had still used those fingers to pick lice from other women's hair. Betsie had died in the camp hospital, her body yellow and small as a child's, and Ruth had seen her afterward through the washroom glass among the others, naked, laid out like stovewood for the cart. If the camp was the hammer, then the hammer had broken the finest stone in that place in half.

"You used it on her," Ruth said out loud to the empty kitchen. "She was already finished. She was finer than anything You'll ever make of me, and You used it on her anyway."

The refrigerator hummed. Nothing answered her.

She did not throw the clipping in the fire. She folded it once and slid it into the back of her King James, behind the newspaper story about Elisabeth Elliot and Rachel Saint, and she put the Bible on her nightstand, where it stayed the rest of her life.

In May of 1963 she read in the same magazine that A. W. Tozer had died in Toronto. She did not cut out the notice. She took the Bible off the nightstand and opened it to the back and read the clipping again, and the underlining had not moved.

---

In October of 1961, at thirty-seven, Ruth paid cash for flying lessons at the county airstrip, a runway of caliche and a tin hangar off the Kerrville highway. She drove out on Sunday afternoons in the green Ford she had bought used from a widow on her route, the radio tuned to the AM station out of Martinsburg. The instructor, who had taught Army cadets in the war, told her after the third lesson that this was not her first time at the controls. She told him she had watched a good deal.

By the next summer she had her license and a used Piper J-3 Cub, painted the same yellow as the little airplane Sam had left for her in the coffee can at the line fence in 1940. The town decided that flying was an odd hobby for a mail carrier and left it at that.

Every Sunday afternoon after that, weather permitting, Miss Ruth flew alone north to Enchanted Rock. She circled the pink granite dome three times, low enough to see the lichen, over the spot where a thirteen-year-old boy with a balsa wing in his overalls had told her, *One day I'm going to fly one of those, Ruth. You watch.* Then she waggled her wings and flew home to feed the cattle.

---

On the twentieth of January, 1961, when the tall man from Johnson City took the oath as Vice President on the radio in Lena's Sunday house, Fritz said one thing to Ruth, in German, from his chair: "That one still wants something."

That year the shoebox took in two voices on paper, and neither one came from a pulpit.

On the seventeenth of January, 1961, three days before he handed the country to a young senator from Boston, President Eisenhower, the general who had run her war and stopped Patton at Pilsen, went on the air from the White House to say goodbye and warned the country about the machine he had built to win it. *In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex.* Eisenhower warned that public policy *could itself become the captive of a scientific-technological elite.* Ruth wrote both sentences on the back of a feed-store receipt before the announcer returned.

On the twenty-seventh of April the new President spoke to the newspaper publishers at the Waldorf-Astoria in New York, and Ruth pinned to the receipt the paragraph the San Antonio paper printed: *The very word "secrecy" is repugnant in a free and open society; and we are as a people inherently and historically opposed to secret societies, to secret oaths and to secret proceedings.*

Under both, in her small square capitals, she copied a line from the Masonic magazine that crossed her sorting case every month without an envelope, on its way to a lodge brother's box on her route: ROOSEVELT. HOLLAND LODGE NO. 8, NEW YORK, 1911. THIRTY-SECOND DEGREE, 1929.

Ruth had sworn her own oath in a room in London and kept every word of it, and she knew the weight of a secret oath.

In May of 1962 the San Antonio paper ran a photograph of the new cathedral at Coventry, raised beside the burned shell of the old one, and named its architect, a Scotsman called Basil Spence. Ruth laid the photograph in the shoebox without a word of pencil.

---

Grandma Hattie died in March of 1964, in the back bedroom of the frame house on the East Side, under the roof a Christmas card had put over her head.

Her grandson was eighteen that spring, a senior at Wheatley with a letter sweater and opinions, and not one notion that a grandmother could be anything but old.

Mount Zion Baptist was full to the walls that Saturday. The ladies of the missionary society wore white, the men wore every suit in the East Side, and the choir sang "Precious Lord" slow enough to break a mule's heart. Grandpa Joseph had been gone six years by then, so Daddy sat on the front pew beside Mama, and the casket stood in front of the pulpit under a spray of yellow roses, and Grandma lay in it in her good black dress with her reading glasses folded in her hands, because she had asked to be buried with them so she could read the Book of Life for herself.

At a funeral, halfway through the second hymn, a boy's eyes go wandering, and that is how the white lady came into the story.

She sat in the very last pew, by the door, alone. A tall woman about forty, in a plain navy dress and a navy hat with no veil, white gloves in her lap, her back straight as a plumb line. Every head in that church had turned when she came in, and every head had turned back again, because the East Side had a long habit of letting a white person's business stay a white person's business. She did not sing. She held the hymnal open in both hands and looked at the casket over the heads of three hundred colored people, and her face never moved.

At the cemetery afterward the white lady was gone. Nobody had seen her leave.

When the family went back to the grave on Monday to tend the flowers, somebody had been there first. A coffee can wrapped in brown paper sat at the head of the fresh dirt, packed full of bluebonnets, the deep blue Hill Country kind with white tips, the kind that don't grow wild on the East Side of San Antonio. No card. Daddy stood and looked at that coffee can a long time with his hat in his hand.

"Miss Ruth" was all he said.

From that day the name had a face.

Grandma left almost nothing behind, and most of it went to Daddy. One thing went to the grandson. In the drawer of her nightstand was a note in her careful pencil: *Sam's letters to Isaiah. He is the one who reads.* Under her bed, behind her winter shoes, sat a Red Goose shoebox tied shut with kitchen string, and in it were the letters her oldest son had written her every week of the war, from Tuskegee and Michigan and the troopship and Italy, more than a hundred of them, soft as cloth from handling.

The box went home to a closet in Daddy's house, and later to one on Hackberry Street, and the string stayed tied around it longer than a young man ought to admit. Eighteen-year-olds don't read their dead uncle's mail. They think there will be time.

---

After they buried her father at St. Paul in the fall of 1965, Ruth stayed by the stone he had cut for himself. For twenty-five years she had held Fritz Vellmer off over a thing he did in 1940, and he had died talking about the stock tank. Except for one night in her kitchen in 1961, she had not spoken a word to God of her own will since a sacristy in the Drôme. Standing by the new grave, Ruth saw that she had done to her Father in heaven exactly what she had done to the one in the ground: sat at His table every Sunday and refused to open her mouth. She thought of the elder son in Luke, angry in the yard, who would not go in to the feast, and of the father who came out to him. "I am not done being angry," Ruth said to the sky over the cedars. "I am done being quiet about it. I am sorry for the twenty-one years." Then she said the Lord's Prayer as far as *Dein Wille geschehe*, which was as far as she could go, and walked out the cemetery gate to Kate's Volkswagen.

---

In March of 1966 Ruth sold the last of the pickups that had followed the Willys on the route and bought a right-hand-drive Jeep Dispatcher, the model built for rural carriers, with the steering wheel on the curb side, so she could reach every box from the seat without leaning across or stepping down into the bar ditch. The same week a yellow Labrador pup, the first dog on the place since Liesl died the winter before, came to her out of the Comfort line that had given Kate her Schatzi, and Ruth named her Bess.

Bess was short for Betsie. Nobody in Pedernales County ever asked her the reason for the name.

By summer Bess rode the passenger side of the mail Jeep with her nose out the window and her ears turned inside out by the wind. She knew every dog on the route by name and every biter by his gate.

---

Kate Vellmer was headstrong from the time she could stand, and Ruth had done nothing to cure her of it.

By 1966 Kate was twenty-four and a registered nurse on the night shift in the emergency room at the Robert B. Green Hospital in San Antonio, where the charity cases came in, the stabbings and the car wrecks and the babies born in the backs of taxis. She drove home to the Pedernales every other weekend in a used Volkswagen and brought her notions with her, and that spring she brought a man.

Bill Hartmann was a Martinsburg boy, a pharmacist with a counter in a drugstore on Broadway in San Antonio, thin and freckled and slow-spoken, with big careful hands that could count out pills faster than a bank teller counts money. He sat at Ruth's kitchen table on a Saturday in April and ate two plates of sausage and sauerkraut and asked for her daughter.

"She's not mine to give," Ruth said. "Ask her."

"I asked her, ma'am. She said ask you."

Ruth looked at Kate across the table. Kate looked right back at her, chin up, the cowlick standing at the crown of her head like a flag on a fort.

"Then yes," Ruth said. "God help you."

Kate was home on the second Monday in May to talk about flowers, sitting cross-legged on the front-room floor with a bowl of popcorn and a pad of paper, when the evening news out of San Antonio ran a piece of film from Hanoi.

The North Vietnamese had filmed an interview with a captured American Navy pilot, a commander named Jeremiah Denton, and released it to the world to show how well they treated their prisoners. **[VERIFY: date the Denton interview footage first aired on U.S. television, May 1966]** The film was grainy. Denton sat under hot lights in a pressed prison uniform, thin, his face the cellar-white of a man who had not seen the sun in months. A voice off camera asked him questions in English, and he answered them slowly, saying that he supported his government.

"Poor man," Kate said. "Look at those tired eyes."

Ruth set her coffee cup down on the end table without looking at it.

Denton blinked as if the lights hurt his eyes, and any man watching in any living room in America would have thought exactly that. Ruth watched the eyes. One long blink. Then long, long, long. Then short, long, short.

She had learned Morse code in the winter of 1943 in the ballroom of the Congressional Country Club outside Washington, tapping it on a card table with a pencil until she dreamed in dots. She had not used it since the summer of 1944.

Her lips moved.

"T," she said. "O. R."

Kate looked up from the popcorn. "What?"

"T. U. R."

"Mama Ruth, what are you saying?"

"E," Ruth said.

The film ended. The anchorman came back and moved on to a story about a dam in Arizona.

Ruth sat with her hands flat on her knees. She was not seeing the Zenith anymore. She was seeing a white-tiled bathroom at 84 Avenue Foch, a bathtub with lion's-paw feet full of cold water, a man in a good grey suit on a stool beside it smoking, waiting for her to come up for air, and her own wrists tied behind her with cord.

"Torture," she said. "He spelled torture. With his eyes."

Kate stared at her. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because I read every letter of it."

"No. How do you know *Morse code*?"

Ruth rose. Her knees were not quite steady, and she kept one hand on the arm of the chair until they held.

"Girl Scouts," she said, and went out the back door.

Kate found her an hour later in the barn, sitting on an overturned bucket in the dark beside the cedar post where Fritz had carved his initials in 1908. Ruth was not crying. She was rubbing the bare third finger of her left hand with her thumb, back and forth, back and forth, over the knuckle.

"There weren't any Girl Scouts in Martinsburg in 1935," Kate said. "I asked Aunt Lena."

Ruth said nothing.

Kate sat down in the straw beside her, a nurse's way, close enough to touch and not touching. "What happened to you? In the war. Where were you?"

Somewhere down in the pecans a chuck-will's-widow started calling.

"That man," Ruth said at last, "is going to be in that place a long time. Nobody in this country is going to believe what he said, because he said it with his eyes, and they'll tell themselves he was just tired."

"I believe it."

"I know you believe it, Katie."

Ruth said nothing more. Kate asked again at Christmas, and again the next summer, and Ruth gave her the same nothing, gently, every time.

---

Kate and Bill were married at St. Paul on the fifteenth of October, 1966, on a blue Saturday with the pecans dropping in the churchyard. Fritz was a year in the ground under the stone he had cut himself, so Henry walked Kate down the aisle with his bad lineman's back held as straight as one of his own poles.

Kate had asked one thing. Ruth sat in the front pew on the left, the bride's mother's place, in a navy dress with a corsage of yellow roses pinned on it, the first time since 1941 she had sat anywhere in that church but the last pew. She sang the hymns. People noticed. Mrs. Grieshaber leaned forward from the sixth pew and whispered "Gott sei Dank" to Mrs. Lindauer in the fifth, and Ruth heard her, and kept singing.

At the reception in the parish hall, Kate found her by the punch bowl with a paper cup in her hand.

"Did you ever want this?" Kate said. "A church, and a dress, and everybody watching?"

Ruth did not answer at once. Kate had Walter's chin and Margaret's eyes, and the lace of Margaret's veil lay across her shoulders.

"I had mine," Ruth said. "Go dance with your husband."

---

The decision came down from Washington on a Monday in June of 1967, and the San Antonio paper had it on the front page Tuesday morning.

Ruth read it on the porch of the stone house in her work clothes, with her coffee going cold on the step beside her and the cicadas starting up in the live oaks. A Virginia couple named Loving, a white man and a colored woman, had been married in Washington, D.C., in 1958, and gone home to Virginia, and been dragged out of their own bed by the county sheriff in the middle of the night for the crime of being married to each other. Nine years later the Supreme Court of the United States had ruled, nine to nothing, that no state could forbid a marriage because of race. Every such law in the country fell with that one ruling, Virginia's and Texas's and the rest.

From the porch she could see the lavender along the south fence, one long purple row in full bloom, the only lavender in Pedernales County. Madeleine Chabrol had sent the seed from Valfrède in 1948, folded in a twist of paper inside a Christmas letter, and the neighbors had decided long ago that lavender was a German thing. Every June Ruth cut it with her mother's shears and hung it head-down in bundles from the barn rafters, the way the bundles had hung over a stone stall in the Drôme, until the whole barn smelled of that July.

Ruth set the paper down on the boards and went inside for the shoebox behind the winter quilts.

The photograph lay where it had lain for twenty-two years, on top of a stack of letters in pencil, turned to the cardboard.

Ruth carried it out to the porch. She sat down on the top step and turned it over in her long fingers, and the young man in the leather helmet grinned up at her with one hand flat on the nose of his airplane, the same grin that had started, barely, on top of Enchanted Rock in 1937, while she sat beside him in a cedar splint.

She propped the photograph against the coffee cup, face up, in the full sun, and she sat beside it on the step through the morning and the long noon, while the shade went off the porch and the cicadas climbed to a scream in the heat, and she did not move to put it back in the box.

Toward supper the shade came back across the porch, and Ruth picked up the photograph. Twenty-five years before, on a seawall in Honolulu, the first word she had sent toward the sky after Pearl Harbor had been *please*. She had never once sent the word that answers it. "Thank You," Ruth said to the sky over the lavender, and she carried Sam back into the house.
