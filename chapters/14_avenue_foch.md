# 14. Avenue Foch

*The Drôme to 84 Avenue Foch, Paris, August 1944*

The chair outside the Café du Pont sat tipped against its table, the way it always stood when the letter box was safe, and Ruth leaned the bicycle against the plane tree and saw the waiter's face through the glass a half second too late.

He was a thin old man named Bonnet who had served her lemonade four times in three weeks and called her *ma fille*. Now he stood behind the zinc with both hands flat on the bar and his eyes on the back room, and in his eyes she saw the whole thing at once, the way a person sees a rattler on a path before the mind has the word for it.

She reached into her coat pocket for the little round tin, thumbed it open, and put a lozenge in her mouth the way a girl does on a dusty road. What went into her mouth was the waxed-paper twist with Sam's tag in it. She worked it under her tongue and closed her teeth.

Two men came out of the back room. One wore a grey suit and a soft hat and the black beret of the Milice folded in his breast pocket. The other wore civilian clothes and held a pistol low against his leg as if it had grown there.

"Mademoiselle Lucie," said the one in the hat. "Good morning."

She ran. She got two steps toward the door. The man with the pistol caught her by the braid and swung her face into the doorframe, and her cheekbone split, and warm blood spilled into her collar. Out on the pavement the bicycle had gone over against the plane tree, and the eggs in her basket lay broken, the yolks running yellow through the wicker.

"Pardon," old Bonnet said, to nobody, over and over, his hands flat on the zinc. "Pardon. Pardon."

The two men searched her in the back room among the wine crates, rough and quick. They pulled the grips off the handlebars in the street and shook out the messages. They emptied the lozenge tin onto a crate and crushed every lozenge under a thumb. The man in the hat found the string around her neck and drew the silver band up out of her blouse and lifted it to the window light.

"Mariée?"

"My mother's," Ruth said in her Alsatian French. "She's dead."

He let the ring drop back against her breastbone. Nobody looked in her mouth. A girl with blood running out of her face is a girl nobody asks to say *ah*.

Somebody had talked. Ruth never learned who, and in sixty-nine years of nights she never once let herself guess out loud.

---

They drove her north up the Rhône valley in a black Citroën with her wrists cuffed in her lap and the grey coat London had cut for her thrown across her knees, sticky with egg. **[VERIFY: SOE agents arrested in the south in August 1944 sent on to the SD at 84 Avenue Foch]** At Lyon she spent a night in a cell that smelled of lye and old urine. In the dark she took the tag out of her mouth for the first time, peeled away the wet waxed paper and held the little rectangle of metal in her fist until morning. At dawn the tag went into the toe of her stocking, under the ball of her foot, where she could feel every letter when she walked.

The man in the hat leaned in at the window before the car for Paris left the curb.

"Paris has asked for you," he said. "They are curious about Lucie."

---

The house stood among chestnut trees on the widest avenue in Paris, a cream stone mansion with iron balconies and tall windows, built for a family with servants and carriages. Ruth saw a gravel court, a black car being washed by a man in shirtsleeves, and a sentry in field grey yawning by the door.

They walked her up five flights of stairs that began in marble and ended in bare wood. The rooms on the fifth floor had been servants' rooms under the mansard roof, and now they were cells with the bolts on the hall side of the doors. Hers held a cot with no mattress, a bucket and a window painted over except for one scratch the size of a thumbnail, through which she could see a chimney pot and a square of sky.

Ruth learned long afterward that in March a Frenchman named Pierre Brossolette had thrown himself from a window high in that house rather than give the Germans one word, and that one November night the autumn before, in an air raid, three prisoners had gone out through the skylights of that same top floor onto the roof. Two of the rooftop prisoners refused afterward to give the Germans their word not to run again, and neither one ever came home.

A matron in a smock searched her there, all of her, and her shoes, and the seams of her skirt. Ruth stood on the tag in her stocking and watched the wall. The woman turned out the pockets of the grey coat, found a handkerchief and a tram ticket from Valence, shook the coat once by the collar and threw it on the cot.

On the plaster beside the cot, people had written with pins and buttons and fingernails: names, dates, *Vive la France*, a mother's address in Lyon. Low near the floor, in small careful English capitals, somebody had scratched *THE LORD IS MY SHEPHERD* and stopped there, as if the guard had come in before *I shall not want*.

In a crack below the words, somebody had left the pin.

That night, by the grey light of the thumbnail scratch, Ruth bit open four stitches where the lining of the coat met the facing at the left front of the hem. She slid the tag deep into the fold. She pulled a thread from the frayed edge of the lining, and with the point of the pin she pushed the thread through the old holes one at a time and drew it tight and knotted it with her teeth, the way Katharina Vellmer had taught her to knot a hem at the kitchen table on the Pedernales, and made her take out every crooked stitch and do it again. When she finished, she could not find the place with her own thumb.

A searched coat was a finished coat. Nobody in that house would search it twice.

---

The first interrogation was polite.

On the landing below the cells an Englishman in shirtsleeves sat at a drawing board with no guard beside him, inking names onto a map of France with a fine pen, and the sentry passing behind him laughed at the Englishman's joke.

A man in a good brown suit received her on the floor below, in a room with a carved marble fireplace, a Persian rug and a vase of yellow roses on the desk. He introduced himself as Herr Wachholz, poured tea from a silver pot and complimented her German.

"Swabian? No. Older. Like a country pastor in 1850. Where did you get that German, Lucie?"

"Mulhouse."

"No," Wachholz said gently. "Not Mulhouse."

She drank the tea, because she had not eaten in two days and a body with sugar in it lasts longer. She told him the story London had built. He listened with his head tilted and wrote nothing.

"Two things only," he said. "The name of your wireless operator, and the American flyer."

Her cup did not move. She made sure of it. If the Germans had her wireless operator, his next message to Algiers would come through clean, without the small mistake London taught every operator to make on purpose, and Ruth thought of the Dutch girl at the stores desk.

"Our Luftwaffe saw a parachute come down over the Jabron in the last week of July," Wachholz said. "A fighter pilot. Our people in the valley say he is a Neger. A black man in those hills is a crow on snow, Lucie. Somebody is feeding him. You will only be confirming what is already in my file. Nobody is hurt by a confirmation."

"I don't know any American."

Wachholz sighed like a doctor whose patient has refused a shot, and rang a little brass bell. A man came in with his tunic off and his shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow, thick freckled forearms, a face like a ham.

"Scharführer Strelow has less patience than I," Wachholz said. "I'm sorry. I truly am sorry."

He took the yellow roses with him.

---

Strelow began with his hands.

He hit her open-palmed across the side of the head, left, then right, then left, the way a man settles a horse that has kicked, without anger and without hurry. Her head rang like a washtub under a ladle. The split on her cheekbone opened again, and the blood ran down her neck.

"Der Flieger."

She said nothing.

He stood her against the wall with her nose two inches from the plaster and her arms straight out in front of her. When the arms dropped, he hit her across the kidneys with a length of rubber hose. By the second hour her shoulders burned like the bottom of a skillet. By the third her legs shook so hard her heels drummed the floor, and urine trickled down the inside of her leg into her shoe, and Strelow looked at the puddle and said nothing about it, which was worse than any curse in German.

The guards let her sleep three hours, and then the bolt scraped again. They kept on that way, three hours and the wall, three hours and the wall, until she lost the days and counted only the scrape of the bolt.

On the second day two guards walked her down a hall to a white-tiled bathroom with a bathtub on lion's feet. The water in it was cold. Strelow tied her wrists behind her with cord, and a man in a good flannel suit sat down on a stool beside the tub and lit a cigarette, and the guards put her under until the cigarette was half gone, and lifted her, and put her under again.

On the third day, by her count, came the cigarettes.

Strelow smoked a cigarette down to a red coal, took her left wrist in his fist, turned the arm over on the desk and pressed the coal to the soft inside of her forearm, two inches below the elbow, and held it there. The skin hissed. The smell came up into her own nose, the smell of the burn ward at Pearl Harbor, of boys pulled out of burning oil, and she screamed, because screaming cost nothing. He lit another.

Somewhere in the second burn, words began moving in her without her leave. *Vater unser, der du bist im Himmel.* The words were her mother's German. Our Father which art in heaven. She had quit God in a sacristy that smelled of turnip greens, and she had meant it, and still the prayer came up out of her, a thing the body did on its own, like a calf going to the gate of the pen where it was born. She fought the words, and she lost, and she fought them again.

He made four burns in a neat row, like buttons on a shirt.

"Der Flieger."

"I don't know any American," she said through her teeth.

Afterward Strelow wiped his fingers on the first cloth to hand. The cloth was her grey coat, which lay over the chair where the guard had dropped it. Strelow scrubbed his knuckles on the collar and tossed the coat back at her, and the hem struck her knee, and she caught it, and she held it in her lap with her burned arm across it like a girl holding a cat.

Wachholz came back with the roses and dressed the burns himself with ointment from a tin, very gently, talking about Texas. He had read the cowboy novels of Karl May as a boy, he said, and so had the Führer, who told his generals to read them too.

"Lyon also asks about the trucks," Wachholz said, and laid a typed sheet beside the roses. "Two of our North Africans died in a gorge below Dieulefit on the twentieth of July, and a boy who walked out of the scrub two days later says the girl at the checkpoint was called Fräulein Brenner."

Ruth kept her eyes off the typed sheet and her burned arm still across the coat in her lap.

"Your forester is dead," he said. "Two days ago, in Valence. You cannot hurt him now. So why not tell me where the flyer is, and spare yourself?"

Ruth looked at the yellow roses. Wachholz might be lying about Gaston. He might be telling the truth. Either way, the next name after Gaston's was Marthe's, and the name after Marthe's was the one stamped into the metal against her knee.

"I don't know any American," she said.

Wachholz screwed the lid back on the tin and looked at her a while with something close to sorrow. Then he rang the bell.

---

On Tuesday, the eighth of August, before dawn, they brought her down the five flights for the last time.

A bus waited in the court with its engine running and its blinds drawn. **[VERIFY: transport of the August 8, 1944 convoy prisoners to the Gare de l'Est]** Inside sat women, a dozen or more, some with bandaged hands, one with both eyes swollen shut. Ruth knew the face in the third seat before her eyes had adjusted to the dark.

Violette Szabo lifted her head. Her dark hair was dirty and her lip was split, and she still looked like a girl on her way to a dance.

"Texas," she said quietly. "Oh, no."

"Afraid so."

The small upright woman beside her was Denise Bloch, a wireless operator, and across the aisle sat a thin, freckled girl named Lilian Rolfe, coughing into her sleeve. The three of them looked at the cut on Ruth's cheek and at the arm she held against her ribs, and none of them asked one question. The cut and the arm told the whole story.

At the Gare de l'Est the guards marched them along the platform past soldiers going home on leave, and Ruth saw the chains. The men prisoners came in pairs, chained at the wrist and the ankle, shuffling, some of them still in the clothes they had worn on the day of their arrest, weeks earlier. One of the men walked like an officer even in chains. Violette murmured that he was a British wing commander named Yeo-Thomas, and that the Gestapo called him the White Rabbit.

A guard knelt and locked an iron shackle around Violette's ankle and the other end around Bloch's, and the two women stood up together as though they had practiced it.

The train lurched east out of Paris into the heat. August sun beat on the steel roof, the windows were bolted shut, and nobody brought water. By midmorning the men in the compartments down the corridor were calling for a drink, hoarse, in French and English, and then they stopped calling, and the quiet from down the corridor was harder to hear than the calling.

In the afternoon, out in the open country of the Marne, the airplanes came.

Ruth heard the engines, then the guns. The train screamed to a stop. The guards jumped down and lay flat in the ditch grass with their arms over their heads, and the prisoners stayed locked in the carriages. Cannon fire walked across a field beside the track. Gravel rattled on the roof. A window down the corridor burst inward, and somebody yelled in English.

Violette Szabo got to her feet, and Denise Bloch got up with her, because the chain gave her no choice and because she was Denise Bloch.

Violette found a jug in the lavatory at the end of the carriage and filled it at the tap. Then the two of them got down on their hands and knees on the floor of the corridor, chained ankle to ankle, and crawled with the jug between them through the broken glass toward the compartments where the men sat chained, while the carriage rocked and the guns went on along the track.

Ruth went after them without deciding to go. Her knees found the gritty boards. She crawled behind Bloch's shackled ankle with a tin cup in her good hand, and at the door of the men's compartment she raised herself and held the cup to a man's cracked lips. He had a week's beard and dried blood in one ear. He shut his eyes and drank the way a dog drinks, fast, spilling half of it down his chin.

"God bless you," he whispered in English.

Ruth filled the cup from Violette's jug and held it to the next man's mouth.

Violette went down the line laughing at them, teasing them, telling one he needed a shave and another that his wife would never let him in the house looking that way. The men laughed back through split lips. Yeo-Thomas took his water from Violette and looked at her a long time.

When the airplanes were gone and the guards climbed back aboard brushing grass seed off their tunics, Violette sat down hard on the bench, Bloch beside her at the end of the chain, and wiped her face with her wrist.

"Well," she said. "What a lark."

Violette's hands were shaking. Ruth took one of them and held it. The train jerked and rolled east again toward Germany, and the women sat in the hot dark with the blinds down, and nobody said the name of the place they were going, because each of them already knew it.

Under the cup of her other hand, through the grey wool across her knees, Ruth found the hard little rectangle in the hem and held it.
