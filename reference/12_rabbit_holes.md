# Down the Rabbit Hole: The True History Inside *A Debt Too Great to Pay*

*A Debt Too Great to Pay* is a novel. Ruth Vellmer never carried the mail in Gillespie County, and Sam Mayfield never flew a red-tailed Mustang over Berlin. Almost everything standing around the two of them is true. The admiral born on Main Street is real. The hotel built like a steamboat is real. The thirty-two sailors cut out of an upside-down battleship, the girl who crawled down a train corridor in chains to give water to men bound for Buchenwald, the two Dutch sisters who thanked God for the fleas, the Black pilots who painted their tails red so the bomber crews would know who brought them home: every one of them is real, and every one of them waits at the bottom of a search bar. This companion follows each rabbit hole in the manuscript to the bottom and writes down the truth found there. The last section marks the line where the history stops and the story starts, so every reader knows exactly which ground is solid.

## The Town

Martinsburg is Fredericksburg, Texas. The novel never prints the word "Fredericksburg," and every landmark in Martinsburg points straight at it: the Steamboat Hotel, the Nimitz boyhood, the Easter Fires, the Sunday houses, the limestone storefronts on Main, the peach stands on the road to Stonewall. Pedernales County is Gillespie County under another name.

### 1. Martinsburg Is Fredericksburg
**Where it appears:** Prologue: Pallbearers; Ch. 1, Christmond; Ch. 2, The Man by the Fire; Ch. 3, The Sixth Vellmer; Ch. 4, The Schoolyard; Ch. 5, Lights on the Pedernales; Ch. 6, Honolulu; The Cabin: I; Ch. 8, Unknowns; Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied; Ch. 11, The Farm; Ch. 15, Ravensbrück; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 18, Kate; Ch. 19, Hammer and Chisel; Ch. 20, The Back Row; Ch. 21, The Widow on the Homeplace; The Cabin: III; Ch. 22, The Office on Main Street; Ch. 23, Letters to Houston; Ch. 24, Jezebel's Paper; Ch. 26, The Hearing; Ch. 27, The Bus; Ch. 28, The Vineyard; Ch. 29, Playoffs; Ch. 31, Christmas Eve; Ch. 32, Pallbearers; Ch. 33, Justice; Epilogue: Christmond
**What the novel says:** Martinsburg is the German county seat on the Pedernales where Ruth was born, carried the mail for thirty-nine years and rode the Mustangs' bus.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Fredericksburg was laid out in 1846 by German settlers of the Adelsverein under John O. Meusebach, and the town still keeps its German bones: limestone houses, Main Street wide enough to turn an ox wagon, and family names that have sat in the same pews for a century and a half. Gillespie County, its county, holds Enchanted Rock, Luckenbach, Stonewall and the LBJ Ranch, every one of them named in the novel. A reader who drives into Fredericksburg with this book on the seat beside him will recognize the town before he reads the city-limits sign.
**Start digging here:** [Fredericksburg, Texas (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredericksburg,_Texas) · [Architecture of Fredericksburg, Texas (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Fredericksburg,_Texas)

### 2. Albert, the Town That Was Martinsburg
**Where it appears:** Ch. 2, The Man by the Fire
**What the novel says:** Marcus finds a ghost town down the road called Albert that somebody sold on eBay in 2007, dance hall and all.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The novel's town took its name from this one. Albert's first post office, open from 1877 to 1886, was named Martinsburg, and in 1892 Albert Luckenbach, the same Luckenbach family behind the famous dance-hall town, registered a new post office under his own first name. In late October 2007 owner Bobby Cave put the whole town on eBay with a $2.5 million reserve, and the bidding closed at about $3.8 million. The Albert dance hall still advertises "dancing and romancing since 1892."
**Start digging here:** [Albert, Texas (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert,_Texas) · [Town sells for $3.8M during auction on eBay (Lawrence Journal-World, 2007)](https://www2.ljworld.com/news/2007/nov/25/town_sells_38m_during_auction_ebay/)

### 3. Chester Nimitz, the Fredericksburg Boy
**Where it appears:** Ch. 2, The Man by the Fire; Ch. 3, The Sixth Vellmer; Ch. 8, Unknowns; Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied; The Cabin: III; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** Walter Vellmer joins the Navy because of a Martinsburg boy named Chester Nimitz, who grows up to command the Pacific Fleet.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Chester William Nimitz was born in Fredericksburg on February 24, 1885, after his father had already died. Nimitz's German-born grandfather, Charles Henry Nimitz, a former merchant seaman, Texas Ranger and Confederate captain, raised the boy his first five years and filled his head with the sea. Nimitz went to Annapolis, became a fleet admiral and signed the Japanese surrender for the United States aboard USS *Missouri* in Tokyo Bay. A Hill Country town with no water deep enough to float a canoe produced the man who ran the biggest naval war in history.
**Start digging here:** [Chester W. Nimitz (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester_W._Nimitz) · [Charles Henry Nimitz (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Henry_Nimitz)

### 4. The Steamboat Hotel
**Where it appears:** Ch. 2, The Man by the Fire; Ch. 3, The Sixth Vellmer; Ch. 8, Unknowns; The Cabin: III
**What the novel says:** The Nimitz Hotel on Main Street had a front built like the bow of a ship, and today it is a museum of the whole Pacific war.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Charles Henry Nimitz opened his hotel on Main Street around 1852 and gave it a front shaped like a steamboat, so travelers on the road west called it the Steamboat Hotel. The building is now part of the National Museum of the Pacific War, the only museum in the continental United States devoted entirely to the Pacific and Asian theaters of World War II. The steamboat front still faces Main Street.
**Start digging here:** [National Museum of the Pacific War (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_the_Pacific_War) · [National Museum of the Pacific War](https://www.pacificwarmuseum.org/)

### 5. The Easter Fires
**Where it appears:** Ch. 3, The Sixth Vellmer
**What the novel says:** On the night before Easter fires burn on the hills, because a frightened mother in 1847 told her children the Comanche signal fires belonged to the Easter Rabbit.
**What you'll find when you dig:** John O. Meusebach rode north and made peace with the Penateka Comanche in March 1847, and the treaty was signed in Fredericksburg on May 9, 1847. The Meusebach-Comanche Treaty is famous as a treaty that was never broken. The Easter Rabbit story blends that treaty with the old German custom of Easter-eve hill fires, and Easter 1847 fell on April 4, after the council, so historians call the tale a legend. Fredericksburg lights the hills every Holy Saturday anyway.
**Start digging here:** [The Unbroken Peace Treaty (Texas Co-op Power)](https://texascooppower.com/the-unbroken-peace-treaty/) · [Meusebach-Comanche Treaty (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meusebach%E2%80%93Comanche_Treaty)

### 6. The War on German Texans, 1917 to 1919
**Where it appears:** Ch. 3, The Sixth Vellmer; Ch. 5, Lights on the Pedernales; Ch. 8, Unknowns; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 21, The Widow on the Homeplace; Ch. 30, The Nativity
**What the novel says:** In 1918 four men from the State Council of Defense in Austin stand Fritz Vellmer on his own porch and make him kiss the flag, Walter tells Ruth that the same year a mob in Illinois wrapped a German named Prager in a flag, made him kiss it, and hanged him, and Fritz never speaks German on the square again.
**What you'll find when you dig:** German Texans made up about five percent of the state when America entered the Great War, and they paid for it. Vigilantes beat and whipped German Texans, and some were murdered for criticizing the war or refusing to buy Liberty bonds. In 1919 the Texas Legislature passed House Bill 304, which made teaching German in any public school or college a crime. Mobs across the country forced German Americans to kiss the flag, and in Collinsville, Illinois, a mob wrapped Robert Prager in a flag in April 1918 and lynched him. Fredericksburg's own First Lieutenant Louis J. Jordan was among the first German-American officers to die fighting Germany.
**Start digging here:** [Texas State Library: German Texans in WWI](https://www.tsl.texas.gov/lobbyexhibits/ww1-germantexan) · [Lynching of Robert Prager, 1918 (Madison Historical)](https://madison-historical.siue.edu/encyclopedia/lynching-of-robert-prager-1918/)

### 7. Texas German, a Language Frozen in 1850
**Where it appears:** Ch. 3, The Sixth Vellmer; Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied; Ch. 14, Avenue Foch
**What the novel says:** The OSS man says Ruth's German is a hundred years out of date, and the Gestapo interrogator says she speaks like a country pastor in 1850.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Texas German grew out of the five or six regional German dialects the settlers carried over in the 1840s, before Germany even existed as one country. Cut off from the homeland, the dialect froze in place and invented its own words for Texas things. About 160,000 Texans still spoke German in 1940, and only a few thousand speak it today. The Texas German Dialect Project at the University of Texas has recorded hundreds of the last native speakers, and Fredericksburg held onto the language longer than almost any town in the state.
**Start digging here:** [Sprechen Sie Texas Deutsch? (Texas Highways)](https://texashighways.com/culture/sprechen-sie-texas-deutsch-how-a-variation-of-german-once-ruled-in-fredericksburg/) · [Texas German (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_German_language)

### 8. Sunday Houses
**Where it appears:** Ch. 5, Lights on the Pedernales; The Cabin: I; Ch. 11, The Farm; Ch. 18, Kate; Ch. 21, The Widow on the Homeplace
**What the novel says:** Lena keeps the family's little Sunday house in town, and Sam's secret letters come there addressed to Miss Lena Vellmer.
**What you'll find when you dig:** German farm families who lived out in the county built tiny town houses, usually one or two rooms with a sleeping loft reached by an outside stair. The family drove in Saturday to trade and market, slept in the Sunday house, went to church Sunday morning and drove home. Most were built between the 1890s and the early 1900s, and old couples retired into them. Dozens still stand in Fredericksburg.
**Start digging here:** [Architecture of Fredericksburg, Texas (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Fredericksburg,_Texas)

### 9. Enchanted Rock Talks at Night
**Where it appears:** Ch. 2, The Man by the Fire; Ch. 4, The Schoolyard; The Cabin: I; Ch. 11, The Farm; Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 19, Hammer and Chisel
**What the novel says:** Camped at the foot of the pink granite dome, Ruth and Sam hear the rock groan and crack all night as it cools.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Enchanted Rock is a pink granite dome rising about 425 feet above the surrounding hills north of Fredericksburg. The rock soaks up heat all day, and when the outer layers cool and shrink at night the dome creaks and groans. The Tonkawa and Comanche held the place sacred because of the sounds and the ghost lights on the summit. People climb the dome at dawn today, the same way two thirteen-year-olds do in Chapter 4.
**Start digging here:** [The History of Enchanted Rock (Texas Parks & Wildlife Magazine)](https://tpwmagazine.com/parks/the-history-of-enchanted-rock/) · [Enchanted Rock State Natural Area (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enchanted_Rock_State_Natural_Area)

### 10. The Shrimp in the Granite
**Where it appears:** Ch. 4, The Schoolyard
**What the novel says:** On top of Enchanted Rock round pools of rainwater hold tiny shrimp darting in the green.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Wind and water have carved shallow bowls called gnammas into the summit of the dome, and every spring they fill with rain. Fairy shrimp appear in those pools, lay their eggs as the water dries and die, and the eggs lie dormant in the dust until the next rain wakes them. Nobody knows for certain how the shrimp reached the top of a granite mountain. Birds may have carried them, or they may be survivors of a shallow sea that once covered Texas.
**Start digging here:** [Enchanted Rock's Ephemeral Ponds (Texas Parks & Wildlife Magazine)](https://tpwmagazine.com/parks/enchanted-rocks-ephemeral-ponds-host-diverse-life/)

### 11. The Pedernales Electric Cooperative and LBJ
**Where it appears:** Ch. 1, Christmond; Ch. 2, The Man by the Fire; Ch. 5, Lights on the Pedernales; The Cabin: I; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 27, The Bus
**What the novel says:** Henry Vellmer signs up for the light in 1938, shakes Congressman Lyndon Johnson's hand and climbs the poles that bring electricity up the Pedernales.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Pedernales Electric Cooperative was incorporated in Austin on May 19, 1938, after freshman Congressman Lyndon B. Johnson lobbied Franklin Roosevelt and the Rural Electrification Administration for the loan. The first lines were energized in October 1939, and Meter No. 1 came on at Babe Smith's ranch at Oakalla on October 31, 1939. PEC is headquartered in Johnson City and is the largest electric cooperative in the United States.
**Start digging here:** [PEC Celebrates 85 Years (Pedernales Electric Cooperative)](https://mypec.com/pec-celebrates-85-years-of-powering-the-texas-hill-country/) · [The LBJ Legacy: Pedernales Electric Co-op (National Park Service)](https://www.nps.gov/places/000/the-lbj-legacy-pedernales-electric-co-op.htm)

### 12. Luckenbach
**Where it appears:** Ch. 5, Lights on the Pedernales; Ch. 6, Honolulu
**What the novel says:** Lena plays fiddle at the Luckenbach dance hall, and a German waltz from that hall follows Ruth all the way to Honolulu.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Luckenbach's old general store and saloon reputedly opened in 1849, and land records point to the 1880s. In 1970 Hondo Crouch and two partners bought the whole hamlet for $30,000, and in 1977 Waylon Jennings and Willie Nelson sang "Luckenbach, Texas (Back to the Basics of Love)" to the top of the country charts. The dance hall still runs on Saturday nights. Albert Luckenbach, whose family name is on the town, is the same man who renamed Martinsburg after himself.
**Start digging here:** [Luckenbach, Texas (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luckenbach,_Texas) · [Luckenbach, Texas](https://www.luckenbachtexas.com/texas-state-of-mind)

### 13. Utopia, the Sabinal and the Lost Maples
**Where it appears:** Ch. 5, Lights on the Pedernales; Ch. 23, Letters to Houston
**What the novel says:** Anna marries a bronc rider at a rodeo dance on the Sabinal River below Utopia, while up the river the maples go red, and Anna is buried at Utopia.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Utopia sits on the Sabinal River in the canyon country southwest of Kerrville, and up the river lies Lost Maples State Natural Area, where a stand of bigtooth maples left over from a cooler age turns red and gold every November. Texans drive hundreds of miles to see those maples, because Texas has almost none anywhere else. The town of Utopia was the setting for the 2011 film *Seven Days in Utopia*.
**Start digging here:** [Lost Maples State Natural Area (Texas Parks & Wildlife)](https://tpwd.texas.gov/spdest/findadest/parks/lost_maples/) · [Lost Maples State Natural Area (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Maples_State_Natural_Area)

### 14. Stonewall and Its Peaches
**Where it appears:** Ch. 1, Christmond; Ch. 22, The Office on Main Street
**What the novel says:** Ruth hands the dog man two jars of preserves from the last of the Stonewall peaches, because the drought got the rest.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Stonewall, east of Fredericksburg on the Pedernales, is the heart of the Hill Country peach belt, and roadside peach stands line U.S. 290 every summer. Lyndon Johnson was born near Stonewall and is buried there on the LBJ Ranch. Gillespie County has grown peaches for market for about a century.
**Start digging here:** [Stonewall, Texas (Texas Escapes)](https://texasescapes.com/TexasHillCountryTowns/Stonewall-Texas.htm) · [Gillespie County Peaches (Fredericksburg Chamber of Commerce)](https://business.fredericksburg-texas.com/blog/Details/gillespie-county-peaches-275736)

### 15. Comfort and the Sisterdale Road
**Where it appears:** Prologue: Pallbearers; Ch. 23, Letters to Houston; Ch. 24, Jezebel's Paper; Ch. 25, Kenji; Ch. 32, Pallbearers
**What the novel says:** The Japanese herbalist lives out the Sisterdale road near Comfort, left at the cattle guard with the boot on the post, and on the morning of Ruth's funeral a deputy waves the Mayfields off the highway and up through Comfort, past a stone obelisk carved with German names and the words *Treue der Union*.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Comfort was founded in 1854 by German Freethinkers, educated men who wanted no church and no slavery. Sisterdale, up the road, was one of the "Latin settlements," where German farmers read Cicero by lamplight. In 1866 Comfort raised the Treue der Union monument to the German Unionists killed by Confederates at the Nueces River in 1862, one of the only monuments to Union men in the old Confederacy.
**Start digging here:** [Comfort, Texas (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comfort,_Texas) · [Nueces massacre (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nueces_massacre)

### 16. Christmond, the Christmas Moon
**Where it appears:** Ch. 1, Christmond; Ch. 23, Letters to Houston; Epilogue: Christmond
**What the novel says:** The old German settlers called the December moon *Christmond*, the Christmas Moon.
**What you'll find when you dig:** *Christmond* and *Christmonat* are real old German names for December, along with *Julmond*, *Heilmond* and *Heiligenmonat*. *Julmond* comes from the pagan midwinter Jul festival, and *Christmond* replaced it when the Germans became Christians. The word means the month or moon of Christ.
**Start digging here:** [Dezember (German Wikipedia)](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezember)

### 17. The Christmas Eve Supermoon of 2026
**Where it appears:** Ch. 1, Christmond
**What the novel says:** On Christmas Eve 2026 a supermoon fills the attic window, the biggest full moon of the year, and the last Christmas Eve full moon came in 1996.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The full moon of December 23, 2026, at 7:28 p.m. Central time (01:28 UTC on December 24), is the last and largest supermoon of the year, about 356,740 kilometers from Earth and roughly 8 percent bigger and 16 percent brighter than an average full moon. By a Texas clock the last full moon on Christmas Eve came in 1996, and the next comes in 2102, the date the newspapers printed that Christmas (UPI, December 23, 1996). The ice storm is fiction, and the moon is on the calendar.

## Pearl Harbor

### 18. The S.S. *Lurline* and Boat Day
**Where it appears:** Ch. 6, Honolulu; Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied
**What the novel says:** Ruth sails to Honolulu on the *Lurline*, and the band plays on the pier while island boys dive off the Aloha Tower wharf for coins.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Matson liner *Lurline* ran the express route from the West Coast to Honolulu, and her arrival brought the Royal Hawaiian Band, flower leis and coin divers to the Aloha Tower pier. On December 7, 1941, the *Lurline* was halfway between Honolulu and San Francisco with a record 765 passengers, and she ran home blacked out under radio silence. The Army took her for a troopship, and Matson's white liners went to war painted grey.
**Start digging here:** [SS Lurline (1932) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Lurline_(1932))

### 19. The Naval Hospital at Hospital Point
**Where it appears:** Ch. 6, Honolulu; Ch. 7, December 7; Ch. 8, Unknowns; Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied; Ch. 22, The Office on Main Street
**What the novel says:** Ruth works the wards of the Naval Hospital at the mouth of Pearl Harbor and on December 7 cuts burned sailors out of their clothes on the lawn.
**What you'll find when you dig:** On December 7, 1941, Naval Hospital Pearl Harbor took in 546 battle casualties and 313 dead, about 452 of them admitted in under three hours, and the midnight census stood at 960. The hospital had 29 Navy nurses, and Red Cross nurses, civilian women with first-aid training and the wives of enlisted men pitched in beside them. A Japanese plane crashed on the hospital grounds that morning and burned a vacant quarters building to the ground.
**Start digging here:** [Pearl Harbor: Navy Medical Activities (Naval History and Heritage Command)](https://history.navy.mil/research/library/online-reading-room/title-list-alphabetically/p/pearl-harbor-navy-medical-activities.html) · [Medical report, Pearl Harbor (HyperWar)](https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/rep/Pearl/Medical.html)

### 20. The *Oklahoma* Rolls Over
**Where it appears:** Ch. 2, The Man by the Fire; Ch. 6, Honolulu; Ch. 7, December 7; Ch. 8, Unknowns; Ch. 28, The Vineyard
**What the novel says:** From the hospital lawn Ruth watches her brother's battleship lean like a live oak under the axe and roll over with her screws in the air.
**What you'll find when you dig:** At least five torpedoes hit USS *Oklahoma*, moored outboard on Battleship Row, and within twelve minutes she capsized at her berth. Of her crew of more than 1,200, 429 were killed. Only the *Arizona* lost more men that morning.
**Start digging here:** [Recovered and Identified on the USS Oklahoma (National WWII Museum)](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/recovered-and-identified-soldiers-uss-oklahoma) · [USS Oklahoma (BB-37), Dictionary of American Naval Fighting Ships](https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/ships/dafs/BB/bb37.html)

### 21. Thirty-Two Men Cut Out of the Hull
**Where it appears:** Ch. 7, December 7; Ch. 8, Unknowns
**What the novel says:** Men trapped inside the overturned *Oklahoma* bang on the steel, and the yard men cut thirty-two of them out through the bottom.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Julio DeCastro, a civilian caulker at the Pearl Harbor Navy Yard, led the rescue that pulled thirty-two sailors out of the upturned hull. The torches set fire to the compartments below, so the crew switched to pneumatic chipping hammers and chiseled through the steel by hand. Trapped men took turns hammering SOS in Morse code with a wrench while the air went bad and the water rose. The rescuers worked almost without stopping from the morning of December 7 into the night of December 8.
**Start digging here:** [The Civilian Worker Who Spearheaded Pearl Harbor's Most Successful Rescue Mission (Smithsonian)](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-civilian-worker-who-spearheaded-pearl-harbors-most-successful-rescue-mission-180979167/)

### 22. "Missing Following Action": The Navy's Telegram
**Where it appears:** Ch. 8, Unknowns
**What the novel says:** Before Christmas 1941 one telegram comes to the cottage in Kaimuki and a second to the Vellmer place on the Pedernales, and both say Walter is missing following action in the performance of his duty and in the service of his country.
**What you'll find when you dig:** In December 1941 the Navy Department notified the families of the men it could not account for at Pearl Harbor by telegram, in a set form of words. The first telegram to the Kentucky family of Lambert Ray Tapp, a sailor on the *Arizona*, said he was "missing following action in the performance of his duty and in the service of his country." The *Oklahoma* lost 429 men, and her families lived with that word for years, because the ship lay capsized in the harbor until 1943 and most of her dead could not be named.
**Start digging here:** [Two Sons, Two Wars: A Kentucky Family's Sacrifice Begins Dec. 7, 1941 (Kentucky Lantern)](https://kentuckylantern.com/2025/12/05/two-sons-two-wars-a-kentucky-familys-sacrifice-begins-dec-7-1941/) · [USS Oklahoma (BB-37) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Oklahoma_(BB-37))

### 23. Tannic Acid on the Burn Ward
**Where it appears:** Ch. 7, December 7
**What the novel says:** Doctors paint the burned men with tannic acid jelly until they lie glazed brown on the lawn as if somebody had varnished them.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Navy's own medical report says the burns at Pearl Harbor were treated mainly with tannic acid jelly and solution, along with picric acid, gentian violet and triple dye. Corpsmen sprayed tannic acid solution with ordinary flit guns, the kind used on mosquitoes, until the supply ran short. Sulfa drugs went to infected burn patients from the second and third day.
**Start digging here:** [Medical report, Pearl Harbor (HyperWar)](https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/rep/Pearl/Medical.html) · [U.S. Navy Medical Department in WWII, Chapter II (HyperWar)](https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-Medical/I/USN-Medical-2.html)

### 24. Morphine on the Lawn
**Where it appears:** Ch. 7, December 7
**What the novel says:** Miss Draper gives each man his morphine shot and marks an M on his forehead so nobody gives him another.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Morphine was the first thing a wounded man got at Pearl Harbor, and the Navy's medical report notes that the new morphine Syrettes, a collapsible tube with its own needle, were easy for first-aid men to use in the field. Marking a man who had his dose was standard battlefield practice in World War II, so the next medic would not kill him with a second one.
**Start digging here:** [Medical report, Pearl Harbor (HyperWar)](https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/rep/Pearl/Medical.html)

### 25. The *Nevada* Runs for the Sea
**Where it appears:** Ch. 7, December 7
**What the novel says:** A battleship comes down the channel with her guns firing, trying for the open sea, and runs herself aground on the point below the hospital.
**What you'll find when you dig:** USS *Nevada* was the only battleship to get under way during the attack. Japanese dive bombers went after her hoping to sink her in the channel and bottle up the harbor, so her crew beached her at Hospital Point, right below the Naval Hospital. Photographs of the *Nevada* aground and burning off Hospital Point are some of the most famous pictures of the day.
**Start digging here:** [USS Nevada (BB-36) (Pacific Wrecks)](https://pacificwrecks.com/ship/usn/BB-36/80g-19940.html) · [USS Nevada (BB-36) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Nevada_(BB-36))

### 26. The Arizona Still Bleeds
**Where it appears:** Ch. 7, December 7; Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied; Ch. 19, Hammer and Chisel
**What the novel says:** A fireman from the *Arizona* dies on the lawn, and two years later oil from the *Arizona* still comes up in a rainbow on the water.
**What you'll find when you dig:** USS *Arizona* lost 1,177 men on December 7, 1941, and more than a thousand of them are still inside her. The wreck still leaks up to nine quarts of fuel oil into the harbor every day, more than eighty years later. Visitors to the memorial call the oil slicks the "black tears of the *Arizona*."

### 27. Dorie Miller at the Gun
**Where it appears:** Ch. 7, December 7; Ch. 8, Unknowns; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** A burned mess attendant tells Ruth that Dorie Miller, a big man from Waco and boxing champion of the ship, carried his captain and fired a machine gun nobody ever taught him to use.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Doris "Dorie" Miller of Waco was the heavyweight boxing champion of USS *West Virginia*, and he was collecting laundry when the torpedoes hit. Miller helped carry the mortally wounded Captain Mervyn Bennion to a safer spot, then took over a .50-caliber machine gun with no gunnery training and fired at the Japanese planes until the gun ran dry. Black sailors in 1941 served in the messes, and the Navy did not train them on the guns. Miller was killed in November 1943 when the escort carrier *Liscome Bay* was torpedoed off Makin, and the Navy named a future aircraft carrier, CVN-81, for him in 2020.

### 28. The Courier Names Him, and Nimitz Pins the Cross
**Where it appears:** Ch. 8, Unknowns
**What the novel says:** The Black press digs up Dorie Miller's name when the Navy will not say it, and in May 1942 Admiral Nimitz pins the Navy Cross on him aboard the *Enterprise*.
**What you'll find when you dig:** For months the Navy credited only "an unnamed Negro messman," and the *Pittsburgh Courier* identified Doris Miller by name in March 1942. On May 27, 1942, Admiral Chester Nimitz presented Miller the Navy Cross on the flight deck of USS *Enterprise* at Pearl Harbor. Miller was the first Black American ever awarded the Navy Cross, and the Fredericksburg admiral pinned it on him.

### 29. Nimitz Takes the Fleet on a Submarine
**Where it appears:** Ch. 8, Unknowns
**What the novel says:** The new admiral arrives on Christmas Day and takes command of the Pacific Fleet standing on a submarine, because his battleships are lying in the mud.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Nimitz reached Pearl Harbor on Christmas Day 1941 after a seven-day trip by train and airplane from Washington. ### 30. The Admiral Who Spoke German

### 31. Hawaii's Japanese, the Spy Rumors and Kuehn's Light Code
**Where it appears:** Ch. 8, Unknowns
**What the novel says:** Mrs. Tanaka, whose son is in the Army, asks Ruth whether they will come for her, while the radio says Japanese spies in Hawaii guided the planes. Ten days after Kate is born a court in Honolulu sentences the Pearl Harbor spy to be shot, a German named Otto Kuehn. Years later Ruth reads the signals Kuehn worked out for the Japanese (a light in a window of his beach house at Lanikai, a star on the sail of a small boat, a want ad for a Chinese rug) and learns that the Navy translated the consul's cable carrying them four days after the *Oklahoma* rolled over with Walter inside her.
**What you'll find when you dig:** In 1940 more than 37 percent of the people of Hawaii were of Japanese descent, and rumors of sabotage ran wild after the attack. No one was ever charged with sabotage, and the one man convicted of spying for Japan in Hawaii was a German, Bernard Otto Kuehn, whom a military commission in Honolulu sentenced to be shot on February 21, 1942, a sentence later commuted to prison. On December 3, 1941, the Japanese consul in Honolulu cabled Tokyo the signal system Kuehn had worked out for the submarines off Oahu: lights in the window of his house at Lanikai Beach, a star on the head of a Star Boat's sail, a want ad reading "Chinese rug etc. for sale, apply P. O. box 1476," and a bonfire on Maui. The Navy's translation of that cable is dated December 11, 1941, and Congress printed it in 1946 as Exhibit No. 2 of the Joint Committee on the Investigation of the Pearl Harbor Attack. Hawaii arrested about 10,000 people and held about 2,000, a fraction of the 110,000 Japanese Americans removed from the West Coast, because the islands could not run without their Japanese workers. Nisei soldiers from Hawaii formed the 100th Infantry Battalion and fought in Italy as one of the most decorated units in the Army.

### 32. The Pearl Harbor Questionnaire
**Where it appears:** Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** Ruth's Lucie letter of December 7, 1991, says a Yugoslav lawyer named Dusko Popov, a double agent working for the British, carried a German questionnaire to the FBI in New York in August 1941, that a full page of it asked about Pearl Harbor, the airfields, the ammunition dumps, the piers and the dry docks, and that Sir John Masterman printed the questionnaire in 1972. Ruth's pencil note says that in August 1941 she changed sheets at Hospital Point, in sight of the dry docks on the German list.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Dušan "Dusko" Popov, a Yugoslav lawyer run by MI5 as the double agent TRICYCLE, came to the United States for the Abwehr in August 1941 carrying a questionnaire, partly in microdot, one section of which asked for details on Pearl Harbor: airfields, ammunition and mine depots, piers and dry docks. Sir John Masterman, who chaired the Twenty Committee that ran Britain's double agents, printed the questionnaire as an appendix to *The Double-Cross System* (Yale, 1972). Popov is often named as one model for Ian Fleming's James Bond.

### 33. The Honolulu "Bombs" That Were American
**Where it appears:** Ch. 8, Unknowns
**What the novel says:** On Monday, December 8, Ruth's bread truck climbs through McCully past a whole block of stores and frame houses burned to the slabs, and the driver says the Jap bombs did it. Two days later a gunner's mate in bed nine tells her the shells that came down on McCully were American, five-inch, fused wrong, so they burst in the streets instead of the sky, and that the papers will call them Japanese bombs.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The National Park Service counts 49 civilians killed on Oahu on December 7, 1941, and records that many of the five-inch anti-aircraft rounds fired at the Japanese planes failed to burst in the air and exploded when they came down in civilian streets around Pearl Harbor and Honolulu. A Pearl Harbor park ranger wrote in 1996 that most of Honolulu's civilian dead were killed by improperly fused U.S. Navy anti-aircraft shells. In McCully a whole block of stores and houses burned while the fire companies were out at the bases, and the first reports and photograph captions blamed Japanese bombs.

### 34. Martial Law and the Blackout
**Where it appears:** Ch. 7, December 7; Ch. 8, Unknowns; Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied
**What the novel says:** The driver of a bread truck tells Ruth the governor handed the islands to the Army on Sunday afternoon, courts and all, and the word for it is martial law; the hospital goes dark at nightfall, cars drive with their headlights painted blue, and Kate is born by lamplight behind a blanket over the window.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Hawaii went under martial law on the afternoon of December 7, 1941, and stayed under it until October 1944. The Army ran a strict curfew and blackout, suspended habeas corpus, fingerprinted every adult and censored the mail and the press. Headlights were painted over, windows were blacked out, and the islands lived in the dark for most of the war.

### 35. The Wounded from Midway
**Where it appears:** Ch. 8, Unknowns
**What the novel says:** In the second week of June 1942 the wounded come in from Midway, men from the *Yorktown* and the ships that sailed with her.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Battle of Midway, June 4 to 7, 1942, cost Japan four aircraft carriers and turned the Pacific war. The United States lost the carrier *Yorktown* and the destroyer *Hammann*, and the wounded came back to Pearl Harbor by ship. Nimitz planned that battle from his headquarters at Pearl Harbor.

### 36. Jack Lucas, the Boy Who Lied
**Where it appears:** Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** A fifteen-year-old Marine who lied his way into the Corps drives a laundry truck at the hospital and swears he will get to the fight anyway.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Jacklyn Harold Lucas of Plymouth, North Carolina, joined the Marines at fourteen by forging his mother's signature. ### 37. The Righting of the *Oklahoma*
**Where it appears:** Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied
**What the novel says:** Through the spring of 1943 salvage crews run steel cables from the hull to winches on Ford Island and pull the *Oklahoma* upright inch by inch.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The righting of USS *Oklahoma* began on March 8, 1943, with twenty-one electric winches anchored on Ford Island hauling on cables run over wooden headframes on her hull. ### 38. The Unknowns
**Where it appears:** Ch. 2, The Man by the Fire; Ch. 8, Unknowns; Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied; Ch. 11, The Farm; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; The Cabin: III; Ch. 22, The Office on Main Street; Ch. 28, The Vineyard
**What the novel says:** Walter lies sixty-six years at the Punchbowl under a stone marked Unknown, in one of forty-six *Oklahoma* graves a military board declared non-recoverable in October 1949, and Ernie says at the feed store that Walter lies in a grave in Hawaii marked Unknown.

### 39. The Submarine in Fredericksburg
**Where it appears:** Ch. 21, The Widow on the Homeplace
**What the novel says:** In 1991 Ruth drives twenty miles up the road to a history conference at the Nimitz Museum in Fredericksburg and sits in the back row while Kazuo Sakamaki, America's first Japanese prisoner of the war, weeps over the midget submarine he ran onto a reef off Oahu the week Walter died.
**What you'll find when you dig:** On December 7, 1941, Ensign Kazuo Sakamaki's two-man midget submarine, HA-19, ran onto a reef off Waimanalo, Oahu. Sakamaki's crewman drowned, and Sakamaki washed ashore and became America's first Japanese prisoner of war. The submarine toured the United States selling war bonds, and Sakamaki spent part of his captivity at the camp at Kenedy, Texas. Sakamaki would not speak about the war until 1991, when he came to a historical conference at the museum in Fredericksburg, now the National Museum of the Pacific War, where his submarine was on display, and he wept to see it for the first time in fifty years. Sakamaki died on November 29, 1999.

## Tuskegee and the Red Tails

### 40. Army Trainers over the Hill Country
**Where it appears:** Ch. 3, The Sixth Vellmer; Ch. 4, The Schoolyard; Ch. 6, Honolulu; The Cabin: I; Ch. 9, Tuskegee; Ch. 13, The Drôme
**What the novel says:** From a ledge on the Harwell place Sam watches blue-and-yellow Army trainers crawl north out of San Antonio, and Marcus finds Randolph Field, the "West Point of the Air."
**What you'll find when you dig:** Randolph Field, northeast of San Antonio, was dedicated on June 20, 1930, and through the 1930s it trained most of the Army's new pilots. Randolph's Spanish-style buildings and its spit-and-polish earned it the name "West Point of the Air," and MGM made a 1935 movie by that title. Army trainers of the period wore blue fuselages and yellow wings. Randolph trained only white cadets, so a Black boy in Texas could watch those planes and nothing more.
**Start digging here:** [Randolph Field Historic District (National Park Service)](https://www.nps.gov/articles/randolph-field-historic-district.htm) · [Randolph Air Force Base (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randolph_Air_Force_Base)

### 41. The Books in the Coffee Can
**Where it appears:** Ch. 5, Lights on the Pedernales
**What the novel says:** Ruth and Sam pass Lindbergh's *We*, Saint-Exupéry's *Wind, Sand and Stars* and the poems of Paul Laurence Dunbar back and forth through a Folgers can on the line fence.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Charles Lindbergh published *We* in 1927, weeks after his solo flight to Paris. Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's *Wind, Sand and Stars* came out in English in 1939 and won the National Book Award, a mail pilot's account of flying the desert and the Andes at night by the stars. Paul Laurence Dunbar, the son of slaves, wrote "Sympathy," whose last line, "I know why the caged bird sings," Maya Angelou later took for the title of her memoir.
**Start digging here:** [Wind, Sand and Stars (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind,_Sand_and_Stars) · [Paul Laurence Dunbar (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Laurence_Dunbar)

### 42. Colored Boys Learning to Fly in Alabama
**Where it appears:** Ch. 5, Lights on the Pedernales
**What the novel says:** In 1940 the colored paper says a college in Alabama is teaching colored boys to fly under a government program, and it is for college men.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Civilian Pilot Training Program worked through colleges and universities, and Tuskegee Institute began its first class in late 1939 with twenty students. Tuskegee was one of six historically Black colleges in the program. Those college flyers became the seed of the Tuskegee Airmen.
**Start digging here:** [Tuskegee Flight Training Program (Encyclopedia of Alabama)](https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/tuskegee-flight-training-program/)

### 43. Stinson Field
**Where it appears:** Ch. 6, Honolulu
**What the novel says:** Sam rides the streetcar to Stinson Field with five dollars in his shoe and is told that for him there is no hour in the air, at a field that carries the name of two white girls, the Stinson sisters, who were flying airplanes out there before the end of the last war.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Stinson Field on the south side of San Antonio opened in 1915 as the flying school of the Stinson family, whose daughters Katherine and Marjorie were among the first women pilots in America. Stinson is the second-oldest continuously operating general aviation airport in the United States.
**Start digging here:** [Stinson Municipal Airport (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stinson_Municipal_Airport) · [Flying Schoolgirl Inspired Early Aviators (U.S. Air Force)](https://www.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/139663/flying-schoolgirl-inspired-early-aviators/)

### 44. Eugene Bullard, the Black Swallow of Death
**Where it appears:** Ch. 6, Honolulu
**What the novel says:** In a letter from San Antonio, Sam writes that Mr. Pettis, a mechanic in France in the last war, knew a colored man from Georgia over there named Bullard, who flew fighters for the French because his own country would not have him.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Eugene Jacques Bullard was born in Columbus, Georgia, on October 9, 1894, made his way to Europe as a boy and joined the French Foreign Legion when the First World War began. Badly wounded at Verdun in 1916, he trained as a pilot, and from August to November 1917 he flew combat missions for France with the Lafayette Flying Corps, the first Black American fighter pilot. When the United States entered the war, the U.S. Army Air Service refused to take him. Bullard fought for France again in 1940, died in New York on October 13, 1961, and on September 14, 1994, the Secretary of the Air Force posthumously appointed him a second lieutenant.
**Start digging here:** [Eugene Bullard, the First African American Fighter Pilot (The Museum of Flight)](https://blog.museumofflight.org/eugene-bullard-the-first-african-american-fighter-pilot-and-veteran-of-two-world-wars) · [Eugene Jacques Bullard (National Museum of the U.S. Air Force)](https://www.nationalmuseum.af.mil/Visit/Museum-Exhibits/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/197458/eugene-jacques-bullard/)

### 45. Two Years of College, Then a Test
**Where it appears:** Ch. 6, Honolulu; Ch. 8, Unknowns; Ch. 9, Tuskegee
**What the novel says:** In 1941 the Army wants two years of college, and in January 1942 a colored boy with an eighth-grade education can pass an examination instead.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The first Tuskegee class, Class 42-C-SE, entered training in July 1941 with Benjamin O. Davis Jr. among its cadets, under a rule that demanded two years of college. On January 5, 1942, the Army dropped the minimum cadet age from twenty to eighteen, and on January 15, 1942, it approved the Aviation Cadet Qualifying Examination in place of the college requirement. A young man who turned eighteen in January 1942, as Sam does, could walk in and take the test.

### 46. Double V
**Where it appears:** Ch. 9, Tuskegee
**What the novel says:** At the end of January 1942 the *Pittsburgh Courier* prints a letter from a colored man who worked the cafeteria at the Cessna airplane plant in Wichita, the one job Cessna would give him, under the question *Should I Sacrifice to Live 'Half-American'?*, and when the Double V follows in February, Sam cuts it out with his pocketknife and tacks it over his workbench.
**What you'll find when you dig:** On January 31, 1942, the *Pittsburgh Courier* printed a letter from James G. Thompson, a twenty-six-year-old cafeteria worker at the Cessna Aircraft plant in Wichita, Kansas, under the headline "Should I Sacrifice to Live 'Half-American'?", asking for a second V, for victory over the enemies at home. On February 7, 1942, the *Courier* launched the Double V campaign: victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism in America. The two V's ran in Black newspapers across the country through 1943.
**Start digging here:** [Double V campaign (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_V_campaign) · [The Double V Campaign (National Park Service)](https://home.nps.gov/poch/learn/historyculture/the-double-v-campaign.htm)

### 47. The Jim Crow Coach
**Where it appears:** Ch. 9, Tuskegee; Ch. 11, The Farm
**What the novel says:** Sam rides to Alabama in the colored coach behind the baggage car, with the cinders in his collar and a dining car four cars back where he cannot eat.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Southern railroads ran Black passengers in a separate coach, usually the one right behind the engine and baggage car, where the coal smoke was worst. Washington's Union Station was the line: north of it a Black passenger could sit where he pleased, and south of it the law put him back in the Jim Crow car. The Supreme Court did not strike down segregated railroad dining cars until *Henderson v. United States* in 1950.
**Start digging here:** [Henderson v. United States (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henderson_v._United_States)

### 48. The Signs Come Down at Tuskegee
**Where it appears:** Ch. 9, Tuskegee
**What the novel says:** Doors on the Army's own field for colored pilots say WHITE and COLORED, until a new white commander named Parrish orders the signs removed.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Noel F. Parrish took command of Tuskegee Army Air Field in December 1942. Parrish was born in Kentucky and earned his history degree at Rice Institute in Houston, and he ended the segregation that had ruled the base under his predecessor. Tuskegee Airman George Hardy said Parrish made life "much easier for blacks on the base," and Parrish fought his own superiors to get his pilots into combat. After retiring a brigadier general he went back to Rice for a doctorate in history.
**Start digging here:** [Rice Alum Noel Parrish: The Wind Beneath Their Wings (Rice University)](https://news2.rice.edu/2014/02/24/rice-alum-noel-parrish-the-wind-beneath-their-wings/) · [Noel F. Parrish (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noel_F._Parrish)

### 49. The Army War College Report of 1925
**Where it appears:** Ch. 9, Tuskegee
**What the novel says:** Lt. Harmon tells Sam there are men up the chain who built the field so they could close it, that the War College put it on paper back in '25 that a colored man cannot control himself in the face of danger to the extent the white man can, and that some of those men can say it by heart.
**What you'll find when you dig:** In 1925 the Army War College produced a study titled *The Use of Negro Manpower in War*. The report called the Black soldier "mentally inferior to the white man" and said he "cannot control himself in the face of danger to the extent the white man can." The report recommended segregated units under white officers, and its reasoning set the Army's course for Black servicemen until the Tuskegee program opened in 1941. Every cadet who earned his wings at Tuskegee proved that page wrong.
**Start digging here:** [The 1925 Army War College Report (CAF Rise Above)](https://cafriseabove.org/the-1925-army-war-college-report) · [The Use of Negro Manpower in War (Social History for Every Classroom, CUNY)](https://shec.ashp.cuny.edu/items/show/808)

### 50. Moton Field and the Black Instructors
**Where it appears:** Ch. 9, Tuskegee
**What the novel says:** Primary training is at Moton Field, the Institute's own grass field, where many of the civilian instructors are Black men.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Moton Field belonged to Tuskegee Institute, which ran primary flight training under an Army contract with civilian instructors. Charles Alfred "Chief" Anderson, hired in July 1940 as the first flight instructor, was the chief civilian instructor pilot, and Black instructors such as Roscoe Draper taught beside him. Moton Field is now the Tuskegee Airmen National Historic Site.
**Start digging here:** [The Airfields (CAF Rise Above)](https://cafriseabove.org/the-tuskegee-airmen/tuskegee-airmen-history/the-airfields/) · [Tuskegee Airmen National Historic Site (National Park Service)](https://www.nps.gov/tuai/index.htm)

### 51. The Stearman
**Where it appears:** Ch. 3, The Sixth Vellmer; Ch. 6, Honolulu; The Cabin: I; Ch. 9, Tuskegee
**What the novel says:** The first time Sam sees the row of Stearman biplanes, blue in the body and yellow on the wings, he stops so hard his bunkmate walks into his back.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Boeing-Stearman Model 75, the Army's PT-13 and PT-17, was the biplane nearly every American military pilot of World War II learned on, more than 8,000 of them built. Tuskegee cadets made about 175 landings in primary and had to solo and fly cross-country before basic training. The tradition of cutting off a pilot's shirttail after his first solo is older than the Stearman and still alive.
**Start digging here:** [Boeing-Stearman Model 75 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing-Stearman_Model_75) · [Training at Tuskegee (U.S. Air Force)](https://www.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/473258/training-at-tuskegee-turning-dreams-into-reality/)

### 52. The Vultee Vibrator
**Where it appears:** Ch. 9, Tuskegee
**What the novel says:** Basic training is in the BT-13, which shakes so hard the cadets call it the Vultee Vibrator.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Vultee BT-13 Valiant was the Army's basic trainer, built by the thousands, and its rattling canopy and shaking airframe earned it the nickname "Vultee Vibrator" from every class that flew it. Tuskegee cadets logged about seventy to seventy-five hours in the BT-13 and learned instrument, night and formation flying in it.

### 53. The AT-6 and the Towed Sleeve
**Where it appears:** Ch. 9, Tuskegee; Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied

### 54. Half Went Home: Washouts and Hondo
**Where it appears:** Ch. 9, Tuskegee
**What the novel says:** Deacon washes out and says half the boys who come to Tuskegee go home without wings, and the washouts are headed for navigator school at Hondo, Texas.

### 56. Two Tags and No Next of Kin
**Where it appears:** Ch. 9, Tuskegee; Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk; Ch. 35, What I Saw; Ch. 36, The Four Drawers; Ch. 37, The Key; Epilogue: Christmond
**What the novel says:** Sam's two tags, strips of stamped steel a little smaller than a matchbook, carry his name, an officer's O-number, his tetanus years, his blood type and a P for Protestant, and the Army has quit stamping next of kin.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Army identification tags of 1944 were notched rectangles of stamped stainless steel, and every soldier wore two, the second hanging from a short extension of the chain. On July 20, 1943, the Army dropped the next-of-kin lines, so a tag stamped after that date shows name, serial number, tetanus dates, blood type and a religion letter: P, C or H. An officer's serial number begins with O. Early tags were stamped in monel, then brass and steel, and from the end of 1943 production went back to stainless steel alone. The notch held the blank in place on the embossing machine.

### 61. Why the Tails Were Red
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street; Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 16, The Shots; The Cabin: II; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 31, Christmas Eve; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk

### 64. Berlin, March 24, 1945
**Where it appears:** Ch. 16, The Shots; The Cabin: II; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** The Red Tails fly sixteen hundred miles to Berlin with the B-17s, the German jets come through the bomber stream, and Sam's Mustang goes down burning. In Ch. 34 Marcus reads that the Tuskegee Airmen shot down three German jets that day and the Army gave the whole group a Distinguished Unit Citation.
**What you'll find when you dig:** On March 24, 1945, the 332nd escorted 5th Bomb Wing B-17s to the Daimler-Benz tank works in Berlin, the longest escort mission the Fifteenth Air Force ever flew, about 1,600 miles round trip. The group earned a Distinguished Unit Citation. Captain Armour McDaniel was shot down and broke both legs, and 2nd Lts. Ronald Reeves and Robert Robinson ran out of fuel and never came home.

### 65. The Fire in St. Louis, July 12, 1973
**Where it appears:** Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** Marcus finds no Samuel Mayfield on the Red Tails' roll, and Isaiah tells him that on July 12, 1973, a fire on the sixth floor of the government's military records building outside St. Louis burned four of every five Army files from Sam's war, the Army Air Forces in with them, that to this day nobody holds a complete list of the lost files, and that the roll Marcus read twice, the first roll of the Tuskegee pilots a person could search on a telephone, was put together by historians out of the paper still standing and published in the fall of 2018.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Shortly after midnight on July 12, 1973, fire broke out at the Military Personnel Records Center in Overland, Missouri, a suburb of St. Louis. It burned out of control for 22 hours, destroyed the sixth floor, where the Army and Air Force records were kept, and was not declared out until July 16. The National Archives puts the loss at 16 to 18 million Official Military Personnel Files, including about 80 percent of the records of Army personnel discharged between November 1, 1912, and January 1, 1960, and its own holdings tables file Army Air Corps and Army Air Forces records under the Army. No duplicate copies, microfilm or index of those files existed, so no complete list of what was lost exists either. On October 31, 2018, the Commemorative Air Force's Red Tail Squadron published what it called the first-ever complete, searchable Tuskegee Airmen Pilot Roster: 1,007 pilots, compiled with data from the Air Force Historical Research Agency and Tuskegee Airmen historians.

## The Resistance and the Gestapo

### 68. The Office of Strategic Services
**Where it appears:** Ch. 10, The Boy Who Lied; Ch. 11, The Farm; Ch. 12, Baker Street
**What the novel says:** A small balding man from the Office of Strategic Services flies to Honolulu to hear Ruth's German and tells her that lying to the Navy is a qualification in his line of work.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The OSS was America's first central intelligence service, created in 1942 under William "Wild Bill" Donovan, and it ran spies, saboteurs and Resistance teams across Europe and Asia. At its December 1944 peak the OSS employed about 4,500 women, roughly 900 of them overseas. The CIA grew out of it in 1947.

### 69. Tents on the Fairways: Congressional Country Club
**Where it appears:** Ch. 11, The Farm; The Cabin: II; Ch. 19, Hammer and Chisel
**What the novel says:** Ruth trains at the Congressional Country Club outside Washington, on a golf course full of tents, with a man blowing up a stump on the eighteenth green, and she learns Morse code in the club's ballroom, tapping it on a card table with a pencil.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The OSS took over the Congressional Country Club in Bethesda, Maryland, and ran it as Training Area F, a school for agents and commando teams, with tents pitched across the fairways. Doris Bohrer and other OSS women passed through the club's training. After the war the club got its golf course back, and it has since hosted the U.S. Open.

### 70. Silent Killing and the Fighting Knife
**Where it appears:** Ch. 11, The Farm
**What the novel says:** A former Marine Raider teaches the students a narrow double-edged knife and where to put it so a man cannot scream.

### 72. Virginia Hall, the Limping Lady
**Where it appears:** Ch. 11, The Farm
**What the novel says:** Around the kitchen stove an instructor tells the story of a Baltimore woman with a wooden leg who ran agents out of Lyon and crossed the Pyrenees in the snow.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Virginia Hall of Baltimore lost her lower left leg in a hunting accident and named her artificial leg "Cuthbert." Hall ran agents for British SOE out of Lyon in 1941 and 1942, the Gestapo hunted the woman it called the limping lady, and she escaped over the Pyrenees into Spain in November 1942. Hall went back into France for the OSS in March 1944 and became the only civilian woman awarded the Distinguished Service Cross in World War II.

**What you'll find when you dig:** Operation Steinbock, the Luftwaffe's last bombing campaign against London, ran from January to May 1944, and Londoners called it the Baby Blitz. The raids killed about 1,500 civilians and cost Germany hundreds of bombers it could not replace.

### 74. F Section on Baker Street
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street; The Cabin: II; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One
**What the novel says:** The French Section of SOE works behind an unmarked door on Baker Street, a few doors from where the British say Sherlock Holmes lived.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Special Operations Executive, Churchill's secret army ordered to "set Europe ablaze," ran its French Section from Norgeby House at 83 Baker Street, and its agents called themselves the Baker Street Irregulars after Holmes's street urchins. Holmes's 221B Baker Street is an address from fiction, and the Sherlock Holmes Museum now stands farther up the street. F Section sent more than 400 agents into France, about forty of them women.

### 75. Vera Atkins
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street; Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 21, The Widow on the Homeplace
**What the novel says:** Vera Atkins smokes through Ruth's interview, names her Lucie and after the war mails her diary and letters home. In the summer of 2000 Ruth reads a London obituary for Vera Atkins, born Vera Rosenberg to a Jewish family in Romania, and knows at last what lay underneath that clipped Baker Street English.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Vera Atkins was born Vera Rosenberg in Galați, Romania, in 1908, and she served as F Section's intelligence officer, the woman who briefed the agents, dressed them, saw them onto the planes and kept their files. Atkins never told her colleagues she was Jewish, and she stayed legally an enemy alien until she was naturalized in February 1944. Ian Fleming is said to have drawn Miss Moneypenny from her. After the war she went into Germany to find out what had happened to the 118 F Section agents who never came back, traced 117 of them and established how Violette Szabo, Denise Bloch and Lilian Rolfe died. Atkins died at Hastings on June 24, 2000, at ninety-two, and several who had worked beside her learned she was Jewish only from her obituaries. Sarah Helm's 2005 biography, *A Life in Secrets*, uncovered a 1940 trip Atkins made into the Low Countries with money to bribe an Abwehr officer for a cousin's passport.

### 76. "Jacqueline MUST Come": The Nearne Sisters
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street; Ch. 15, Ravensbrück; Ch. 21, The Widow on the Homeplace
**What the novel says:** On Ruth's first morning at Baker Street two FANY girls on the stairs are laughing over the Lysander that went to fetch a Miss Nearne home from France that month with *Jacqueline MUST come* chalked along its fuselage. In Ruth's first weeks at Ravensbrück another of Baker Street's girls, a wireless operator named Eileen Nearne who had been taken in Paris in July, refuses to do the camp's work and is told she will be shot if she refuses again. In September 2010 Ruth reads on the BBC that council workers in Torquay, clearing the flat of an old woman found dead alone, came on her medals and learned that Eileen Nearne had been a wireless operator in occupied Paris and a prisoner at Ravensbrück.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Jacqueline Nearne carried messages for SOE's STATIONER circuit in central France for more than fourteen months. London ordered her home, and on April 9, 1944, the Lysander sent for her landed with "Jacqueline MUST come." chalked on its fuselage. Jacqueline's younger sister, Eileen, called Didi, was by then a wireless operator in Paris, and each sister had tried to keep her work secret from the other. Eileen Nearne was arrested on July 25, 1944, after her transmitter was detected, came through the Gestapo's cold-bath torture without giving herself away and was sent to Ravensbrück on August 15, 1944. At Ravensbrück she refused to do prison work, had her head shaved and was told she would be shot if she kept refusing. Moved on to labor camps, Eileen escaped from a work gang on April 13, 1945, and hid until the Americans came. Eileen received the MBE and the Croix de Guerre and then lived quietly for sixty-five years without speaking of the war. On September 2, 2010, her body was found in her Torquay flat, at eighty-nine, and council workers searching for a next of kin found her medals and wartime papers. Strangers filled her funeral on September 21, 2010. Jacqueline played herself in the RAF film *School for Danger*.

### 77. Patton's Phantom Army, the Oil Dock at Dover and the Architect of Coventry
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street; Ch. 19, Hammer and Chisel
**What the novel says:** The Prague tailor at Baker Street chalks Ruth's coat pattern on a London paper carrying General Patton's words at the opening of a club for American soldiers in a Cheshire town, the destiny of the British and the Americans to rule the world, with the Russians left out of the sentence, and around his pins the tailor says the general has a whole army waiting in Kent and an oil dock going up at Dover, though nobody he knows in Kent has laid eyes on one of the general's tanks. In May 1962 Ruth cuts out a newspaper photograph of the new cathedral at Coventry, raised beside the burned shell of the old one, whose architect is a Scotsman called Basil Spence, and lays it in the shoebox without a word of pencil.
**What you'll find when you dig:** On April 25, 1944, at the opening of a Welcome Club for American soldiers in Knutsford, Cheshire, Patton said it was the destiny of the British and Americans (and, by his account, the Russians) to rule the world. The papers dropped the Russians, and Eisenhower nearly sent him home. Patton's "army" was Operation Fortitude's First U.S. Army Group, a force invented to hold Hitler's divisions at Calais, built mostly from false wireless traffic, with dummy tanks, canvas landing craft in the harbors and a counterfeit oil terminal at Dover, which King George VI and Montgomery inspected for the cameras. The camouflage officer who designed the Dover dock, Basil Spence, went on to design the new Coventry Cathedral, consecrated on May 25, 1962, beside the ruin of the medieval cathedral the Germans burned on November 14, 1940.

### 78. The Germans' April Fool's Wire and the Word CAUGHT
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street; Ch. 14, Avenue Foch
**What the novel says:** Down the corridor from the stores desk a girl from the Dutch section says the Germans in Holland wired London on the first of April, in plain English, to complain that Baker Street had lately tried to do business in Holland without them after such long and successful cooperation, and to promise the next agents the same care as every agent before them. One Dutch wireless man in German hands, she says, left out his security checks two years before and worked the letters of CAUGHT into his messages, and London paid it no mind. At Avenue Foch Ruth knows that if the Germans have her wireless operator, his next message to Algiers will come through clean, without the small mistake London taught every operator to make on purpose.
**What you'll find when you dig:** From March 1942 the Abwehr's Hermann Giskes ran captured SOE radio sets in Holland, the *Englandspiel*, and London kept dropping agents, money and arms straight to German reception committees: fifty-four agents were captured and only four survived. The first captured operator, Hubertus Lauwers, left out his security checks and put the letters CAU and GHT at the beginning and end of his messages, and London accepted the traffic as genuine. SOE's code chief Leo Marks noticed the Dutch messages never carried a coding mistake, too perfect for men working under fire. On April 1, 1944, Giskes signed off in English: "In the last time you are trying to make business in Netherlands without our assistance," and promised future visitors the same care as all the agents sent before them.
**Start digging here:** [Englandspiel (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Englandspiel) · [Leo Marks (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Marks) · Leo Marks, *Between Silk and Cyanide* (1998) · M. R. D. Foot, *SOE in the Low Countries* (2001)

### 80. Alsace, Annexed
**Where it appears:** Ch. 11, The Farm; Ch. 12, Baker Street; Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 14, Avenue Foch; Ch. 31, Christmas Eve
**What the novel says:** Ruth's cover is Lucie Brenner of Mulhouse, one of the Alsatians the Germans put on the roads in 1940, whose French carries German in it.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Germany annexed Alsace and the Moselle in 1940 in all but name and put Gauleiter Robert Wagner over Alsace with full powers. The Nazis expelled about 35,000 people from Alsace and more than 100,000 from the Moselle, targeting Jews, French speakers and anyone they judged "non-Germanizable," and many of those refugees settled in the south of France. Alsatians grew up speaking a German dialect at home and French at school, so a German accent in French was an Alsatian's alibi.

### 81. Saint Lucy and Her Eyes
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street
**What the novel says:** Vera Atkins tells Ruth the old pictures show Saint Lucy carrying her own eyes on a dish, which suits a courier.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Saint Lucy of Syracuse was martyred around 304 under the emperor Diocletian, and painters show her holding her eyes on a dish, after a legend that her eyes were torn from her head. Lucy's name comes from the Latin word for light, and Scandinavians still crown girls with candles on Saint Lucy's Day, December 13.

### 82. V-Mail
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One
**What the novel says:** Sam's letter from Italy arrives as V-mail, in the small photographed hand the Army used to save shipping space.
**What you'll find when you dig:** V-mail, Victory Mail, began in 1942. A soldier wrote on a printed form, censors read it, and the letter was photographed onto 16mm film, more than 1,500 letters to a roll. At the other end the film was printed out at about a quarter size, and a few pounds of film replaced a fifty-pound sack of mail. More than a billion V-mail letters crossed the oceans between 1942 and 1945.

### 83. Violette Szabo
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street; Ch. 14, Avenue Foch; Ch. 15, Ravensbrück; Ch. 16, The Shots; The Cabin: II; Ch. 33, Justice; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** At a London pistol range Ruth meets a small dark-haired widow who shoots better than anyone, bought dresses in Paris on her way home from France, and has a little girl named Tania.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Violette Szabo was born in Paris on June 26, 1921, to an English father and a French mother. ### 84. Blida, the Halifax and the Drop
**Where it appears:** Ch. 12, Baker Street; Ch. 13, The Drôme
**What the novel says:** A Halifax lifts out of Blida, below Algiers, and Ruth parachutes into a field of lavender in the Drôme in July 1944.

### 85. Dieulefit, the Town That Hid Everyone
**Where it appears:** Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One
**What the novel says:** Between twelve and fifteen hundred refugees live under false names around Dieulefit, not one of them betrayed, and a town-hall secretary named Jeanne Barnier forges their papers.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Dieulefit, in the Drôme, had about 3,500 people in 1940, a third of them Protestants, and it sheltered between 1,200 and 1,500 refugees, most of them Jews. Not one was denounced or arrested. Jeanne Barnier, the young secretary at the town hall, forged identity papers and ration tickets and chose birthplaces whose records had been destroyed so no German could check them. Nine people of Dieulefit are honored by Yad Vashem as Righteous Among the Nations, and the French still call the town's war "the miracle of Dieulefit."

### 86. The Lavender Harvest in the Drôme
**Where it appears:** Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 19, Hammer and Chisel; Ch. 27, The Bus; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** Ruth parachutes into a field of lavender, bundles hang head-down from the barn rafters, and in the last days of July she cuts lavender beside the women of Valfrède, because late July is harvest in the Drôme.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Lavender in the Drôme comes into flower from mid-June, and the harvest runs from early July around Crest to early August in the high Diois and the Baronnies, with the stills working on past the middle of August. The ripe stage of the flower lasts three to four weeks, and the weather moves the cutting forward or back by days. The Drôme provençale, from the Tricastin plain and Grignan up into the hills, is one of the lavender countries of France to this day.

### 87. The Church of the Desert
**Where it appears:** Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 18, Kate; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** The French Protestants call their church a temple, and the pastor's great-great-great-grandfather was married at night in a ravine below Anduze while a boy watched for the king's dragoons.
**What you'll find when you dig:** After Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, French Protestant worship became a crime, and the Huguenots met in secret in ravines, caves and forests, the "Church of the Desert." The law refused to recognize marriages not performed in the Catholic Church until King Louis XVI's Edict of Toleration of November 1787 let Protestants marry and register their children's births before a royal judge. To this day a French Protestant church is a *temple*, and the Catholic church is an *église*.

### 88. The Temples Torn Down and the Huguenot Cross
**Where it appears:** Ch. 13, The Drôme
**What the novel says:** The king's men pulled the first temple of Valfrède down after 1685, the descendants carried the same stones back up the hill when the law let them build again, and a Huguenot cross with a little dove hanging from its foot is cut into the pulpit.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes on October 18, 1685, and the demolition of the Protestant temples began at once, starting with the great temple at Charenton outside Paris. For a century the Huguenots worshiped in the "Desert," in ravines, woods and caves, with portable pulpits built to pass for barrels. The Edict of Toleration of 1787 and Napoleon's settlement with the churches in 1802 brought them back into the open, and the temples began to rise again. At Le Poët-Laval, a few miles from Dieulefit, the old temple survived the Revocation only because it was turned into a house, and it became a temple again in 1860. The Huguenot cross, designed in Nîmes in the 1680s, hangs a dove from its foot for the Holy Spirit, and in the years of persecution a pearl, for a tear, sometimes took the dove's place. It is the emblem of the Reformed Church of France.

### 90. The North African Brigade, Mussidan and Klaus Barbie
**Where it appears:** Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 14, Avenue Foch; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** Gaston warns Ruth that the North African Brigade, some three hundred Algerians raised by Henri Lafont, the gangster who runs the French Gestapo out of the rue Lauriston, and by Mohamed el-Maadi, whose own men call him Mohamed SS, has left massacres behind it in the Dordogne, and that at Mussidan in June the SD picked out the men of the town and shot them, fifty-two dead in one day, with the brigade's Algerians standing beside the SD men. A detachment lent to Werner Knab's Sipo-SD in Lyon, where Klaus Barbie ran the Gestapo, stops at the bridge below Dieulefit, and Ruth's Lucie carbon of August 22, 1983, names Barbie as the Gestapo chief of Lyon who tortured Jean Moulin to death and whom the Army's Counter Intelligence Corps sent down a ratline to Bolivia in 1951.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Brigade Nord-Africaine was raised in the winter of 1943 to 1944 by Henri Lafont, chief of the French Gestapo at 93 rue Lauriston in Paris, and the Algerian Mohamed el-Maadi, about three hundred men in German uniform, and it left a trail of massacres in the Dordogne. At Mussidan on June 11, 1944, the head of the SD, Michaël Hambrecht, came with practically all his men, followed by North African auxiliaries under Raymond Monange. The SD selected fifty men and shot them on the chemin de Gorry, two of whom survived, and four more people were killed in the street, the mayor among them, fifty-two dead in all, as the Musée André Voulgre in Mussidan records it. In August 1944 el-Maadi fled to Germany, where the Grand Mufti, Amin al-Husseini, welcomed him and later helped him reach the Middle East, and he died in Egypt in the mid-1950s. Klaus Barbie took over the Gestapo in Lyon in November 1942, tortured Jean Moulin to death in 1943 and sent forty-four Jewish children of Izieu to Auschwitz in April 1944. The U.S. Army's Counter Intelligence Corps recruited him in 1947, an American ratline carried him to Bolivia in 1951 as Klaus Altmann, and on August 16, 1983, the United States formally apologized to France. A court in Lyon convicted Barbie of crimes against humanity on July 4, 1987, and he died in a Lyon prison in 1991.

### 96. 84 Avenue Foch

**What you'll find when you dig:** 84 Avenue Foch was the Paris headquarters of the SD, the SS counterintelligence service, under Josef Kieffer, and its top floor held a guardroom, an interpreter's office and cells for prisoners under interrogation. ### 97. Over the Roof of 84 Avenue Foch, and the Man at the Drawing Board

### 106. The Rabbits
**Where it appears:** Ch. 15, Ravensbrück; Ch. 25, Kenji; Ch. 26, The Hearing
**What the novel says:** In the camp infirmary Ruth meets Polish girls from Lublin whose legs SS doctors cut open and packed with bacteria, wood and glass, and one says a doctor was a woman.
**What you'll find when you dig:** From 1942 SS doctors at Ravensbrück cut open the legs of Polish women prisoners, infected the wounds and treated some with sulfa drugs and some with nothing, to test sulfonamides for wounded soldiers. Surviving rabbits testified against her.

### 107. Letters Written in Urine
**Where it appears:** Ch. 15, Ravensbrück
**What the novel says:** In the Revier the girl with the cut leg tells Ruth that a letter home could carry two letters at once, one in pencil for the censor and one between the lines in the one ink no guard could take from a prisoner, her own water, and that a mother in Lublin held such a page to a hot iron and watched the names of the cut girls come up brown. The Farm had taught Ruth the same trick with lemon juice and a candle, and Ruth says nothing about the Farm.
**What you'll find when you dig:** In 1943 and 1944 four of the Polish "rabbits" at Ravensbrück, Krystyna Czyż, Wanda Wójtasik and the sisters Janina and Krystyna Iwańska, wrote the truth about the leg operations between the lines of 27 censored letters home in their own urine, which dries invisible and turns brown under heat. Czyż tipped off her family with the first letters of her opening lines, which spelled *list moczem*, "letter in urine," and her mother in Lublin raised the hidden text with a hot iron. The list of operated women went to the Polish underground and on to London, and Polish radio in London broadcast the experiments on May 3, 1944. The letters came to light again in Poland in 2017.

**Where it appears:** Ch. 16, The Shots; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk; Epilogue: Christmond
**What the novel says:** White buses with red crosses come up the sand road in April 1945, Danish crowds throw flowers at Padborg, and the ferry carries Ruth to Malmö.

### 119. Himmler's Masseur and the Meeting at Half Past Two
**Where it appears:** Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One
**What the novel says:** In Stockholm the man in the tweed coat tells Ruth that one of his own colleagues feigned a bad back through the winter of 1943 so he could lie on a table under the hands of Himmler's masseur, and that the same masseur flew into Berlin that April with a Swedish Jew named Norbert Masur, and at half past two in the morning after Hitler's birthday Himmler sat down with Masur at the masseur's country place north of the city and agreed to let Jewish women out of Ravensbrück.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Felix Kersten was Heinrich Himmler's personal masseur. In October 1943 Abram Hewitt, an OSS officer in Stockholm under cover of the United States Commercial Company, met Kersten and feigned a bad back as cover for frequent visits to his table, and through Kersten he met Walter Schellenberg of the SD. The American minister wanted Hewitt "got out of Sweden as soon as possible," and Hewitt left on February 2, 1944. In April 1945 Kersten flew to Berlin with Norbert Masur of the World Jewish Congress's Swedish section, and Himmler arrived at Kersten's estate at Hartzwalde, north of Berlin, at 2:30 a.m. on April 21, the night after Hitler's birthday. The two men talked about two and a half hours, and Himmler agreed to release Jewish women from Ravensbrück.

### 121. Patton's Death in Heidelberg
**Where it appears:** Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk
**What the novel says:** On December 22, 1945, the paper reports that General George S. Patton has died in an Army hospital in Heidelberg, twelve days after an Army truck turned across the path of his staff car outside Mannheim, after a week of printing that he was mending, and the paper wants Texas to believe a crumpled fender on a Sunday drive to hunt pheasants killed him. Ruth reads the paragraph twice. Ruth's Lucie carbon of November 10, 2008, says the fender bender put Patton in a hospital bed he never left, that he meant to resign his commission, come home, run for office and tell the country the truth about Eisenhower and the Russians, and that in *Target: Patton* an OSS man named Douglas Bazata says the OSS paid him to set up the wreck, while Robert Wilcox lays out how Russian agents finished the general in his hospital bed.
**What you'll find when you dig:** On Sunday, December 9, 1945, General George S. Patton was carried to the Army hospital at Heidelberg, the papers reported him improving, and he died there on December 21, 1945. That fall Patton had lost command of the Third Army over his words to the press, and on the eve of the wreck, Robert K. Wilcox reports, he was vowing to reveal what he knew about Eisenhower's conduct of the war, rally Americans against the Soviets and possibly run for office. In *Target: Patton* (2008) Wilcox lays out the account of Douglas Bazata, an OSS Jedburgh, who said OSS chief William Donovan ordered him to kill Patton, that he and an NKVD accomplice set up the crash, and that the Soviets finished the general in the hospital.

### 133. Doc: Fort Sam Houston, the 25th Infantry and Cu Chi
**Where it appears:** Ch. 1, Christmond; Ch. 2, The Man by the Fire; The Cabin: II; Ch. 20, The Back Row; The Cabin: III; Ch. 35, What I Saw; Ch. 36, The Four Drawers; Ch. 37, The Key

**What you'll find when you dig:** A college man's 2-S deferment lasted only while he was enrolled, and in 1968 the government ended most graduate-school deferments, so the class of 1968 went from the commencement lawn to the draft board. ### 164. A Felony in Texas
**Where it appears:** Ch. 13, The Drôme; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 23, Letters to Houston; Ch. 34, The Last Thing in the Trunk; Epilogue: Christmond
**What the novel says:** Ruth tells Sam that in Texas their marriage means the penitentiary, two to five years, and is void the minute they cross the state line, her letters say the law of Texas made such a marriage a felony until 1967, and in the Epilogue the newspaper's plain-fact line says the law of her own state made that marriage a felony, two to five years in the penitentiary.

### 165. The Commanding Officer's Signature
**Where it appears:** Ch. 13, The Drôme

### 166. No Ban in Illinois or Michigan, 1944
**Where it appears:** Ch. 13, The Drôme

### 167. *Loving v. Virginia*

### 170. Guardianship in Texas, 2013
**Where it appears:** Ch. 24, Jezebel's Paper; Ch. 26, The Hearing
**What the novel says:** An Austin guardian files to take over Ruth's affairs with a doctor's certificate, and Eric Rogers tells her a guardian can ask the court to sell her land and that government auditors counted hundreds of complaints against guardians in forty-five states, then went through twenty closed cases and found guardians had taken $5.4 million from 158 wards.
**What you'll find when you dig:** In 2013 the Texas Probate Code barred a court from creating a guardianship unless the applicant presented a letter or certificate from a Texas-licensed physician based on an examination within 120 days before the filing. ### 171. Five Percent In, Five Percent Out
**Where it appears:** Ch. 24, Jezebel's Paper; Ch. 26, The Hearing
**What the novel says:** Eric Rogers tells Ruth the statute calls five percent of everything that comes into a ward's estate, and five percent of everything paid out, a reasonable fee, paid from the ward's own money, and Ruth repeats it to Rusk from the witness box.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Section 665(b) of the Texas Probate Code, as amended by S.B. 1417 in 2001, presumed reasonable a guardian's fee of five percent of the gross income of the ward's estate and five percent of all money paid out of the estate. ### 172. The Beers List
**Where it appears:** Ch. 24, Jezebel's Paper; Ch. 25, Kenji; Ch. 26, The Hearing
**What the novel says:** Eric Rogers asks the doctor whether he knows the American Geriatrics Society's Beers list, and the doctor admits the bladder pill, the dizziness pill and the sleeping pill cause confusion in an eighty-nine-year-old.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The Beers Criteria, first published by Dr. Mark Beers in 1991 and updated by the American Geriatrics Society in 2012, list medicines older adults should usually avoid. Strongly anticholinergic drugs such as oxybutynin for the bladder and meclizine for dizziness can cause confusion and memory loss in the elderly, and sleeping pills such as zolpidem raise the risk of falls, confusion and "complex sleep behaviors," including sleepwalking and sleep-driving. Drug-induced confusion in old people often clears when the drugs stop.

### 181. Reading the Eyes
**Where it appears:** Ch. 25, Kenji; Ch. 26, The Hearing
**What the novel says:** Kenji Morita looks into Ruth's grey eyes with a penlight and a brass glass, reads a dark ring around the pupil and tells her she has parasites.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Iridology, the practice of reading the body's condition in the iris, traces to the Hungarian physician Ignaz von Péczely, who said that as a boy he broke an owl's leg and saw a dark mark appear in the bird's eye. ### 182. The Threadworm That Never Leaves
**Where it appears:** Ch. 25, Kenji; Ch. 26, The Hearing
**What the novel says:** Kenji tells Ruth a threadworm came in through bare feet in bad ground, its young go back into her over and over, English prisoners of the Japanese carried it thirty and forty years, cortisone lets it multiply, and Dr. Castellanos's blood test comes back positive.
**What you'll find when you dig:** *Strongyloides stercoralis* enters through bare skin from contaminated soil, and its larvae can mature inside the body and reinfect the host, so an untreated infection can last a lifetime. Doctors in Liverpool found it in British survivors of the Japanese camps and the Burma Railway decades after the war: a 1979 study found a mean of thirty years since their return, and of 2,072 former prisoners examined at the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine from 1968 to 2002, 248 were infected. A French patient was diagnosed seventy-five years after his exposure. The CDC warns that corticosteroids can turn a quiet infection into a deadly hyperinfection, and an antibody blood test finds the worm.

### 183. Makuri, the Seaweed for Worms
**Where it appears:** Ch. 25, Kenji; Ch. 31, Christmas Eve
**What the novel says:** Kenji breaks red seaweed off his drying rack and calls it makuri: in the days of the shoguns every Japanese mother gave it to her new baby, after the war the schools in Japan gave it to the children for worms, and in 1953 two scientists in Japan found the medicine inside it and named it kainic acid, after kaininsō, the seaweed's other name.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Makuri, also called kaininsō, is the red seaweed *Digenea simplex*, gathered off southern Kyushu. In 1953 Shinzo Murakami and Tsunematsu Takemoto isolated its active compound and named it kainic acid, after kaininsō. Neuroscientists around the world still use kainic acid in the laboratory.

### 184. Paico and Epazote
**Where it appears:** Ch. 25, Kenji

### 185. Wasabi, Ginger and Shiso

### 187. Antifreeze in the Hamburger

### 189. Orders That Sit in a Drawer

John breeds Labrador retrievers, he lived in Fredericksburg in the first half of 2011 while he fought a lawsuit over a film about the ten Boom house, and every line quoted from his letters in Chapter 23 comes from the emails he actually sent. ## Ruth's Money

### 274. What the Mail Paid
**Where it appears:** Prologue: Pallbearers; Ch. 17, The Widow at Twenty-One; Ch. 21, The Widow on the Homeplace; Ch. 22, The Office on Main Street; Ch. 23, Letters to Houston; Ch. 33, Justice
**What the novel says:** Ruth carries the rural route out of the Martinsburg post office for thirty-nine years, drives one used truck, not one man at the feed store can remember her spending a dime she did not need to spend, and clipped to her last brokerage statement lies her last Post Office pay stub, March 1986, two weeks on the route for about a thousand dollars.
**What you'll find when you dig:** Rural carriers were paid by the length and workload of the route, plus an allowance for using their own vehicles, and a full-time rural carrier's base pay ran close to a top-step city letter carrier's. That benchmark stood at $9,657 a year in 1970, $12,714 in 1974, $20,320 in 1980 and $25,874 in 1985. A carrier who worked April 1947 through March 1986 earned about $372,000 in a lifetime, and never more than about $27,000 in a single year.
**Start digging here:** [NALC Cumulative Pay History (National Association of Letter Carriers, PDF)](https://nalc.org/union-administration/body/Cummulative-pay.pdf)

**What you'll find when you dig:** *Forbes* profiled Buffett in its November 1, 1969, issue under the headline "How Omaha Beats Wall Street," the year he closed his investment partnership and kept control of Berkshire Hathaway, a failing New England textile mill he was turning into a holding company.

### 276. The Crash of 1974
**Where it appears:** Ch. 21, The Widow on the Homeplace; Ch. 33, Justice
**What the novel says:** In October 1974, with Berkshire Hathaway down more than half since New Year's Day and trading near forty-two dollars, Ruth puts in about $20,000, borrows $5,000 more against her cattle in the worst cattle market in decades, buys 595 shares and never sells.
**What you'll find when you dig:** The 1973–1974 bear market cut the S&P 500 almost in half, and Berkshire Hathaway's stock lost 54 percent of its value in 1974 alone, ending the year near $40 a share. That fall Buffett told *Forbes* he felt "like an oversexed guy in a harem," because the bargains were everywhere. Cattle prices that same fall were the worst in decades, so a banker who saw a mail carrier borrow against her herd to buy that stock had two reasons to call her crazy. A $25,000 stake at about $42 a share bought 595 shares.

## Where the Truth Ends and the Story Begins

The history in this book is real. The people at the center of it are invented, and so is every scene in which a real person meets one of them. Here is the line.

- **Ruth Marie Vellmer** never lived. Neither did Fritz and Katharina Vellmer or their children, Walter, Henry, Anna, Ernie and Lena. No Ruth Vellmer worked the wards at Pearl Harbor, trained with the OSS, dropped into the Drôme, sat in a cell at 84 Avenue Foch or rode a white bus out of Ravensbrück.
- **Sam Mayfield** never flew. No Samuel J. Mayfield appears on any Tuskegee class roll or any list of the 332nd Fighter Group, and no Red Tail was lost over southern France in late July 1944. The men the 332nd really lost on the Berlin mission of March 24, 1945, were Armour McDaniel, Leon Spears, James Mitchell, Ronald Reeves and Robert Robinson. Sam's shootdown is built on Robert O'Neil's.
- **The Mayfield family**, Hattie, Joseph, Eli, Isaiah, Gloria, Ruthie, Daniel, Marcus, Lily and Ben, is invented, and so is the Red Goose box of letters.
- **Martinsburg** as a county seat, **Pedernales County**, St. Paul Lutheran, the Mustang Grill, Waidner Field, the Martinsburg Mustangs and Bus No. 7 are invented. Fredericksburg and Gillespie County are the real places behind them, and the town's goodness is meant as a portrait of the real one.
- **The French village of Valfrède**, Pasteur Jean Chabrol and Madeleine, Odile Fabre, Gaston and Marthe Peyrol, the Café du Pont, the STONECUTTER circuit, the marriage paper and the borrowed ring are invented. Dieulefit, Jeanne Barnier and the Protestant villages of the Drôme are real.
- **Ruth's meetings with real people** are invented: Nimitz on the ward, Jack Lucas at the loading dock, Violette Szabo at the range, the ten Boom sisters in the bunks, Yeo-Thomas on the train, Vera Atkins at her desk. The real people did what the novel says they did, and Ruth was never there.
- **Corrie ten Boom's letters to Ruth** are invented. Corrie's Munich story is her own, told in *Guideposts* and *Tramp for the Lord*.
- **Miss Draper, Mrs. Tanaka, Clarence Banks, Arthur Leland, Amos Jessup, Lt. Harmon, Deacon Hawes, Lucius Garrow, Emmett Pierce, Wachholz and Strelow** are invented.
- **Walter Vellmer's identification in 2018** is invented. The DPAA project and its 355 identifications are real.
- **Kenji Morita** is invented. Crystal City, the Japanese Peruvians and the redress payments are real, and so are makuri, paico, the Liverpool threadworm studies and Ōmura's ivermectin.
- **Isaiah's tour at Cu Chi**, his mortar scar and Doc's platoon are invented. The 25th Infantry at Cu Chi, the Agent Orange spraying and the Agent Orange Act of 1991 are real.
- **Dr. Matt Ahrens**, the clinic on the Kerrville road and Honey's trust are invented. Texas pet trusts, antifreeze poisoning and the shooting of Marcus Luttrell's dog DASY are real.
- **Clayton Rusk, Margo Strand, Dr. Prescott, Whitney Hale, Judge Wehmeyer, Dr. Castellanos** and the guardianship case are invented. Eric Rogers carries the real Eric Parker under a new name, and his part in Ruth's case is fiction.
- **Coach Ray Winslow, Dixie Beck and Teddy Rausch** are modeled on John Tate's real classmates Randy Winfrey, Dixie Bamer and Teddy Richards, under invented names. The players are invented.
- **Lyle Mosher the bus driver** carries the real Lester Meier's character under a new name, and the bus is fiction. The homemade smoked sausage is the real Meiers'.
- **Honey, Ruth's dog,** is invented, named for John Tate's real childhood Lab.
- **The dog man's letters** are real, and **Ruth's answers** are invented. Ruth's year of paid storage rent at Miss Tipton's is invented, and Miss Tipton is real.
- **Tarek Haddad, Karim Nasser and Samir Darwish** are invented, along with Deputy Kyle Brandt, the vandalized nativity, the shooting on Elm Street and the inquest. No such Texas case exists. The legal machinery around them, the Class C grading, the 48-hour detainer, the fugitive backlog, the justice of the peace's inquest and the Texas law of self-defense, is real.
- **The news outlets** are invented: *The Daily Beat* (a New York website), KVAN 36 News (Austin) and CNM (cable).
- **Ruth's Berkshire Hathaway fortune** is invented. Berkshire's 1974 collapse and its rise afterward are real.
- **Ernie as a guard at Camp Hearne** is invented, and Camp Hearne is real.
- **The statue on the square** is invented.
- **The eleven R. V. churches** are invented: Shiloh Missionary Baptist below Luling, the Reverend Amos Tolliver and his shoebox, the Mount Zion ledger entries, the little white church in Martinsburg, the 425 money orders and the $30,960. Watch Night itself is real.
- **Shari Whitcomb**, the one Martinsburg voice the cameras found, is invented, and so is the page from the guardianship file that the press printed beside Ruth's photograph.
- **The Easter Fires**, the town's own story, is labeled a legend in Chapter 3, because the history labels it one.
